Advocacy report · Council member

Earl Funk: what they said and did when people asked council for something

What did Earl Funk say and do when residents and groups brought requests to Steinbach City Council between December 2015 and October 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor 2010–2018 · Mayor since November 2018 (re-elected by acclamation in 2026)

In short

Earl Funk is involved in more requests in this record than anyone, mostly because he has chaired every meeting since November 2018. In practice the chair decides what happens to a letter: discussed, sent to staff, sent to a closed budget session, or “taken as information.” Under his chairing, almost every funding letter went to the budget sessions without public discussion.

He has also driven the largest yeses: the event centre partnership (2019), the economic development office (2021) and the event centre’s $400,000 loan (2026). He put residents’ street-racing letters on the agenda (2021), and he changed his mind on the Clearspring Greens levy after meeting the neighbourhood (2020).

“And when there is a young person that was injured or possibly injured and there could be chances for more, this is a serious request and we need to take it seriously.”

As a councillor, on the school division’s traffic-safety letter · Nov 21, 2017 · 0:28:46

Term by term

2014–2018

As a councillor under Mayor Goertzen he often questioned how council handled requests. He moved a motion asking the school division to freeze school taxes (2016), raised the Canada Summer Jobs problem for local groups (2018), opposed buying the golf club’s land as a bailout (2017), and voted against the 2018 budget partly because the Pat Porter seniors’ centre had followed the right process and got “a pat on the back.” He also held to the rules: on a resident’s drainage request he told council “we have to stick to our policy” (2016).

Took part in 23 of the requests in this report during this term. Read the 2014–2018 story.

2018–2022

Elected mayor in 2018. He kept the routine of filing funding letters but championed large partnerships when a group brought private money: the event centre ($10 million plus $500,000 a year, 2019) and the economic development office (2021). On Clearspring Greens he first defended charging the neighbourhood most of the cost, then backed moving the lighting cost to the city after meeting residents: “there’s nothing wrong with changing your mind after you’ve heard new information.” In 2021 he put a sample of residents’ street-racing letters on the agenda, which led the same night to a request for a dedicated traffic officer.

Took part in 37 of the requests in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

He sent most letters to staff or the budget sessions, ruled an independent sewer study out of order at a variance hearing (2026) and voted against the Loewen–Hespeler stop sign (2024). He also championed The Bridge on Third supportive housing, the push for provincial disaster aid for flooded residents and the seniors’ task force (2026). A promised answer to Southeast Cancer Support Services (2024) does not appear on the record.

Took part in 32 of the requests in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

Becoming mayor changed his role more than his views. As a councillor he criticized how grants were handled; as mayor he runs the process that handles them, mostly out of public view, while personally carrying the projects he believes in.
