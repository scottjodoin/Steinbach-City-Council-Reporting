Advocating to Steinbach City Council: what has worked, and what hasn't

When residents and community groups asked Steinbach City Council for something between December 2015 and October 2026, which requests succeeded, which stalled, and why? Which routes into council, which kinds of evidence (including surveys) and which timing moved decisions? How did that change from one council term to the next and from year to year, and how did each council member respond? And what should an advocacy group do, step by step, so that its time is spent where it counts?

Written for residents and advocacy groups. Quotes link to their moment in the official meeting videos; by-laws and peer sources are linked; the underlying data can be downloaded. Last updated Nov 6, 2026.

Bottom line

  • What you ask for matters most, and it has for eleven years. Across 164 requests and three councils, asks that cost the city nothing (a letter of support, consent to apply for an outside grant, a tax-receipt arrangement) won 11 times out of 14 and were never refused. Requests for money mostly went to council's closed budget sessions: 32 of 57 got no public answer at all.
  • Who asks, and how, comes next. Individual residents won outright only 4 times in 46 requests (10 more partly). The same kinds of concern did better when a council member carried them (6 won and 4 partly out of 15), when the neighbours affected signed together (Cottonwood, 2022), or when a group arrived with the plan already worked out with staff (Southeast Event Centre, 2019; Hanover School Division, 2023). A focused delegation can move the capital budget: Georgetown, 2025.
  • The staff report usually decides, and surveys count only when the city runs them. Residents' requests for stop signs were refused or replaced with something else every time after an engineering review (five since 2018); the two stop signs granted went to a business with staff's support (2017) and to the school division (2018). Council did override staff, for neighbours who showed consensus, for schools, a downtown church and a developer. Residents' own surveys, like the 83-home Georgetown survey, were not discussed again.
  • Timing and follow-up are where groups lose. Budget requests need to arrive before council's July or November study sessions. Promised answers and staff reports sometimes never came back (pool accessibility, 2019; Southeast Cancer Support Services, 2024; Parkhill School, 2026). Groups that returned every year, like STARS, kept their funding, and the Clearspring Greens pathway was built after a three-year campaign (2018–2021), with residents paying half.
  • Terms and members. Each council left fewer requests without a public answer (51% in 2014–2018, 40% in 2018–2022, 35% in 2022–2026). Most decisions on requests were unanimous; in the recorded split votes, Jac Siemens most often sided with the person asking. Two of the most active voices on requests, Michael Zwaagstra and Jac Siemens, left council in 2026, and the two new members, Ron Falk and Greg Carlos, have no record on requests yet.

Part 1 · The council record

The routes into council, and what each one usually gets

Council meets on the first and third Tuesday of the month at 7:30 p.m. Its 2006 procedure by-law (By-law 1809) sets the formal routes in. Items for the agenda must reach the City Secretary “at least 120 hours prior” to the meeting (s. 8.2), which means by the Thursday before. Delegations are recommended to register 120 hours ahead and say what they will cover (s. 14.2), and the chair “may require the delegation to appoint a spokesperson” (s. 14.1). In practice delegations are scheduled for 7:45 p.m., and the mayor described the usual time limit:

“Well, normally something like this, we give— we allow 10 minutes for a delegation like this.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jan 4, 2022 · 0:18:09

Turning up without booking does not work. When flooded residents filled the gallery in September 2025, the mayor explained the route:

“I'm very sorry for the short notice tonight. We can't have you speak, but it's great you're here… We've heard you. And we do welcome you to set a date and come as delegation. And if you could pick one person to do all the speaking at that delegation, it would be great.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 16, 2025 · 0:38:25
Context

By-law 1809 does allow more than this. A member of the public may ask to speak on a subject not on the agenda for “generally 5 minutes,” at the chair's discretion, and the chair may then place it on a future agenda or refer it to the City Manager (s. 8.4(5)). The “Council member question period” is described as “Intended to be an open session, allowing questions to the Mayor or any member of Council” (s. 8.4(9)). In the recordings neither is offered to the public. We found no delegation request form or booking page on the city's website; the City Clerk's office is the contact.

2014–2018: business rules, a narrow grants policy, and one big decision made in private

Business requests did best. The Chamber of Commerce rewrote the mobile-sign rules with a delegation, written submissions and drawings of real storefronts (2016), and a commercial developer won an exception from storm-sewer standards despite the city manager's warning about precedent (2016). Residents' drainage and sidewalk letters mostly ended in a staff report or “the policy stands”: Home Street North, Albert Street, Old Tom Road. Grants followed a “simplified and focused” policy, and the Pat Porter Active Living Centre's request for a grant in lieu of taxes was left out of the 2018 budget:

“the reality is that there hasn't been a change in the policy of the grants of the city, and so that will not be included in this budget.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen, at the 2018 financial plan hearing · Mar 20, 2018 · 0:16:26

The largest request of the term was decided out of public view. The Steinbach Fly-In Golf Club asked for a $500,000 grant in a closed meeting; it was refused, and came back as a purchase of 40 acres of the club's land for the same amount, leased back to the club for $1 a year. The first public discussion was the night of the vote:

“That's defeated 4-3, and so here we are. … It is a bailout that is dressed up as a land deal.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on his closed-meeting motion to have the club present in public · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:09:41

“The reality is, is that we have a win-win scenario here.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:21:30

Council did change its mind when a group came back better prepared. The Arts Council's $65,000 matching grant for repairs to the city-owned arts building was tabled 4–3 in June 2018, with one councillor calling it “reactionary,” and passed unanimously in September after the Arts Council presented a costed plan. A resident's request for a plebiscite on Sunday shopping was defeated 2–5.

2018–2022: partnerships, levy fights and a pandemic

Mayor Funk's first council kept the routine for funding letters (“received as information”) but took on large partnerships when a group brought money and a plan. The Southeast Event Centre group arrived in October 2019 with an agreement already drafted with staff and a $15 million private pledge, and council committed that night:

“I want you to know that tonight's resolution is to commit $10 million plus $500,000 a year.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Oct 1, 2019 · 0:51:37

In 2021 council agreed to match private money for a Chamber of Commerce economic development office, up to $65,000 a year for five years. (That is the grant council ended in 2025 for lack of results.)

Residents learned to use formal objections. The Clearspring Greens pathway, first asked for in 2018, was put off in 2019 (“Our 2019 budget did not allow us to do anything in that area at all”) and then proposed as a local improvement charged mostly to the neighbourhood. After about 20 residents spoke against the levy in January 2020, council moved the $208,000 lighting cost to the city within two weeks:

“I think, um, um, there's nothing wrong with changing your mind after you've heard new information, and that's what we heard as we met with, with the community, the neighborhood.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jan 21, 2020 · 0:18:21

When 78 owners still objected, Councillor Susan Penner rebuilt the plan as a 50/50 split capped at $1,610 a lot, and it went ahead in 2021:

“While the initial proposal had 78 objections, this new plan has only received 6 objections, which shows how far the city and the neighbourhood have come in working together and coming to an agreement that we both can support.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Feb 16, 2021 · 0:16:15

COVID-19 narrowed the routes in: there were four delegations in all of 2020 and none between mid-March and September. Public hearings stayed in person and became the main place residents spoke. In 2021, when letters about street racing piled up, the mayor put a sample on the agenda and council asked the same night for a dedicated RCMP traffic officer, at about $200,000 a year:

“I have been on some weeks bombarded with emails and phone calls and just visits to my store about the racing issue.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 7, 2021 · 1:10:15

2022–2026: staff reports, plain answers and the province

The same council, with more decided on the record: 35% of requests got no public answer, against 51% in 2014–2018. Requests that cost nothing passed the same night (the Skimmers' grant consents, the Pistons' arena glass, the golf club's tax receipts, the 55+ Games bid). Traffic requests were judged against the warrant guideline, and a councillor's motion against a consultant's report lost 3–4 (Loewen and Hespeler, 2024). Council also said no more plainly than before: it declined to host the RCMP Musical Ride without a local organizer (2023), declined to apply for federal money for Steinbach Community Outreach's mobile outreach unit after a staff memo said social services are outside the city's role (2024), ended the economic development grant (2025) and refused a cannabis-retail plebiscite 6–1 (2025). Requests to the province ended in letters (crosswalk lights, disaster aid).

The term's biggest change came from residents: the Georgetown sewer delegation (see the timeline). It ended with a $400,000 operating loan to the event centre group, an endorsement of The Bridge on Third supportive housing, and a mayor's task force on seniors placed outside Steinbach while they wait for a care-home bed, an issue Councillor Susan Penner raised after hearing from residents:

“dozens and dozens of people have shared their stories with me in our community, either through emails, through phone calls, through direct messages.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Oct 6, 2026 · 1:42:33
Context: 2026–2030, what changes for advocates

The council elected on October 28, 2026 was sworn in on November 3. Mayor Earl Funk was acclaimed for a third term; Susan Penner (now Deputy Mayor), Jake Hiebert, Damian Penner and Bill Hiebert return. Michael Zwaagstra and Jac Siemens did not run. Apart from the mayors, they took part in more of the requests in this record than anyone else, and in the recorded split votes below they were usually on opposite sides. The new councillors, Ron Falk (a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years) and Greg Carlos (a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association), have no record on requests yet. The routes, deadlines and budget calendar stay the same unless the new council changes the procedure by-law.

By council member

Ten people served on council in these years, and two more joined in November 2026. This section describes what each of them did on requests from residents and groups: what they raised, moved and voted for or against, and what they said. It is a record, not a score.

Read with care

Most requests were decided unanimously or by consensus, and councillors take turns moving routine motions, so moving a motion is not the same as championing it (see Who moves council's public-hearing motions). The mayor chairs and so handles most letters, which is why the mayors appear most often. Recorded split votes are the clearest evidence of where members differed, and they are listed first. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings; remarks are attributed to one of them only where the speaker is certain.

Earl Funk

Councillor 2014–2018; Mayor since November 2018

As a councillor he turned residents' concerns into motions (asking the school division to freeze school taxes, 2016; the Canada Summer Jobs letter for local groups, 2018), held the line on drainage policy (2016), opposed the golf-club land deal as a bailout (2017) and criticized the grants process using the Pat Porter case (2018).

As mayor he chairs, so he decides in practice how a letter is handled: most funding letters are received as information or sent to a study session, and he steers residents to book delegations. He championed the event centre (2019) and the economic development office (2021), put residents' street-racing letters on the agenda (2021), and changed position on the Clearspring Greens levy after meeting residents (2020). His promised answer to Southeast Cancer Support Services (2024) does not appear on the record.

“And when there is a young person that was injured or possibly injured and there could be chances for more, this is a serious request and we need to take it seriously.”

Chris Goertzen

Mayor until October 2018 (record from December 2015)

Chaired most meetings in 2015–2018 and usually decided how a letter was handled: “take that as information” or a staff report. He defended a “simplified and focused” grants policy, proposed tabling the Arts Council match before supporting it, and called the golf-club land purchase a win-win. He declared a conflict of interest and left the room for the 2016 drainage exception.

“for me, I'm not a fan of governing by referendum, and so I'll vote against it.”

Michael Zwaagstra

Councillor in all three terms; did not run in 2026

The most active member on requests after the mayors. He asked the cost-and-evidence questions and named the tests council applied (neighbourhood consensus, the engineers' warrant). He moved many same-night approvals and low-cost fixes (the Chapel Drive plan, the crosswalk letter to the province, Harv's Air, the Southwood school zone beyond staff's recommendation) and turned residents' street-racing letters into a motion for a traffic officer (2021).

He led the opposition to the golf-club deal and asked for it to be heard in public (2017), opposed the developer's demolition grant (2019) and shifting Clearspring Greens costs to all taxpayers (2020), moved the enVision levy (2020) and voted against the Loewen–Hespeler stop (2024).

Jac Siemens

Councillor in all three terms; did not run in 2026

In the recorded split votes he was the member most often on the side of the person asking: for a Sunday shopping plebiscite (2018), for a cheaper Clearspring Greens pathway (2020), against charging the enVision charity (2020), for the Loewen–Hespeler stop he moved for residents (2024) and against refusing a cannabis plebiscite (2025). He moved the Cottonwood parking ban against staff's advice (2022) and kept after the province, through staff, until highway signal timing was fixed (2023).

He was also cautious with money and precedent: he voted to table the Arts Council match (2018), opposed dropping Calvary Chapel's conditions (2018), voted against the city paying for Clearspring Greens lighting (2020), asked of Serving Seniors' request “Do we have a history?” (2024) and argued for ending the economic development grant (2025).

Councillor in all three terms; Deputy Mayor from November 2026

Context: why the residents' survey carried little weight, and how to do better

No council member criticized the survey's method on the record; this is our assessment of why it was easy to set aside. It was run by two affected residents, with no stated way of choosing homes, no count of homes that didn't answer, and no map. “Experienced sewer backup” was self-reported and not separated from overland flooding or seepage. The figures were a few sentences inside a long presentation that ended in questions rather than one request. And the provincial disaster-aid decision council then pursued turned on a different test, regional insurability (Adam Thiessen, Senior Manager of Corporate Services, Apr 21, 2026), which the survey didn't measure. The coalition's transit survey had a similar problem: most of its 751 respondents came from its own client groups, so it showed need among those groups but not city-wide demand.

A resident survey is more likely to count if it: covers every address in a defined area, with a map and a list of non-responses; uses neutral questions, ideally shown to staff first; measures what the decision turns on (dates, type of damage, dollar losses, insurance denials, backwater-valve status); comes with a short written summary of the method; and ends in a single request. Even then, expect council to treat it as a reason to collect its own data, and ask in writing for that data to be published.

Key findings

1Money is the hardest ask, and the least visible decision

Nearly every grant request in eleven years went to closed budget sessions, and groups learn the result, if at all, from the budget; of 57 requests involving money, 32 got no public answer. The few decided in public were large partnerships that arrived with private money and a drafted agreement (the event centre, 2019; the economic development office, 2021). Council's reason, that requests should be weighed together (reason 1), is sound. Brandon, Winkler and Portage weigh them together too, but through a grant program with an application form, criteria and a deadline. That tells groups what to bring and when, and gives council a list it can publish. Without one, a strong pitch like Southeast Cancer Support Services' can disappear.

2The rules that decide requests are not written where residents can see them

Requests are judged by tests residents rarely see in advance: the traffic warrant guideline (reason 2), an unwritten bar for “neighbourhood consensus” (reason 3), and precedent (reason 5). Groups that won either learned the test from staff first or happened to meet it. Publishing the warrant guideline, the counts behind each report, and what council expects in a neighbourhood request would save residents and staff time.

3Requests are referred, but not tracked

“Refer to administration” is council's usual first step, and reports came back in 2 to 10 months. But no list of open referrals is published, so when one stalls (the 2022 water co-op, the 2024 cancer-support grant, a 2026 school-safety report) nobody is prompted to ask. On provincial matters council passed resolutions but did not follow up when the province didn't act (reason 4). A simple public log of referrals, with a due date and an outcome, would close the gap.

4Surveys run one way

Council relies on surveys it commissions and has not engaged with the ones residents bring, even when, as in Georgetown, the residents' concern drove a budget decision. The city also doesn't report back its own survey results in detail. Selkirk, a smaller city, published its plan survey's numbers and how they were used; Red Deer and Camrose do the same for budgets. Advocates can raise the quality of their own surveys (see the checklist), but they also need a city that says what it heard.

Part 3 · A playbook for advocacy groups

Step by step, from first idea to follow-up

Drawn from what worked in the record above, Steinbach's procedure by-law, and peer practice. Process details can change; confirm deadlines with the City Clerk's office.

1Before you ask

  • Find out who decides. Main Street and Highways 12 and 52 are provincial; policing priorities go through the RCMP and council's advisory group; schools are Hanover School Division's; disaster aid is the province's. Council can “add its voice” with a resolution. Ask for one, then follow up with the province yourself, citing it.
  • Write the ask in one line, with a cost, who pays, and what your group will contribute. “A letter of support,” “consent to apply for a provincial grant” and “$70,000 toward a $212,000 project we will fund two-thirds of” are asks council can act on; “a partnership” is not.
  • Talk to staff first. Ask which department handles it, what standard or warrant applies, and whether counts or reports already exist. Requests designed with staff and the RCMP passed on the night.
  • Find a council member who will understand the issue, and ask them to read your material before the meeting. Don't let it be your only route: councillors' motions lost when they ran against a staff report.
  • Read council's term priorities and the budget calendar, and say how your request fits.

3Presenting

  • Book through the City Clerk's office at least 120 hours (five days) ahead, give your topic, and send a written summary for the agenda package. Walk-ins are not heard.
  • One spokesperson, about 10 minutes. Bring a one-page handout. Have others attend; a full gallery is noticed, but council has said it wants one voice.
  • Open and close with the ask. Council's questions in the record were about cost certainty, who else is paying, peer examples and measurable results; have those answers ready.
  • Ask for a decision and a date. If it is referred, ask when the report or budget decision will come back.
  • At a public hearing, stay on the application. Other issues are ruled out of order. Petitions are accepted but are not formal objections; a formal objection to a zoning by-law now needs at least 300 eligible people.

4Evidence that counts

  • Match the test. If a warrant guideline decides it, bring counts at the times that matter, speed readings or dated photos. If disaster aid turns on insurability, record insurance denials and losses.
  • Show who agrees: signatures with addresses from the people affected, not just supporters city-wide. Research on planning meetings finds that speakers are often unrepresentative of their neighbourhoods (Einstein, Palmer & Glick, 2019), and councils know it.
  • If you run a survey, follow the checklist in Surveys and publish the method: who ran it, exact questions, which addresses, how many didn't answer, and dates, as the AAPOR disclosure standards require of pollsters. An opt-in or self-selected survey shows that a problem exists, not how common it is.
  • Peer examples help. Council asked several groups whether other municipalities were doing the same thing.

5After the meeting

  • Track it. Read the minutes and the next agendas. If a promised answer doesn't appear, write to the Clerk and your councillor asking for it to be put on an agenda.
  • Check the budget. Grants are adopted in the financial plan each spring after a public hearing, where you can ask whether your request was funded.
  • Say thank you, and report back each year. Groups with ongoing funding kept it by returning with local results.
  • Talk to the city before the media. Council objected when a request reached the press before the city had heard it; media coverage afterwards was welcomed as adding “profile.”

About this page

  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts. The one exception is the city's name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to its moment in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Speakers were matched to names by hand from how they are addressed in each meeting, and every quote here is from a speaker identified with high confidence. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations and other members of the public, including people presenting for organizations, are identified only by initials. Organizations and businesses are named.
  • Coverage. Every regular council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos and minutes from January 2018 on (the city's older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (September to December 1, 2015, not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist; and three meetings with no minutes online (July 17, 2018; May 19 and November 17, 2020), where the video was used. Committee and study sessions are closed and unrecorded, so funding outcomes decided there are mostly unknown; where we could not find an outcome, the tables say “No answer.” Classifying a result as won, partly, no answer or lost is our judgment, and the reasons are recorded for each request in the data.
  • What counts as a request. 164 requests from residents, community groups, businesses and institutions such as the school division, including those a council member raised for residents. Not counted: council's own initiatives, requests from other governments and municipal associations, routine agency reports, and public-hearing objections to planning applications (see Public notice). Several routine funding letters from the same year are grouped into one row, so counts are of requests, not letters. Funding letters that were only “received as information” count as “No answer”; other letters filed without action count as “Lost” only where council clearly declined.
  • Speakers before 2022. Meetings from January 2022 have speaker maps checked by hand. For earlier meetings, speakers were identified from how they were addressed and from the minutes; only speakers identified with high confidence are quoted by name. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often could not be told apart, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain.
  • Data. The data downloads list every request, every council member's part in it, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence, and leave out any that name a member of the public. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Not confirmed. Whether the Eastman Raiders, Steinbach Minor Hockey or Southeast Cancer Support Services were funded in a budget; whether the Southwood school-zone by-law amendment has been passed; and which neighbourhood the mayor meant by the delegation that “asked for a path.” The safety survey count (827 adults and 33 youth) is from the published plan; at the March 3, 2026 meeting the mayor recalled it as 872. For 2015–2017, where there are no minutes, decisions come from the recordings and most were made by consensus without a counted vote; the December 5, 2017 golf-club vote is inferred from members' stated positions. Later results not traced: whether the 2021 request for red-light camera authority was granted, and whether the Legion got space in the event centre.
  • Peers and law. Peer practices come from each city's own website and by-laws, checked November 6, 2026. Morden's website blocks automated reading, and some Winkler details (its procedure by-law and petition rules) could not be found online. The 300-person objection threshold is from S.M. 2025, c. 3.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Council membership by term is from the minutes' attendance lists and the inaugural minutes of 2018 and the 2026 oaths of office; the 2026 election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts). Process rules may change with a new procedure by-law.
