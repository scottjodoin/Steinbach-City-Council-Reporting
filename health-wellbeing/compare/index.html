Health and wellbeing · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How does Steinbach City Council’s work on doctors, clinics, the hospital, ambulances, mental health, seniors’ care and healthy living compare with other Manitoba councils and a few comparable towns elsewhere, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong on advocacy and recreation buildings. Steinbach’s reserved-seats idea became a priority for all of Manitoba’s cities, and its event centre and pool are large investments for a city its size.
  • Behind on money and space for primary care. Niverville runs its own clinic; Winkler and Morden raise special levies for their hospital and clinics; towns elsewhere pay doctors to come and stay. Steinbach has no recruitment budget line and no clinic-space program.
  • Little on mental health and walking routes. Council took no action on a federal overdose fund, and its walking and cycling plan has no funded project, while neighbours built a 10 km pathway for a $750,000 municipal share.

Key findings

1Advocacy is council’s clearest health success

Reserving medical-school seats for rural students came from Steinbach’s 2024 task force, and the mayor credits Councillor Susan Penner with the idea. Manitoba’s cities adopted it in 2025, council backed a Brandon medical campus, and in January 2026 the mayor reported a minister’s letter promising rural doctors “streamed for family medicine.” It cost the city almost nothing, which fits council’s view that health care is the province’s job (reason 1). Its results will take years, and no count of doctors recruited has been reported.

2The small money question was left open

The task force asked for about $20,000 a year, modest beside Tillsonburg’s $70,000 or Killarney-Turtle Mountain’s $245,000. The mayor said in July 2024 he wanted to “act now”, but the package went to the budget and no decision appears at a recorded meeting (reason 7). Councillor Zwaagstra’s question, whether recruited doctors stay, has a standard answer elsewhere: pay on a five-year commitment, with repayment if a doctor leaves early (reason 3). The task force’s chair came from Steinbach Economic Development, whose city grant ended in December 2025 without recruitment being discussed.

3Space, not interest, is the next hurdle, and space is a city tool

By 2026 doctors were coming: a new clinic in the mall and a medical office permit on Main Street. Councillors said the city needed more clinic space. Land, zoning and building partnerships are municipal tools. Niverville, a third of Steinbach’s size, took over its clinic for $1 in 2020 when its founding doctors could not continue, runs it through a non-profit whose board includes all of council, and grew from 12 doctors (2024) to 15 (February 2026). Strathmore, Alberta pays toward new clinic offices and reports four more doctors.

4Hospital money: once a steady partner, now undecided in public

From 2018 to 2022 the city gave the Bethesda Foundation at least $1.5 million, and earlier help went to the Rest Haven care home. Since the foundation asked for $3 million over ten years in 2023, no decision has been recorded, and the 2026 plan shows no Bethesda line. Winkler and Morden made their hospital pledge in public and set a dedicated levy to pay it, so residents can see the cost (reason 5, reason 8).

5Mental health and addictions were left to others

Council’s one direct choice, the 2024 federal overdose fund that only a municipality could apply for, ended in “take no action” on staff’s advice that health programs are outside the city’s duties (reason 2). Local agencies and the province carry the work; the city’s wellbeing plan names the gaps (addiction services, a mental-health space at the hospital outside the emergency room) but assigns no owner, cost or date.

6Healthy living: big buildings, small paths

Steinbach invested heavily in recreation buildings, and the outdoor pool is next. The cheaper levers lag: walking and cycling routes have no funded project from the 2025 plan, and fees are set to recover costs (reason 4). Neighbouring Winkler, Morden and Stanley show a regional pathway can be built with federal money for a small municipal share.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Decide the task force package in public, about $20,000 a year, and report results each year: residents hosted, doctors recruited, patients attached.
  • Tackle clinic space with city tools: land, zoning, or a Niverville-style partnership for a clinic or clinic-daycare building.
  • If paying a recruiter or incentive, pay only on success, tie it to a four- to five-year stay and a minimum patient roster with prorated repayment, and ask Southern Health to share the cost.
  • Answer the Bethesda Foundation in public, and if the city contributes, consider a dedicated, time-limited levy as Winkler and Morden did, so residents see the cost.
  • Keep pressing for the rural medicine stream and pair it with hosting residents, since doctors often stay where they train.
  • Give the wellbeing plan’s health actions owners, timelines and costs, starting with advocacy for addiction services and a mental-health space at the hospital outside the emergency room, and say how the city will respond to the next fund only it can apply to.
  • Fund the first walking and cycling projects, including routes to the hospital and clinics, using federal Active Transportation money.
  • Publish what the seniors task force finds, with a cost for each recommendation and who would pay.
