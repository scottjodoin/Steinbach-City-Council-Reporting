Quality of life · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s rules, plans and programs for noise, flags and proclamations, newcomers and inclusion, accessibility, parks and trees, recreation and the arts compare with other Manitoba cities, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong on facilities, on par on accessibility and green space. The event centre and renovated pool put Steinbach’s recreation facilities ahead of most peers; its accessibility plan is current, and its green space per resident is close to Brandon’s.
  • Behind on written rules and plans. The noise by-law is from 2003 with no fine schedule (Brandon and Morden have recent ones with set fines); there is no published flag or proclamation process, no urban-forest plan and no recreation or culture plan.
  • Newcomers and inclusion are handled at arm’s length. Like Winkler, Steinbach relies on a federally funded non-profit for settlement; Brandon and Portage la Prairie have Local Immigration Partnerships, and Morden runs its own immigration program. The city’s 2026 wellbeing plan names belonging and inclusion as a priority, without timelines yet.

Key findings

1Flags and proclamations are decided case by case

Council members disagree about whether City Hall should fly cause flags or issue proclamations, and peers differ too: Brandon, Portage la Prairie and Altona have raised the Pride flag; no city flag raising was reported for Winkler’s first Pride march in 2026. What Steinbach lacks is not a particular answer but a written one. Without a policy, requests are received as information with no stated reason, and residents can’t know in advance how a request will be treated. A policy either way (government flags only, or a request process like Portage la Prairie’s) would make the rule visible. See council’s reason 1.

2The city’s own plan names inclusion as a priority

The Community Safety and Well-Being Plan, finished in 2026 and built on a survey of 827 residents, lists community belonging and inclusion among its priorities, including more 2SLGBTQIA+ supports and cultural-safety training for frontline workers. Its actions have no timelines yet, and council has not discussed them on the record. Because the plan is the city’s own document, acting on it does not require taking a position on flags. See reason 3.

3Noise rules are more than 20 years old

Complaints about racing and loud vehicles came up often, and council relied on the RCMP and on requests to the province. Brandon (2023) and Morden (2025) have updated their by-laws with set fines; Steinbach’s 2003 by-law has none, and no complaint or enforcement numbers are published, so residents can’t see whether blitzes are working. See reason 7 and reason 4.

4Newcomer support runs through others

About 21% of Steinbach residents were immigrants in 2021, more than Brandon, Morden, Portage la Prairie or Selkirk; only Winkler (26%) was higher. Settlement services are run well by others with federal money, but the city has no welcoming strategy or partnership of its own, and in 2025 it chose not to renew its grant to the office running the federal immigration pilot. Portage la Prairie, with under half Steinbach’s immigrant share, has a Local Immigration Partnership. See reason 3.

5Green space is ample overall but traded one parcel at a time

Steinbach’s green space per resident is close to Brandon’s measured level. But council closed or realigned park reserve land many times, at least six in 2022–2026 alone, each time unanimously and usually saying the area still had more than required. Without a parks plan or a map of walking distance to parks, residents objecting at a hearing can’t check that claim. See reason 6.

6Big facilities, no plan for what comes next

The event centre and pool show council will invest in recreation. But the arts venue, the outdoor pool, floodlights, festival funding and pathway projects compete one at a time in unrecorded budget sessions, and several referrals never came back. Brandon shows that a Manitoba city can set those priorities in a published recreation and culture plan. See reason 2 and reason 8.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Update the 2003 noise by-law with a fine schedule and clear quiet hours, as Brandon and Morden have done, and publish complaint and enforcement numbers.
  • Adopt a written flag and proclamation policy, whichever way council decides (government flags only, or a request process), so requests are decided by rule rather than case by case.
  • Give the wellbeing plan’s belonging and inclusion goals owners and timelines, and report on them.
  • Define the city’s own role with newcomers, for example a welcoming strategy or a Local Immigration Partnership with the Chamber, as Brandon and Portage la Prairie have.
  • Adopt a recreation and culture plan that ranks the outdoor pool, sports fields, pathways and a possible performance venue, and decide the Arts Council’s operating grant for more than one year at a time.
  • Map park access against the Official Community Plan’s standards, and report public reserve land taken and given up each year.
  • Start an urban-forest plan with a tree inventory, as Selkirk has.
  • Report back on referrals such as the pool lift and Main Street accessible parking, with a yearly list of requests and outcomes.
