Quality of life · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons have Steinbach council members given since 2015 for what the city does, and doesn’t do, on flags and proclamations, newcomers, noise, parks and trees, recreation and the arts, and what facts and counterpoints sit beside them?

In short

Eight reasons recur. Some are about values, and members disagree on them: whether City Hall should take up causes through flags and proclamations. Most are practical: cost, rules and precedent, the role of other governments and community groups, and whether the city already has enough. The practical reasons are often sound on their own terms; the weak points are follow-through (referrals and requests that never came back) and the absence of written policies and plans that would let residents see the rule in advance.

  • 1. City Hall should stay out of causes
  • 2. It costs too much, or the budget is set
  • 3. Others should lead
  • 4. It’s another government’s call
  • 5. Fairness and precedent
  • 6. There’s already enough
  • 7. Enforcement belongs to the police, and they’re short-staffed
  • 8. Study it first

1City Hall should stay out of causes

Two councillors said plainly that City Hall should not fly cause flags, issue proclamations or apply diversity criteria. The mayor declined to fly the Ukrainian flag while backing practical help. Council did act on recognition when the federal and provincial governments had acted first.

“I'm proud that we don't spend our time making symbolic climate emergency declarations or issue a different proclamation for a new cause every week or raise multiple flags in front of City Hall.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, final opening remarks · Sept 1, 2026 · 0:00:11

“often general debate and discussion is readily labeled as uncompassionate, homophobic, racist, and even hate speech at seemingly a moment's blush. … It's not a giant leap to conclude that by hiring under DEI principles, you are working towards a key point in the definition of groupthink.”

Councillor Damian Penner, opening remarks · Oct 1, 2024 · 0:00:13

“I get it. I see the desire to have the Ukrainian flag flying, but we're Canadians. This is our Canadian flag. But I want to work hard to save and keep as many people safe as possible.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on a request to fly the Ukrainian flag · Mar 15, 2022 · 0:41:14

“I think we recognize and appreciate the important role of Indigenous people in Canada. They are the original peoples in, in our country, and there have been a lot of mistakes made by previous governments very serious mistakes”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving to recognize the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation · Sept 21, 2021 · 0:46:42
Context

Whether a city flies flags or issues proclamations is a values choice, and councils differ. The record shows what Steinbach did: proclamation requests (2018, 2019), a resident’s city-flag design (2019) and the Ukrainian flag request (2022) were received as information; Truth and Reconciliation Day (2021) and the Ukraine resolution (2022) passed unanimously. These positions were stated by individual members in debate and in opening remarks, not adopted as a policy, and we found no written flag or proclamation policy, so each request is decided as it comes. Outside council, residents raised Pride flags on their own poles at City Hall in 2022 after, organizers said, the city refused (CBC). The city’s own Community Safety and Well-Being Plan (2026), based on a survey of 827 residents, calls for more 2SLGBTQIA+ supports and cultural-safety training; those goals do not depend on flying any flag. Other Manitoba councils have raised the Pride flag (Brandon since 2016; Portage la Prairie, 2024; Altona, 2022); no city flag raising was reported for Winkler’s first Pride march (2026), and Morden’s mayor spoke at the region’s first Pride parade there in 2019. See How Steinbach compares.

2It costs too much, or the budget is set

“$24 million for a performing arts centre, $12 million for this year, $12 million for next year, borrowing $2.5 million this year. It dwarfs all of the other capital plans.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, at the 2017 budget hearing · Feb 21, 2017 · 0:16:03

“Yeah, because the challenge is once we do set budget, our budget is fixed.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on a football club’s request for about $70,000 toward floodlights · May 6, 2025 · 0:34:55

“That renewal project has been on the city's long-term capital program in various forms over the past at least 10 to 12 years. It is a challenge to find funding for funding sources that, that will fit recreation projects”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on the outdoor pool · Sept 16, 2025 · 0:52:50

“The price tags are very high, and so no one should look at this and assume that, okay, all of this is going to happen in the near term.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the Active Transportation Plan · May 6, 2025 · 1:03:48
Context

Cost was the most common reason in this record (13 items), and council did spend when it chose to: it kept the Dutch elm program at about $20,000 a year more (2016), built the soccer-park washrooms, renovated the Aquatic Centre from reserves (2020) and built the event centre. The cost argument also cut the other way: council opposed the provincial pesticide ban because organic products would raise lawn care from $18,000 to over $200,000 a year. Where it said no, the request usually went to unrecorded budget sessions, and outcomes such as the floodlights and Summer in the City’s 2027 request were not reported in a public meeting. Without a recreation or culture master plan, residents cannot see how such requests are ranked.

3Others should lead

“Okay, so usually that's how they call it, pilot project, and then all of a sudden, it's great for the community, you get involved, and before you know it, you're funding it 100%.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on the federal Rural Community Immigration Pilot · Mar 18, 2025 · 1:22:05

“And while loneliness is not a municipal responsibility, the fact is the health and well-being of, of our community is important to us. That's why spaces like our new Southeast Event Centre matter.”

Councillor Susan Penner, opening remarks · Jun 17, 2025 · 0:00:47
Context

Much of this work is in fact done by others. Newcomer settlement is run by the Chamber of Commerce’s Eastman Immigrant Services with federal money (about $2.6 million for 2025–2028); the city gave the Chamber $170,000 in 2026, purpose not itemized. Festivals are run by volunteers, and the mayor suggested in 2026 that a community group lead any planning for a theatre, as donors did for the event centre (SteinbachOnline). The question is what the city’s own role is. In August 2025 council chose not to renew its grant to the economic development office that runs the immigration pilot, and no city newcomer or welcoming strategy has been proposed. Peers differ: Brandon and Portage la Prairie have Local Immigration Partnerships, and Morden runs its own immigration program (about 50 families a year in a 2018 report).

4It’s another government’s call

“We've lobbied for many years asking The province to lift the ban on cosmetic pesticides”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jun 6, 2023 · 0:35:32

“It's unfortunate that the funding formula was changed in 2023 … basically a better set of rules from the province”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on ending regional library talks · Jan 20, 2026 · 0:11:56

“Well, we know that we hit June 30th and we did not receive all the funding, and so the project will not move forward in its present form.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen, on the performing arts centre · Jul 4, 2017 · 0:21:01
Context

These are real limits: highway signs need the province’s approval, red-light cameras need provincial permission, pesticide rules are provincial, and the arts centre depended on federal and provincial money. Lobbying sometimes worked: the province loosened the pesticide ban and the city resumed weed control in 2023. But no reply to the 2021 red-light camera request or the 2022 engine-brake signs request appears on the record, and some tools are the city’s own: its noise by-law, last passed in 2003, could set fines as Brandon (2023) and Morden (2025) have done.

5Fairness and precedent

“We have a city policy, and the city policy is one that requires planting of trees and shrubs and curbing that accommodates that, and it's a good policy.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, refusing a tree waiver · Jan 15, 2019 · 0:29:46

“We do have a longstanding policy over the last number of years anyway of what you stated, which is a fairly simplified and focused policy.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen, to the Pat Porter seniors’ centre · Feb 20, 2018 · 0:30:27

“let's actually redo those sections of the policy and do this on a proper permanent basis and look at some of those things because we're setting a precedent here.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving to table the Arts Centre match · Jun 19, 2018 · 1:08:21

“It is difficult, and I like green spaces, especially in areas that don't have, but The overriding principle for me here is what's good for Steinbach and our senior housing list.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on seniors’ housing on Friesen Avenue · Jun 2, 2026 · 0:47:40
Context

Applied consistently, rules protect residents from one-off deals, and the tree rule was enforced both times it was tested. Members did not always agree on when a rule should bend: Councillors Siemens and Susan Penner voted for both tree waivers, arguing trees would not survive in asphalt. Councillors Funk and Susan Penner argued in 2018 that the grants policy was applied unevenly, since the golf club got a land deal while the seniors’ centre got nothing. And the Clearspring Greens neighbourhood’s share of its pathway was cut twice after residents objected, showing that the “who pays” formula can be negotiated.

Context

The city reports 260 acres of parks and green space (2026 budget), about 5.9 hectares per 1,000 residents at the 2021 census count (our calculation; definitions differ between cities). Brandon reported 6.98 hectares per 1,000 in its 2015 Greenspace Master Plan. 7Enforcement belongs to the police, and they’re short-staffed

“with respect to other matters pertaining to noise or other nuisances, the City of Steinbach does have bylaws that regulate those items.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, to neighbours complaining about a home garage · Mar 1, 2016 · 0:30:39

“We're often 2, 3, or 4 officers short of the amount that we have requested, and of course that affects the ability to do proper enforcement.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on Main Street racing · May 6, 2025 · 1:13:46

“being a business on Main Street, I've seen a huge reduction in the racing and the revving of cars and just the noise.”

Mayor Earl Funk, after the traffic officer started in 2022 · Apr 2, 2024 · 0:44:48
Context

Council did act within this approach: it asked for a dedicated RCMP traffic officer (2021), credited it with fewer complaints, and backed joint enforcement when complaints rose again (2025). But the city’s own rule is old. Noise Control By-law 1662 (2003) bans “unreasonably loud or unusual” sounds and sets hours for power tools and vehicle repair, with no decibel limits and no fine amounts; penalties are by reference to The Municipal Act. No review of it came to council in this record. Complaint and enforcement numbers are not published.

“I think it's important to look at how we can be as accessible as possible as a city.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen, supporting a study of Main Street accessible parking · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:37:18

“I would rather see us doing something where we maybe go and have a concrete plan to move forward and not just throw money at something”

Councillor John Fehr, on tabling the Arts Centre match · Jun 19, 2018 · 0:59:26

Context

Studying first can lead to better decisions: the Arts Centre match passed unanimously once the group presented its plan. But several referrals never came back in public: the Main Street accessible-parking study (2017), a strategy for the emerald ash borer (2017) and the outdoor-pool lift (2019). The 2016 request for dog park options produced no report; the dog park was built in 2021 after residents kept asking. Committee and study sessions are not recorded, so residents can’t tell whether these were looked at and dropped or forgotten.
