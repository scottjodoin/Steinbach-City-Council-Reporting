Quality of life · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council’s handling of noise, newcomers and inclusion, flags and proclamations, parks and trees, recreation, and arts and culture change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

Some things stayed the same across all three councils: parks and trees were funded without dissent, park reserve land was traded for development when a project needed it, noise was treated as a policing matter, and newcomer services were left to the Chamber of Commerce. What changed was the scale. The first council’s arts ambitions ended with the performing arts centre in 2017; the second built practical facilities and acted on recognition when senior governments led; the third adopted the city’s first pathway and community plans, and its members spoke openly, on both sides, about flags, proclamations and belonging.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

The multiplex dropped and the performing arts centre ended; Dutch elm, playgrounds and soccer-park washrooms funded; an Arts Council grant tabled, then approved.

Mayor Funk’s first council

Pool renovation, first dog park, the Clearspring Greens pathway; street-edge trees enforced; Truth and Reconciliation Day and a Ukraine resolution.

Mayor Funk’s second council

The first Active Transportation Plan and a new community plan, park reserves closed, the library kept independent, and symbolic gestures debated in openings.

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, the member who spoke most often against flags, proclamations and diversity criteria and who moved many of the recognition and recreation motions, did not run; nor did Jac Siemens, council’s library liaison and its most frequent voice for pathways and green space. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), was named council’s representative on the Jake Epp Library Board at the inaugural meeting. Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association, holds elected office for the first time. Susan Penner, who topped the poll, is Deputy Mayor.

What the new council inherits:

  • Grant decisions due in November 2026: whether the Arts Council’s operating top-up (from $55,000 to $85,000, approved for one year) becomes permanent, and Summer in the City’s request for 2027.
  • The outdoor pool, nearly 50 years old, with a $3.85 million renewal waiting on a provincial grant application made in October 2026.
  • The wellbeing plan’s inclusion goals. The city’s Community Safety and Well-Being Plan (2026) names belonging and inclusion as a priority, including more 2SLGBTQIA+ supports and cultural-safety training, with timelines still to be set.
  • Unwritten rules: no written flag or proclamation policy was found, and the 2003 noise by-law has no fine schedule.
  • Unfunded plans: the Active Transportation Plan’s projects, and no parks, urban-forest, recreation or culture master plan. The library’s request for security cameras was left undecided in July 2026.

The new council’s inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026 made its board appointments; it had no other quality-of-life decisions.
