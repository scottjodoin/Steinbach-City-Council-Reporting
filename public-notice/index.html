Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Public notice and having a say

Public notice and having a say: do residents’ objections count?

When a rezoning, variance, conditional use, subdivision or plan change is proposed near their homes, how has Steinbach told residents since December 2015, and what chance did they have to respond? How often did their objections change council’s decision, how did each council member act, and how does this compare with other Manitoba and Canadian cities?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026. This report replaces our October 2026 page on 2022–2026 and corrects it (see About this report).

Executive summary

For eleven years Steinbach has heard every resident who came to a public hearing, and large neighbourhood turnouts have stopped some projects. But the city’s notice has stayed at the legal minimum, and the formal ways to object have narrowed.

  • Notice is the provincial minimum. Owners within 100 metres get a letter at least 14 days before a hearing; mail counts as received four days after it is sent, so neighbours can have about 10 days. Since 2024 the letter is clearer, and notices go online 13 days ahead with an email subscription and an online comment form. There is no early letter when an application is filed, tenants get nothing, and whether a sign is posted on the site could not be confirmed.
  • Everyone is heard, and objections sometimes win. Of roughly 275 planning hearings from 2016 to October 2026, about 74 drew objecting neighbours. Objections changed about 20 of those results: about 13 denials and 7 approvals with changes, such as a fence, a height cap or more parking. Staff re-ran hearings when they found errors in their own notices (2022, 2025).
  • Big turnouts can stop infill in the middle of a block; small ones rarely change anything. Large petitions and full galleries defeated townhouses on Maplewood Street (2018), an apartment on Hanover Street (2019), 32 units on Third Street (2023) and 20 units on McKenzie Avenue (2026, against staff advice). But a 43-name petition did not stop 32 units on Home Street (2024), and since November 2022 no application with fewer than about six objecting households has been denied. The mayor says a majority against “doesn’t mean we won’t vote for it.”
  • The province has nearly closed the formal objection route. Before June 2018 any objection to a rezoning could send it to the Manitoba Municipal Board. Then it took 25 voters or half the neighbours within 100 metres; since June 3, 2025 it takes 300 voters; and since June 1, 2026 the Board only reviews the file and advises council. Variances and conditional uses were never appealable. Councillor Michael Zwaagstra welcomed the first change in 2019; council did not discuss the later ones in any meeting we found.
  • Outside the hearing, residents’ tools are thin. Talking to neighbours first is up to the developer. A 2022 request to postpone a hearing so neighbours could prepare was not acted on, and no hearing in the record was put off to give residents more time. Traffic and drainage studies done for developers are not published. Turnout and survey results from the 2023–2025 plan rewrite were never reported back.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council turned neighbours’ requests into conditions and, in 2018, refused several projects that drew large opposition. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council made the developer’s own consultation the main test, while a provincial change meant a handful of objectors could no longer send a rezoning to the Municipal Board. His second council improved the notice letter and denied four heavily opposed projects, while approving most infill, and the province raised the objection bar to 300. The council elected in October 2026 starts under the new rules.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

One objection could send a rezoning to the Municipal Board; neighbours’ requests became conditions; four projects denied after large opposition in 2018.

33 items · 12 split votes · objections changed 8 of about 19 contested results

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

“Did the developer talk to the neighbours?” became the test; 78 owners rewrote a pathway levy; four or five objectors were no longer enough for a Board hearing.

48 items · 21 split votes · objections changed 6 of 32

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

A clearer notice letter, four heavily opposed projects denied, most infill approved, and the province’s 300-objector rule.

43 items · 17 split votes · objections changed 6 of 23

2026–2030

The new council

Michael Zwaagstra and Jac Siemens are gone; Ron Falk and Greg Carlos are new. The first council to work entirely under the 300-objector rule and paper-only Board reviews.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents, consultants and other members of the public are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts (“impact impact”). The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added and no two speaking turns were joined. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence: from hand-checked speaker maps for meetings from January 2022, and for earlier meetings from how speakers were addressed and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations, the city’s planning consultant and other members of the public are identified only by initials, including in quotes, tables and context boxes. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain. One question we report, A.A.’s at the April 1, 2025 plan hearing, sits on a transcript voice label shared by several public speakers; he identifies himself just before and the minutes record his question.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos, and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered, or covered by minutes only: the first 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021); the April 5, 2022 meeting (no audio); and some meetings with minutes but no transcript, including several in 2019, May 19, 2026 and November 5, 2024. Minutes for May 19, 2020 and December 6, 2022 are missing, and the October 20, 2026 minutes were not yet available. Committee meetings, the city’s correspondence and Municipal Board decisions were not reviewed.
  • Counting hearings and objections. A hearing is “contested” when at least one neighbour, business or group objected or raised concerns in a letter or in person. Counts for 2016–2017 come from the videos and are approximate; from 2018 they come from the minutes. City-wide by-laws (the new plan and zoning by-law) and local-improvement levies are counted separately. “Objections changed the result” means council denied the application, or approved it with changes or conditions answering objectors; we did not count denials where staff had already recommended denial and the objection was not the deciding factor (295 Lowen Blvd, 2016), or design changes the applicant made before the hearing. Counting objectors and judging what changed a result is our judgment; treat the counts as approximate.
  • What counts as an item. 124 items: each contested hearing, each year’s uncontested hearings grouped together, notice errors and changes, Municipal Board referrals, provincial rule changes, plan engagement, requests for studies or more time, and local-improvement hearings where objections mattered. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data. In the record, “done” means approved as applied.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017 except July 18, 2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so who voted which way is taken from what members said.
  • Law and peers. Planning Act wording is from the official consolidation (current to Nov 5, 2026) and the amending acts. Peer practices come from each city’s own notices, by-laws and web pages, checked November 7, 2026; see How Steinbach compares for what could not be confirmed.
  • Not confirmed. Whether Steinbach posts a notice sign on application sites (its 2015 application guides say notices are “posted on the subject property,” but no notice, minutes or web page confirms it); which newspaper carries the legal notice for zoning and plan hearings; whether notices go on the city’s Facebook page; whether Procedures By-law 1809 (2006) has been amended; the Municipal Board’s decisions on By-laws 2217 (205 McKenzie Ave) and 2241 (Home St) and third reading of 2241; why the 2018 objection threshold, passed in June 2018, was not applied in Steinbach until 2019 (see By term).
  • Corrections to our October 2026 page. That page rated the lack of a property sign a confirmed “Gap”; the city’s own guides say signs are posted, so it is now shown as unconfirmed either way. The 2018–2025 rule also let half of the owners within 100 metres trigger a Board referral, and Brandon’s zoning by-law requires applicants to reach out to neighbours and file a report, a stronger point than we made. A short Q&A was already in the January 2024 notice. The 2026 Municipal Board change is S.M. 2026, c. 26. Morden can now be partly compared. Red Deer, on the old page, was not re-checked and is no longer compared. The city’s planning consultant is now identified by initials, like other people who are not city staff.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026, with no hearings. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
