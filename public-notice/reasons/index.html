Public notice and having a say · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for approving projects over neighbours’ objections, and for not doing more to notify and involve residents, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given the same eight reasons for a decade. Most are sound planning practice: decisions should rest on the plan, staff advice and the facts of a site, not on a head count. The weaker points are what residents are asked to take on trust (traffic, drainage and studies they cannot see), what is left to developers (talking to neighbours first), and the assumption that meeting the Planning Act’s minimum means the neighbourhood knows.

1A hearing is not a vote: we judge the project against the plan

“It's not that just because everyone comes out against it or there's a majority against it doesn't mean we won't vote for it. It's just we vote it and we look at it on the basis of the project and what it does and what it will bring to the community.”

Mayor Earl Funk, at the hearing on the new zoning by-law · Apr 1, 2025 · 1:39:51

“We look at the plan that's presented. We look at the reaction of the neighbourhood. And a key thing is we consider how well does the proposed development fit the neighborhood”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving to deny a six-plex on Ellice Ave · Aug 18, 2020 · 0:59:29

“What is particularly significant is that there are no variances that are being requested here.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, approving 48 units on McKenzie Ave over a petition · Apr 7, 2026 · 1:23:24

“Now, when a neighborhood's not ready to transition, it won't work. It doesn't work as well.”

Mayor Earl Funk, denying 20 units at 330 McKenzie Ave · Oct 6, 2026 · 0:59:40
Context

This is how planning law is meant to work: council must weigh planning reasons, not count objectors. And council’s deciding tests have been fairly consistent: whether a project fits the character of the block, whether it sits mid-block among single-family homes or on a busy street, whether it falls in the plan’s transitional area, and whether it needs exceptions. The trouble for residents is that these tests are applied case by case and are not mapped in advance. Steinbach has an infill policy and a transitional area, but no map of where apartments are expected, so neighbours come not knowing whether anything they say can matter. At the Home Street height hearing, D.K. told council that “probably nothing that we're going to say here will sway that” (Mar 4, 2025); a year earlier E.W. asked “how much opposition does it take to be heard on this matter?” The record answers in part: since November 2022, every denial after objections came with about six or more objecting households, and five of the nine most-opposed applications were still approved.

Context

The need is real: the seniors’ building at 185 Friesen Avenue had a waiting list of almost 200, and infill on serviced lots is cheaper for the city than new streets. Housing need was cited in about 20 of the items in this record. But neighbours rarely got anything in return for absorbing the change. Fences, buffers and design limits residents asked for were usually left to the builder’s goodwill rather than made conditions (Woodhaven Avenue 2020, 600 Main Street 2024, William’s Creek 2026), and the mayor said fences are generally not required on new construction. At Home Street, objectors argued the project would replace affordable starter homes in a well-kept single-family area outside the transitional zone.

“When things are changing and things are evolving in a neighborhood, you go talk to your people around you and you make sure they're good with it, or you make adjustments accordingly.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the Clearspring Greens pathway · Jan 7, 2020 · 1:34:06

“Normally we don't do this, but a lot of councils do this. It's actually normal practice.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on adjourning the 185 Friesen Ave hearing · Mar 17, 2026 · 1:51:59
Context

Council has consistently rewarded developers who talk to neighbours first and punished those who don’t (Hanover Street 2019, Third Street 2023). “Part of the rule of law process is that there is an opportunity for anyone who wants to make an application for a variance to be able to appear before council in a public hearing. That public hearing must be properly advertised. Any owners in the nearby vicinity get proper notification.”

Context

Every hearing in the record was noticed as the Act requires, and staff restarted the process when they found their own errors (2022, 2025). The province also caught a 2024 subdivision change that needed a hearing. The city’s letter has improved, and notices now go online with an email subscription. But the Act sets a floor: owners only, within 100 metres, at least 14 days ahead, with mail “deemed to be received” on the fourth day after it is sent (s. 163(2)), so neighbours can have about 10 days. Residents described what that floor misses: “We only really had 9 days to put this together” (C.K., Nov 1, 2022); a truck-yard neighbour heard by email the same day (D.M., Jan 16, 2024); in 2021 a First Street neighbour said few people had seen the notice; in 2018 an objector said it was posted on only one of the two streets a site faced. Tenants get nothing unless the owner passes it on. The Act already allows notice by email to anyone who agrees in writing (s. 163(1)(c)), and the city’s own 2015 guides say notices are also posted on the property, which we could not confirm happens.
