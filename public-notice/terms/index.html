Public notice and having a say · By term

The story by term

How did the way Steinbach told neighbours about planning changes, and listened to their objections, change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

The basics never changed: a mailed notice to owners within 100 metres, a public hearing where anyone may speak, and a decision usually made the same night. About one hearing in four drew objecting neighbours, and objections changed about one in four of those results. What changed was the weight of a single objection. Under Mayor Goertzen one objector could hold a rezoning for a Municipal Board hearing; from 2019 it took dozens; since June 2025 it takes 300, and since June 2026 the Board no longer holds hearings at all. Objections changed outcomes most often in 2016–2018 (8 of about 19 contested files), less often after (6 of 32, then 6 of 23).

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

One objection could stop the clock; Councillor Zwaagstra turned neighbours’ requests into conditions; 2018 brought four denials after neighbours objected, including 44 townhouses on Maplewood Street.

33 items · 12 split votes

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

Developers’ own consultation became the test; 78 owners rewrote a pathway levy; Jac Siemens and Jake Hiebert were the votes most often with objectors.

48 items · 21 split votes

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

A clearer notice letter; four heavily opposed projects denied and five approved; the 300-objector rule and paper-only Board reviews.

How the formal objection route narrowed

A public hearing lets anyone speak, but under Manitoba’s Planning Act only a formal objection to a zoning by-law can make council wait for the Municipal Board before third reading. Variance and conditional-use decisions are “final and not subject to appeal” throughout (ss. 100, 109). The rules for zoning by-laws changed three times:

  • Before June 2018: any objection. One objector who renewed the objection after second reading sent a rezoning to the Board. In Steinbach the objector lost or dropped out every time (Bridge Park 2016–17, 24 Ellis Ave and 333 Lowen Blvd 2017). The Board held hearings on two 2018 rezonings with one or two objectors (First St and Brandt St), approved both, and added conditions, including a paved back lane on First Street.
  • June 4, 2018: 25 voters, or half the owners within 100 metres (S.M. 2018, c. 14). The change passed in June 2018, but Steinbach’s records show one or two objectors still sending rezonings to the Board later that year; the City Manager first described the new rule to council in July 2019, and in June 2020 told council that four or five objections were “not sufficient.” We could not resolve this difference. The Board went on to review Wyndham Estate Drive (2022), 205 McKenzie Ave (2023), Home Street (2024) and the new zoning by-law (2025).
  • June 3, 2025: 300 voters in a municipality of Steinbach’s size, and the 50%-of-neighbours route repealed (S.M. 2025, c. 3). No Steinbach by-law reached the Board afterwards; 62 letters against 330 McKenzie Ave (2026) would not have been enough.
  • June 1, 2026: a review on paper. On a referral the Board “must not hold a hearing or receive new evidence or representations”; it sends council recommendations that council must consider but need not follow (S.M. 2026, c. 26).

Local-improvement levies run under The Municipal Act, with their own threshold: “at least 25 or 10%” of the owners being charged, as the City Manager put it in 2021. That is why 78 objections from 146 Clearspring Greens lots carried weight in 2021.

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, who moved most hearing decisions in both directions for a decade, and Jac Siemens, the member who most often voted against contested approvals, did not run. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), and Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association, have no record on this topic yet. Susan Penner, the member most consistently in favour of infill, is Deputy Mayor; Jake Hiebert, the other member most often voting with objectors, returns.

What the new council inherits:

  • The new provincial rules. It is the first council to work entirely under the 300-objector threshold and paper-only Board reviews. The local hearing is now, in practice, neighbours’ only chance; the city’s notice does not explain the threshold or that variances and conditional uses cannot be appealed.
  • The new plan and zoning by-law (third readings Sept 16, 2025 and Jan 20, 2026). Emergency shelters, and from June 2026 transitional housing, are now conditional uses that need a hearing; the draft council endorsed in 2024 also made offices, restaurants and clinics in the transitional district permitted uses, with no hearing.
  • Unfinished files: third reading of the William’s Creek rezoning (second reading in August 2026, over neighbours’ requests to keep some lots single-family), and residents’ requests to see the drainage and sewer analysis behind new projects near Lift Station 1.
  • Procedure rules from 2006. By-law 1809 still describes an open question period and five-minute public comments that are not used in practice.

The new council’s inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026 held no hearings.
