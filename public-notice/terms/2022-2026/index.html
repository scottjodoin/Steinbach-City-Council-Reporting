Public notice and having a say · By term

2022–2026: Mayor Earl Funk’s second council

How did Steinbach City Council tell neighbours about planning changes and weigh their objections from November 2022 to October 2026, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • Big turnouts against mid-block infill won four times. Council denied 32 units on Third Street (2023), a truck yard beside homes on Tower Avenue (2024), a sixplex on Hanover Street (2025) and 20 units on McKenzie Avenue (2026, against staff advice, after 62 letters). No application with fewer than about six objecting households was denied.
  • But a majority against does not decide. Of nine applications with strong opposition, five were approved, including 32 units on Home Street (4–3, over a 43-name petition) and 48 units on McKenzie Avenue. The mayor says council votes on “what it will bring to the community,” not on the count.
  • The notice got clearer, but not wider. Since 2024 the letter explains hearings in plain language and accepts comments online or by QR code, and notices go on the city’s website with an email subscription. It still goes only to owners within 100 metres, about two weeks ahead; some objectors heard about hearings the same day.
  • The formal route nearly closed. Two contested rezonings and the new zoning by-law went to the Municipal Board under the old rules; the zoning by-law came back unchanged and the fourplex went ahead as applied. From June 2025 a referral needs 300 objectors, and from June 2026 the Board only reviews the file. We found no council discussion of either change.
  • Residents asked for more than a hearing. A 2022 request to postpone, repeated requests to see traffic and sewer studies, and flooded residents who came to speak in 2025 were not accommodated. The 2023–2025 plan rewrite had a workshop, surveys and an open house, but no report back on what residents said.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert.

What happened

2022–2023: nine days to organize, and a hearing that ran to the end

The term’s first meeting, on November 1, 2022, heard a fourplex at 205 McKenzie Avenue. A neighbour brought 22 letters of objection, said neighbours had little time to organize, and asked council to postpone the hearing 60 to 90 days and to do a traffic study:

“We only really had 9 days to put this together.”

C.K., resident speaking for 22 objecting households · Nov 1, 2022 · 1:03:45

Council did not discuss the request and gave second reading the same night, 5–2:

“I'll vote against the motion. I'll take the position of the people that live in that area and know that area.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Nov 1, 2022 · 1:24:53

Mayor Funk also voted against. The objections sent the by-law to a Municipal Board hearing in April 2023; third reading came in June 2024. In August 2023, 13 neighbours objected to 32 units on Third Street, with a petition of more than 40 names. The developer, who had not gone door to door, asked mid-hearing to put the file off two months so he could. Council refused, saying it would get the public’s input and decide that night, and that due diligence belongs before a hearing (see council’s reason 6).

When the developer then offered to withdraw, a resident asked that neighbours still be heard, and the mayor agreed:

“Even though the project's been pulled, I respect what you're saying and I will let everyone be heard.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Aug 1, 2023 · 1:01:15

Council denied it unanimously and kept the petition on file. In September 2023 the city began rewriting its plan and zoning by-law with a visioning workshop and an online survey.

2024: a truck yard denied, Home Street approved

In January about 17 letters, many arriving that day, and eight neighbours objected to a 19-truck yard beside homes and a school path on Tower Avenue. One said he had heard of the hearing only that day, by email. Council denied it unanimously. In March, 32 units on Home Street, across from the hospital, drew eight letters including a 43-name petition, and 12 speakers against and four for. The developer had left round-table invitations at 14 or 15 homes less than a week before; adjacent neighbours said they never got one.

“ultimately the decision is yours. And I'd just humbly like to ask, how much opposition does it take to be heard on this matter?”

E.W., resident · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:22:39

Council split on whose views count:

“The point of a public hearing is to hear both from the applicant and also to hear from the neighbourhood. There's a reason why we send out notices to the neighbourhood, because we want to hear feedback from the neighbourhood and we take that into consideration.… But the people we're really interested in hearing from are the people who actually live in the area.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, voting against · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:36:10

“I get it that there are people that don't live in the area that have experience. And I think we need to listen to their experience as well.”

Mayor Earl Funk, casting the deciding vote for · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:46:14

It passed 4–3 and went to a Municipal Board hearing in August 2024. In December council endorsed the draft plan and zoning by-law, which made emergency shelters a conditional use so that neighbours get a hearing. From late 2024 the mailed notice carried a plain-language Q&A; by January 2025 it accepted comments through the city’s website or a QR code.

2025: “this is where your voice matters”

At the April 1 hearing on the new plan, a resident asked how many people in Steinbach even knew about it:

“it's in our meetings, it's discussed and there's stories done on it. But if that's not well known, I guess we could try and do a better job”

Mayor Earl Funk · Apr 1, 2025 · 0:30:19

Turnout and survey results were not reported to council. Enough objections were filed against the new zoning by-law, mostly over shelters, to send it to the Municipal Board under the old 25-objector rule; the Board ordered no changes. In March council unanimously denied a sixplex in the middle of a single-family block on Hanover Street after eight letters; seconding the denial, Councillor Zwaagstra said it did not fit the character of the neighbourhood and that council needs to listen to neighbours.

Two weeks earlier the Home Street builder won a fourth, partly underground storey 3–2 over objections from four households; one neighbour said he expected nothing they said would sway the vote. In August staff caught a missing item in a variance notice and held the decision for a second hearing. In September, residents flooded days earlier came to a council meeting hoping to speak and were asked to book a delegation instead; two presented in October.

2026: adjourned for parking, denied for character

In March council adjourned the hearing on a seven-storey seniors’ building at 185 Friesen Avenue, on staff’s advice, because the plan had 10 parking stalls where 150 were required; it later passed with one stall per unit. At 290 Stone Bridge Crossing in July, residents of seven households spoke for over an hour, mostly about repeated sewer backups, and asked for an independent study:

“she asked, how were the proposed changes determined, and may we receive a copy of the results of any studies done to determine changes?… Once again, I am asking for a third-party study for our sewer systems. for Lift Station 1.”

M.R., resident, reading an email exchange with city staff · Jul 21, 2026 · 0:05:38

The City Manager said the developer’s drainage engineers work “under contract with the developer or landowner.” Council approved six storeys 6–1, raising parking from one stall per unit to 1.25; no study was offered.

“but public hearings do matter. We do listen. We do factor things in. Just because a decision— one disagrees with a decision doesn't mean you weren't listened to.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jul 21, 2026 · 0:57:45

In October, 20 units on a large lot at 330 McKenzie Avenue drew 62 letters, 28 of them delivered that evening, and a full gallery. Staff had recommended approval; council denied it unanimously:

“I believe council needs to listen to these concerned citizens who are objecting due to a multiplex building that does not fit the neighbourhood character.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, moving to deny · Oct 6, 2026 · 0:47:10

Where members differed

Most hearing decisions were unanimous. The splits were mostly about infill in established neighbourhoods and about parking. Jac Siemens voted against the 205 McKenzie fourplex at every reading and was the lone vote against Stone Bridge Crossing; Jake Hiebert was the lone vote against 288 Henry Street and voted against reduced parking twice. On Home Street, Damian Penner, Jake Hiebert and Michael Zwaagstra sided with the neighbours and lost 4–3; Susan Penner moved the approval, saying “the benefits outweigh the costs.”

2025–26Province raises the bar for formal objections to 300 and ends Municipal Board hearingsBefore June 2025, 25 objections to a zoning by-law or plan sent it to the Municipal Board for a hearing (explained by the city's consultant on Apr 1, 2025; Steinbach's new zoning by-law went this way).
