Public notice and having a say · By term

2014–2018: Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council

How did Steinbach City Council tell neighbours about planning changes and weigh their objections from December 2015 to November 2018, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • Most hearings were quiet. About 42 of roughly 52 land-use hearings in 2016–2017 drew no objector, and 20 of 29 in 2018. Notice was the mailed letter the Planning Act requires; staff reported only whether letters had come in, and letters that arrived on the day went into councillors’ mail bins.
  • One objection could stop the clock, but never stopped a project. A single formal objection held a rezoning or local improvement for the Manitoba Municipal Board. Every objector lost or dropped out, though the Board added conditions to two 2018 rezonings.
  • Objections changed 8 of about 19 contested results, the highest share of the three councils. In 2016–2017 neighbours’ requests became conditions (fences, access, a height cap) and an office plan for 80 Brant Street was refused. In 2018 four projects were denied after neighbours objected, including 44 townhouses on Maplewood Street (49 letters).
  • Talking to neighbours first was expected, not required. Council praised applicants who knocked on doors and relied on that, the infill policy and development agreements rather than formal conditions.
  • Having a say beyond hearings was limited. A 2016 survey on the arts centre drew a strong response, and the 2017 plan review promised consultation; but Councillor Zwaagstra said a motion to hear the public before the golf course land deal was defeated in a closed meeting.

Council: Mayor Chris Goertzen; Councillors Earl Funk, Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Cari Penner and John Fehr. This record starts in December 2015, the first meeting with a transcript.

What happened

2016: neighbours at the door, conditions in the motion

Every hearing opened with the City Manager reporting that notices had gone out under the Planning Act and whether any letters were on file; the chair then asked three times for objectors. On Hanover Street in February, no letters had been filed, but at least three neighbours across the street came to object to two three-storey apartment buildings. Council approved them, with one Councillor Penner against:

“No new development, you know, of a 3-story nature is popular with its neighbors. It never is.”

Councillor John Fehr, on 242–250 Hanover St · Feb 2, 2016 · 1:03:40

In July council rezoned 1.6 acres behind Albert Street homes for future seniors’ housing with no site plan at all. A neighbour objected, and Councillor Zwaagstra explained why council would not wait for a plan:

“because if you have even one objection, that can delay the process by months, and then that window of opportunity could be gone.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the Bridge Park rezoning · Jul 19, 2016 · 1:01:43

The lone objector later did not appear at the Board hearing. In October, two letters about a five-storey seniors’ building at 90 Brant Street asked for a fence and for access off Woodhaven Avenue rather than across a condo lot. Councillor Zwaagstra made both conditions:

“So I would suggest that we incorporate those 2 things as a condition of the second reading.…and then these would be letters of support as opposed to potential objections.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Oct 18, 2016 · 1:08:03

A neighbour’s concerns about lost winter light, traffic and property values were not addressed by conditions; the City Manager said there had never been “verified impacts on property values” from multifamily housing in the city. In December council denied a parking variance at 295 Lowen Boulevard that neighbours opposed, as staff had already recommended.

2017: a height cap on Ellis, 120 feet on Lowen, no to commercial on Brant

At 24 Ellis Avenue a neighbour asked for a full-length fence; council required a 6-foot wood fence and capped the six-plex at 1.5 storeys. At 333 Lowen Boulevard, Albert Street residents accepted a mixed-use rezoning but objected to a 120-foot building; council approved the full height in August. On Brant Street, Councillor Siemens moved first reading of a plan change to commercial so the neighbours could be heard:

“The reason I'm recommending that we pass first reading is so that we can go to a public hearing. I'd like to hear what the public has to say about it.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on 80 Brant St · Nov 7, 2017 · 0:05:40

At least five neighbours objected in December, not to the charity that wanted the offices but to commercial use creeping down a residential block. Council denied the plan change:

“there was a very good reason why we kept this area a few years back as a residential area, and those reasons still exist today, as we heard from the neighborhood.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen · Dec 5, 2017 · 0:37:22

The same night council bought 40 acres from the golf club. Councillor Zwaagstra said the public had been given no chance to comment; Councillor Fehr answered that every city land deal has been negotiated behind closed doors:

“Perhaps the public should have the right to hear about this proposal and have a chance to weigh in. That's defeated 4-3, and so here we are.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, describing a closed-meeting vote (no minutes exist to confirm it) · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:09:41

In September 2017 Mayor Goertzen promised that the coming review of the community plan and zoning by-law would “include public consultation.”

2018: four denials, two Board hearings

Nine of 29 hearings in 2018 drew objectors, and five ended differently because of them. Council denied a funeral home on PTH 52 over parking (five letters, seven speakers), an after-the-fact sunroom on Edgewood Street, and a small-lot split at First Street and Barkman Avenue, where neighbours recalled objecting to a similar plan in 2006. On First Street, two objectors sent townhouses to the Municipal Board:

“there will be additional notices given out to the 2 official objectors, and they will have a further opportunity to object. If that is the case, then this will move to the municipal board”

Mayor Chris Goertzen, on By-law 2092 · May 1, 2018 · 0:50:57

The Board approved the First Street and Brandt Street rezonings with conditions of its own, including a paved back lane. In August, 44 townhouses on Maplewood Street drew 49 letters with 84 signatures and ten speakers. One objector said notice had been posted on only one of the two streets the site faces:

“the posting for this was on Maplewood only, and we believe that the public notice should have been on both Maplewood and Stonebridge because it faces 2 directions.”

T.B., resident · Aug 21, 2018 · 0:35:23

“When you're going to have a change of this magnitude, we have to listen to neighbors, and we also have to take into consideration what things are presented by the developer.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving to deny · Aug 21, 2018 · 0:54:57

“I think for the residents that are there, I think we need to defend them.”

Councillor Earl Funk · Aug 21, 2018 · 1:02:04

Council denied second reading 5–1. A redesigned, two-storey plan for the same lots, from a new applicant who consulted the neighbourhood, was approved in 2020. Whether the 2018 “posting” was a sign on the property or the mail-out is not stated.

Where members differed

Most hearing decisions were unanimous. Councillor Susan Penner was the lone vote against denying the Maplewood townhouses, pointing to the plan’s designation of Maplewood as a collector street; she, Councillor Zwaagstra and Councillor Funk also backed a front-yard variance on First Street that staff and the majority refused. The Harmony Lane public reserve closing passed 4–3 over two neighbours who had bought for the green space. Before 2018 votes were by voice, so counts are from what members said.
