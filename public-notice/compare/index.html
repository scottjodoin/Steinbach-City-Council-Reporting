Public notice and having a say · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s notice of planning changes and residents’ chances to respond compare with Manitoba’s Planning Act and with other Manitoba and Canadian cities, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong at the hearing itself. Anyone may speak, letters count up to the end of the hearing, and council debates and votes in public. Several peers cut off written comments days earlier and limit speakers to five minutes.
  • The legal minimum before it. Steinbach mails owners within 100 metres about two weeks ahead and posts notices online 13 days ahead. It has no early notice when an application is filed, no required neighbourhood meeting, and no application file online. Brandon, under the same Planning Act, does all three.
  • Little explanation of the rules. The notice says petitions are not formal objections, but not that it now takes 300 objectors to reach the Municipal Board, or that variance and conditional-use decisions cannot be appealed.

The provincial objection rules (300 objectors since June 2025; Municipal Board review on the record since June 2026) are the same for every Manitoba municipality, so they are not rated. Winnipeg’s threshold changed the same way.

Key findings

1Steinbach meets the minimum, and the minimum is thin

Every hearing in the record was noticed as the Act requires, staff restarted the process when they found their own errors, and the notice letter and website have improved since 2024. But the Act’s floor reaches owners only, within 100 metres, about 10 days before the hearing. Residents quoted in this report learned of hearings by word of mouth, the same day, or not in time to organize. The next steps cost little and are allowed by the Act: confirm and publish whether signs go up, mail earlier or wider for larger projects, and offer email notice by address. This answers reason 7: meeting the law has not been the same as reaching the neighbourhood.

2Objections change outcomes in numbers, and the tests aren’t written down

About one contested application in four ended differently because of objections, and since 2022 every such denial followed strong opposition to infill in the middle of an established block. Council’s tests (character, mid-block or busy street, transitional area, variances) are reasonable, but they are applied case by case (reason 1, reason 5). A map of where apartments are expected would tell residents and builders in advance what council will weigh.

3Talking to neighbours first is optional here, required in Brandon

Council’s strongest test, across all three councils, is whether the developer talked to the neighbours (reason 6). Yet Steinbach leaves that to the developer, and what developers did ranged from an open house to nothing. Brandon writes it into its zoning by-law, with a report filed before the hearing is scheduled; Lethbridge and Kitchener add an early letter when the application arrives. Either would turn council’s expectation into a rule.

4The hearing night is now residents’ main chance

The province has made the formal objection route much harder: 300 objectors instead of 25, and a review on paper instead of a Board hearing. Variance and conditional-use decisions were never appealable, and the city’s notice says petitions are not formal objections. Council prefers to decide on the night (reason 6) and has welcomed limits on objectors before (reason 8). That makes the time residents have to prepare, and what the notice tells them about the rules, matter more than before.

5Residents are asked to trust studies they can’t see

Traffic, drainage and sewer capacity are the most common objections, and council answers them with staff advice and engineering by consultants the developer hires (reason 3, reason 4). Brandon and Kitchener post the application file, and Kitchener the studies themselves, before the hearing. Steinbach’s notice still sends residents to City Hall during office hours.

6Engagement on the big plan happened but wasn’t closed off

The 2023–2025 plan and zoning rewrite had the steps the Planning Act expects, and its hearings were well attended. But residents were never told how many took part or what changed because of them, and the mayor said there was no measure of how many people knew about the plan. Selkirk, a smaller city doing the same work in 2024, published its numbers and how feedback was used.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Confirm the sign, and say so. If notices are posted on sites, as the city’s guides say, mention it in the notice; if not, post them, as Brandon does under the same Act, so passers-by and tenants see them.
  • Send an early letter when an application is filed, as Lethbridge and Kitchener do.
  • Require neighbourhood outreach for infill, with a short report of who was contacted and what changed, as Brandon’s zoning by-law does.
  • Mail sooner or wider for larger projects, for example 21 days ahead or 150 metres for multi-unit buildings.
  • Offer email alerts by address, which the Act already allows with written consent (s. 163(1)(c)), as Winnipeg does by radius.
  • Put the application file online with each notice: site plan, staff report, and any traffic or drainage study the developer submits.
  • Publish written submissions, or at least their count and a summary, with the agenda, as Lethbridge and Saskatoon do; Councillor Susan Penner asked in 2023 that objections be formally counted.
  • Explain the rules in the notice: what counts as a formal objection, the 300-person threshold, and that variance and conditional-use decisions cannot be appealed.
  • Say when council will adjourn a hearing so residents can respond to new information, not only when staff or applicants need time.
  • Report back on engagement with turnout, survey results and what changed, as Selkirk did, and adopt a short public participation policy.
  • Map where infill density is expected, so hearings test a project’s design rather than whether apartments belong on the block at all.
  • Use the openings in By-law 1809: let residents ask questions at the open session it describes, and publish how to book a delegation.
