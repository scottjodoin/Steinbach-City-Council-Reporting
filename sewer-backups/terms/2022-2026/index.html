Sewer backups and flooding · By term

2022–2026: Mayor Earl Funk’s second council

How did Steinbach City Council respond to the basement sewer backups and flooding of September 2024 and September 2025, what did it do before them, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • Before the storms, sewers meant growth. Lift Station 2 was upgraded so east-side subdivisions could proceed; the second force main to the lagoon was in the plan but deferred for cost; development and sewer impact fees were held to 5% a year, below staff’s 7.5%.
  • A slow first response. After the September 2024 storm council received the report without a question, asked for disaster aid (refused in February 2025), and hired engineers for work already planned. It approved that work in July 2025, ten months later.
  • A faster second response, built on the same projects. After September 2025 the mayor promised to “leave no stone unturned.” The 2026 budget put over $20 million into wastewater, tenders came in under estimate, and council rerouted the Clear Spring Greens sewer and passed Land Drainage By-law 2274. Staff said the three core projects had been on the plan since 2022.
  • What residents asked for and did not get: an independent study of the Georgetown sewer (asked three times), an answer on compensation, help with backwater valves and sump pumps, and a pause on nearby development. The province refused aid again; council’s request to reopen both files had no reply on record. Homeowners sued in August 2026 (pending; allegations unproven).
  • United votes. Every vote on the flood response was unanimous. The only splits touching sewers were growth fees (2023) and the Bridgepark building, where Jac Siemens voted no on height, not sewers.

Council: the same seven as 2018–2022. Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert. Susan Penner, Jake Hiebert and Bill Hiebert said their own homes flooded.

What happened

2022–2023: capacity for growth

Council borrowed $2 million to upgrade Lift Station 2 on Loewen Boulevard and add a force main. No one spoke at the hearing. Members presented it as capacity for new subdivisions:

“one of the reasons that they haven't been able to develop as readily as they have is because we don't have the the sewer requirements for those subdivisions, and this upgrade will help with that.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Jan 3, 2023 · 0:12:59

When a road and sewer tender came in about $690,000 over budget, part of the force main work was pushed to 2024. The 2023 budget listed “the twinning of a force main” from Lift Station 1 to the lagoon for 2024. In October and November council raised development and sewer impact fees, which pay for growth’s share of capacity such as the lagoon, by 5% a year instead of staff’s 7.5%. Councillor Zwaagstra said “even 7.5% wouldn't cover the cost of growth” but chose affordability; Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert voted against at every reading.

2024: the first storm

The 2024 budget raised the city’s tax rate 5.1%, and some renewal projects were spread into later years. Before the storm, Councillor Siemens described the city’s pipes as being in good shape:

“And we have a lot fewer issues within our community with our infrastructure than many other communities have, and where we continue to do a lot of work.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, at the 2024 budget hearing · Apr 2, 2024 · 0:23:20

In spring, residents of Chrysler Gate and Harmony Lane wrote about standing water; staff found the swales built to plan and expected drier weather to fix it. In June council asked the Manitoba Water Services Board to share the design cost of the second force main. On September 16–17 about six inches of rain fell. The next day the City Manager reported ditches and creeks over capacity, the flood pump at the main lift station running, roads washed out and the T.G. Smith Centre flooded; staff would count homes with overland flooding or sewer backups. No council member asked a question:

“Council, any questions for administration? It's pretty straightforward. Thank you very much for that, for that info. That will definitely help as we talk to residents in the next few days.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 17, 2024 · 0:29:20

On November 5 (minutes only; no recording) staff reported that several dozen owners had reported basement flooding, and council asked the province for Disaster Financial Assistance. The same day it hired Associated Engineering to design the Lift Station 1 upgrade and the force main twinning ($463,201). In December it applied for a federal grant toward a climate risk assessment; the storm was not mentioned. No count of affected homes, cause analysis or homeowner program came to council in 2024.

2025: approval, a refusal, and the second storm

In February council held the hearing on a $6.6 million local improvement for the force main and Lift Station 1, with up to $2.75 million borrowed over five years; members called it growth capacity, and it passed third reading on July 2. Also in February the province said Steinbach did not qualify for aid for the 2024 storm:

“It seems that we met all 3 criteria that they've listed here in the letter, but we were still denied.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Feb 18, 2025 · 1:00:55

The City Manager said the city’s own damage was well below the threshold and he knew of no appeal. In April council applied for federal help with a $132.5 million wastewater project. On September 11–12, 126 mm of rain fell in four hours. Councillor Susan Penner, whose basement flooded for the second year, opened the next meeting:

“There is a drainage issue and we need to do something about it. Not in the 5-year plan, not in the 10-year plan, but now.”

Councillor Susan Penner, opening remarks · Sept 16, 2025 · 0:00:00

“We'll leave no stone unturned to see what we can do to make this work.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 16, 2025 · 0:04:06

The City Manager’s report said the rain exceeded a 1-in-100-year storm and listed possible causes on private property. In October a Georgetown delegation, M.R., reported a survey of 83 homes: 47% backed up in 2024 and 69% in 2025. She asked where the extra flow comes from, for engineering analysis, and what the city would do about compensation. The mayor sent her material to council’s study session; the compensation question was not answered. The Steinbach & Area Animal Rescue, flooded twice, asked for a creek beside it to be cleared and reassessed. Council met for two days in November, in a session that was not recorded.

2026: the flood plan, the by-law, and the hearings

In January the province refused aid for the 2025 storm too. The mayor called the criteria “very vague”, and council asked the province to explain them; in April staff reported that the province judges whether losses were insurable across the region first and does not count repeat events. The 2026 budget, adopted in March with no tax-rate increase, carried the flood plan. The City Manager explained where its core came from:

“prior to the storm events occurring in both 2024 and 2025, City Council had directed administration to place 3 significant projects on its long-term capital plan, and those were already on that plan beginning in 2022.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, at the 2026 budget hearing · Mar 17, 2026 · 0:17:48

Contracts came in under estimate: the Millwork Drive main ($1.46 million against $2.13 million), the force main ($1.49 million against $2.4 million) and Lift Station 1 ($4.33 million, raising its design capacity from 482 to 843 litres a second). New items were a Keating Drain cleanout, drain work on PTH 52 East and Park Road East, a review of the sump pump by-law with private-property inspections, and rerouting the Clear Spring Greens low-pressure sewer so it no longer empties into the Golfview Crescent line. A drainage master plan, flow sensors and a detention plan were listed only as ideas for the future. On the reroute:

“that was an initiative that was entirely council-initiated. It wasn't due to any particular study or analysis”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Mar 17, 2026 · 0:34:25

Its contract was awarded in July at $396,500. In June, after the Premier promised that rules would not stand in the way of aid for floods elsewhere in Manitoba, council unanimously asked the province to reopen Steinbach’s 2024 and 2025 files; no reply was reported. On August 4 council passed Land Drainage By-law 2274, which bans sump pumps, weeping tile and downspouts from draining into the sanitary sewer, lets staff inspect homes, and sets fines:

“gives the city additional abilities to inspect properties to make sure that drainage is flowing appropriately, that sump pumps are going in the way that they're supposed to. … it's not the city's intent to unnecessarily penalize homeowners that want to add sewer backup protection to their homes.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Aug 4, 2026 · 1:21:34

Flooding came up at almost every development hearing in 2026: McKenzie Avenue (where staff said a 2025 backup was caused by a blockage), the six-storey Bridgepark Manor near Georgetown, Williams Creek and Giesbrecht Street. Residents asked council to wait, or for third-party review of developers’ servicing studies. Council approved each one:

“We can't stop developing the city in perpetuity until we've satisfied the timeline of whatever it arbitrarily may be that residents start to— are feeling, feeling okay with it.”

Councillor Damian Penner, at the Bridgepark Manor hearing · Jul 21, 2026 · 1:01:16

On August 13, homeowners from Georgetown and nearby areas filed a lawsuit against the city alleging negligence and nuisance (news reports; pending, allegations unproven). The climate risk assessment promised for spring was presented on August 18 as a roadmap and was not adopted. No recorded meeting from September to the end of the term reported inspection results, the reroute’s completion, the province’s answer or the lawsuit.
