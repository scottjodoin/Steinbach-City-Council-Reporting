Sewer backups and flooding · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council’s handling of sewers, flooding and drainage change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

For two councils, water was a development question. Drainage was settled lot by lot in development agreements, residents’ complaints went to staff reports, and sewer pipes were renewed on a schedule and expanded for growth. Storms barely reached the agenda. The third council met two storms in two years that backed sewage into many of the same basements. Its response came slowly after the first and faster after the second, and it was built mostly from projects already planned. It added a stricter drainage by-law and pressed the province for aid, but not the homeowner help, independent study or compensation residents asked for.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

The Home Street North swale studied and left as it was; swales allowed for a commercial subdivision; drainage plans enforced for homeowners; no storm discussed.

16 items · 5 split votes, all on development drainage

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

Ponds, relined pipes and rising sewer rates; small variances refused on staff’s drainage advice; large developments approved over neighbours’ flooding worries.

24 items · 4 split votes

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

Growth-framed sewer work, two storms, two refusals of disaster aid, a flood plan, a new drainage by-law and a lawsuit.

45 items · every flood-response vote unanimous

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, who moved most sewer decisions for a decade and argued that flooding was no reason to refuse development outside Georgetown, and Jac Siemens, who said the city cannot afford pipes for every storm, did not run. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), and Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association, are new. Susan Penner, whose own basement flooded in 2024 and 2025 and who pushed hardest for urgency, topped the poll and is Deputy Mayor.

What the new council inherits:

  • Work to finish and report on. The Lift Station 1 upgrade and the Clear Spring Greens reroute were due in the 2026 season; no recorded meeting confirmed either was finished, or showed flow data on whether the reroute helped Georgetown.
  • The inspection program. Land Drainage By-law 2274 lets the city inspect homes and fine owners whose sump pumps or drains feed the sanitary sewer. Council has not said how it will report results, or whether it will help owners pay for the fixes it orders.
  • Unanswered requests. Residents’ requests for an independent study of the Georgetown sewer and Lift Station 1, and their question about compensation. Council’s June 2026 request that the province reopen the 2024 and 2025 disaster-aid files had no reply on record.
  • Plans not yet adopted or funded. The climate risk assessment presented in August 2026; a drainage master plan, flow sensors, a basement-depth policy and a drain bypass or detention plan were listed only as future possibilities. First Street’s 1960s underground services are on the five-year plan with no date.
  • The wastewater plant. A $132.5 million federal application (city share at least $65 million on the 2025 estimate) for treatment upgrades, primary lift station building upgrades and a second force main; no funding decision was made public.
  • The lawsuit. Homeowners’ claim against the city in the Court of King’s Bench is pending; its allegations are unproven.

The new council’s inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026 had no sewer or flooding items.
