Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Sewer backups and flooding

Sewer backups and flooding: what has council done?

What has Steinbach City Council done about basement sewer backups, overland flooding and drainage since December 2015, before and after the storms of September 2024 and September 2025? What reasons has it given for not doing more, how did each council member act, and is the city doing as much as other cities?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026. This report replaces our October 2026 page on the 2024–2026 backups and corrects it (see About this report).

Executive summary

For most of the past decade council treated water as a question for developers and engineers. Two storms in two years changed the pace, but less the approach.

  • Before 2024: growth, not backups. From 2015 to mid-2024 council handled drainage lot by lot through development rules, sent residents’ drainage letters (such as the Home Street North swale, 2015–2017) to staff reports, and described sewer work as renewal and capacity for growth. Before the September 2024 storm, sewer backups came up only when residents mentioned them at development hearings (2018, 2022), and council never took them up as a problem to solve; a 2019 storm got a passing remark, and spring 2022 flooding a disaster-aid request three months later.
  • After September 2024: slow, and mostly already planned. Council received the storm report without a question, asked the province for disaster aid, and hired engineers for a Lift Station 1 upgrade and a second force main to the lagoon that were already in the capital plan. It approved that work as a $6.6 million local improvement in July 2025, ten months after the storm and two months before the next one.
  • After September 2025: work moved up, a new by-law, no new kinds of help. The 2026 budget put “over $20 million” into wastewater with no tax-rate increase (and $7.25 million in new debt), raised Lift Station 1’s pumping capacity from 482 to 843 litres a second, replaced the Millwork Drive main, rerouted the Clear Spring Greens sewer away from Georgetown without a study, and adopted Land Drainage By-law 2274 with home inspections and fines. Council offered no subsidy for backwater valves or sump pumps, never commissioned the independent study residents asked for three times, gave no answer on compensation, and approved every development where residents raised flooding.
  • Members voted together. Every vote on the flood response was unanimous. Susan Penner, whose basement flooded both years, pushed hardest for urgency; Michael Zwaagstra and Damian Penner argued development cannot wait; Jac Siemens said the city cannot afford pipes for every storm. The province refused disaster aid twice; council’s request to reopen both files had no reply by November 2026.
  • How it compares. On big pipes and pumps Steinbach is doing well. On helping homeowners it is behind: Brandon paid 75% of backwater valves and sump pumps in 2026, Toronto (80%) and London (90%) pay more, and Ottawa gives a no-fault $1,000 grant after backups. Steinbach’s new inspection program finds problems in private plumbing but offers no help paying to fix them. Homeowners sued the city in August 2026; the case is pending and its allegations are unproven.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council dealt with drainage one development and one letter at a time. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council built ponds and relined pipes, but storms barely reached the agenda. His second council faced two storms that flooded basements in the same neighbourhoods two years running, and a lawsuit. The council elected in October 2026 inherits the unfinished work.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

A Home Street North swale studied for two years and left as it was; swales allowed for a commercial subdivision; runoff rules in development agreements; no storm discussed.

16 items · 5 split votes, all on development drainage

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

Ponds, relined pipes and higher sewer rates; small variances refused on staff’s drainage advice; a 2019 storm mentioned in passing; a 2022 aid request.

24 items · 4 split votes

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

Two storms, two aid refusals, a flood plan built mostly from projects planned before them, a new drainage by-law, and a lawsuit.

45 items · every flood-response vote unanimous

2026–2030

The new council

Michael Zwaagstra and Jac Siemens are gone; Ron Falk and Greg Carlos are new. Open files: inspections, the reroute’s results, disaster aid, the climate plan and the lawsuit.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words, false starts and misheard names. The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added and no two speaking turns were joined. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence. Speakers were matched to names with AI assistance, from how they were addressed in each meeting and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations and other members of the public are identified only by initials, including in quotes and context boxes. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos, and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered or covered by minutes only: the first 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021); the April 5, 2022 budget hearing (no audio); and meetings with minutes but no recording in this collection, including November 5, 2024 (when council asked for disaster aid), May 19, 2026 (first reading of the drainage by-law) and August 18, 2026 (the climate risk assessment). Council’s two-day November 2025 flood study session and the March 10, 2026 committee meeting were not recorded.
  • What counts. 85 items: storms and storm reports, sewer and drainage capital work, lift stations, sewer rates and growth fees, sump pump and drainage rules, disaster-aid requests, residents’ letters and delegations, and development hearings where flooding or sewer capacity was raised. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so who voted which way is taken from what members said.
  • Figures. Rainfall, pump rates, contract amounts and budget figures are from staff reports in the meetings and the minutes. Rainfall figures differ by station and time window: the city used 95.9 mm for September 17, 2024 alone, while media reported about 150 mm over two days; the city reported 126 mm in four hours on September 11–12, 2025. No official count of flooded basements was published for either storm. Staff reported “several dozen” owners calling the city each time; CBC reported more than 100 calls on September 17, 2024; a residents’ door-to-door survey of 83 Georgetown homes found 47% backed up in 2024 and 69% in 2025 (a delegation’s figures, not checked by the city).
  • Lawsuit. Homeowners filed a claim against the city in Manitoba’s Court of King’s Bench (August 13, 2026, according to CTV News), alleging negligence and nuisance. SteinbachOnline reported 13 homeowners; CTV reported 22 plaintiffs. The case is pending. Its allegations are unproven and have not been tested in court, and the details here come from news reports, not the court filing. The suit did not come up in any recorded council meeting, and it may limit what council says in public.
  • Not confirmed. The date of the 2019 storm the mayor mentioned; how many 2022 disaster-aid claims were approved; any reply to council’s June 2026 request to reopen the 2024 and 2025 aid files; when the city’s inflow and infiltration inspection program began and what it has found; whether the Clear Spring Greens reroute and the Lift Station 1 upgrade were finished; whether the climate risk assessment has been adopted; whether Steinbach joined Manitoba’s 2011–2015 cost-shared backwater valve and sump pump subsidy.
  • Corrections to our October 2026 page. Winnipeg’s backwater valve subsidy ended in 2016 (Winnipeg now requires the devices in new homes), so it is no longer listed as a current program. The 2026 sewer work is not “debt-free”: the 2026 plan takes on $7.25 million of new debt, including $2.75 million for the force main, and Lift Station 1 and the force main were approved as a $6.6 million local improvement (By-law 2255) in July 2025. Steinbach now has an inflow and infiltration inspection program, so that measure is rated Partial rather than Gap. Brandon’s 2026 subsidy program has since concluded. One quote on the old page joined two speaking turns and another added a sentence break; both are now shown as single, unchanged turns.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
