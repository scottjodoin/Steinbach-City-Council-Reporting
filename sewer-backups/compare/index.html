Sewer backups and flooding · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How does Steinbach’s response to basement sewer backups and flooding compare with other Manitoba cities and larger Canadian cities, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong on pipes and pumps. The 2026 sewer projects are the standard response, priced under estimate, and comparable to what peers are building.
  • Behind on helping homeowners. Brandon paid 75% of backwater valves and sump pumps in 2026; Toronto, Ottawa and London pay 80–90% or set amounts. Steinbach offers nothing, and its new inspections can order fixes without helping pay for them. Smaller Manitoba cities checked have no subsidy either.
  • Behind on finding the cause. No study of where rainwater enters the Georgetown sewer has been published; larger cities study an area and measure flows before and after a fix.
  • In line on compensation. Most cities pay only when found negligent; Steinbach pressed the province for disaster aid twice. Ottawa’s no-fault $1,000 grant is the exception.

Key findings

1Capital work is strong, but mostly planned before the floods

Steinbach is building the right kind of projects at good prices, and quickly by municipal standards since late 2025. But the City Manager said the three core projects were on the plan from 2022, and members called them growth capacity until the second storm. Bigger pumps and a second force main help the whole system; on their own they do not stop rainwater from getting into a neighbourhood’s sewer. See council’s reason 2.

2“Beyond a 1-in-100-year storm” only partly explains it

Council’s main reason is that no system is built for storms this big. That is partly right:

  • Storm pipes are commonly sized for a 1-in-5-year storm and overland routes (streets, ditches, ponds) for 1-in-100, as in Calgary’s standard. Older neighbourhoods were often built before such rules. A storm well past those levels explains flooded streets and water through windows.
  • The sanitary sewer is meant to carry no rainwater at all; Steinbach’s own 2026 by-law calls putting rainwater into it a nuisance. When sewage comes up through basement drains in heavy rain, rainwater is getting in, through sump pumps, weeping tile, leaky pipes or flooded manholes.

So a record storm explains overland flooding, but not repeated sewage backups in one neighbourhood: Georgetown residents report five since 2002. Toronto designs its sanitary system to cope with a known local storm; Steinbach has published no standard at all. See reason 1.

3Helping homeowners protect themselves: the clearest gap

Backwater valves and sump pumps are the main protection a homeowner can add. Brandon, the closest Manitoba model, paid 75% of the cost in 2021–2023 and again in 2026, retroactive to 2020. Toronto, Ottawa and London pay more. The province offers a device subsidy only when it declares a disaster-aid program, which it refused to do for Steinbach in 2024 and 2025. Winnipeg ended its subsidy in 2016 but has required valves in new homes since 1979 and sump pits since 1990. To be fair, the smaller Manitoba cities checked (Winkler, Morden, Portage la Prairie, Selkirk) also have no subsidy found. Many Georgetown homes already had valves when sewage came in, so a subsidy would not fix their problem; it would help the rest of the city. See reason 5.

4Inspections arrived late, and without help

Steinbach has banned sump pumps draining into the sewer since 2003. The 2026 by-law finally adds inspections and fines, and council was careful not to penalize older homes that add backup protection. Elsewhere, cities that push disconnection usually help pay for it: London covers 90% of a sump pit and pump with weeping-tile disconnection, and Toronto’s subsidy covers weeping-tile severance. The homeowners’ lawsuit claims the city knew of illegal sump connections by 2015 and relied on “education and self-regulation”; that claim is unproven. See reason 5.

5No study of the cause

Residents asked three times for an independent study of why Georgetown backs up. The reroute went ahead without one, and flow sensors and a drainage master plan are listed only as future ideas. Measuring flows in the affected sewers before and after a fix is how cities like London tell whether it worked, and Toronto studies an area before choosing its projects. After their own storms, Toronto and Ottawa ran staff reviews with public consultation or an engineering consultant; neither was independent, but both were public. See reason 8.

6Compensation: the usual route, now in court

Canadian cities generally pay for sewer backups only when found negligent; Toronto, London, Calgary and Edmonton say so plainly. Ottawa’s no-fault $1,000 grant is the exception. Steinbach pressing the province for disaster aid is the usual route, and council pressed harder than most: two applications, a demand for the criteria and a request to reopen. Homeowners filed suit in Manitoba’s Court of King’s Bench in August 2026, alleging negligence and nuisance. The case is pending, its allegations are unproven, and it has not come up in a recorded council meeting. See reason 6.

What “doing all it can” would add

Based on what other cities do, these are steps Steinbach has not taken, roughly from cheapest to most involved:

  • Report back in public on the inspection program’s results, the reroute and Lift Station 1, and the province’s answer on disaster aid.
  • A backwater valve and sump pump subsidy modelled on Brandon’s, retroactive to the 2024 storm, or a push for the province to offer its device subsidy without a declared disaster.
  • Help paying for the fixes inspections order, as London and Toronto do for weeping-tile disconnection.
  • Flow monitoring and smoke and dye testing of the Georgetown system, before and after the reroute, with results published.
  • An independent engineering review of why Georgetown backs up, which residents have asked for since October 2025.
  • Adopt the climate plan and publish design standards, and fund the drainage master plan now listed only as a future idea.
  • Apply for federal disaster-mitigation money, as Selkirk and Saskatoon did.
