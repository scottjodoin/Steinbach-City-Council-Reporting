Sewer backups and flooding · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons have Steinbach City Council and its staff given since 2015 for not doing more about basement sewer backups, flooding and drainage, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council and staff gave the same eight reasons across the decade. Several are fair: storms of this size do overwhelm drainage, the province does decide disaster aid, and much of the capital work is real and well priced. The weaker points are where a reason answered a different question than residents asked. A record storm explains flooded streets, but not sewage coming up through the same basements year after year. Development rules protect new subdivisions, but not the older ones already flooding. And waiting for a city-wide climate plan is not the same as finding out why one neighbourhood’s sewer backs up.

1The storms were beyond what any system is built for

“the rainfall intensity and volume exceeded a 1-in-100-year storm event, which is far beyond the reasonable planning level for underground pipes”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, report on the September 2025 storm · Sept 16, 2025 · 0:45:27

“It is a combination of reasonable cost implications measured against what is the average for the City of Steinbach, and that's based on historical weather patterns. No city in the world has designed a system that is absolutely foolproof”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on design standards · Jul 21, 2026 · 0:50:07

“there is no municipality, no city, no municipality that actually can prepare for a 1 in 125 rain event”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jul 21, 2026 · 1:09:59
Context

The September 2025 storm was extreme: 126 mm in four hours, close to the city’s July 2002 record of 132.8 mm. But the city published no frequency analysis behind the “1-in-100” label, and no design standard for its pipes. Residents disputed calling the storms outliers, and so did one councillor:

“We can say that these 2 rain events were outliers, but the fact is many people on my street have flooded multiple times over the years”

Councillor Susan Penner, opening remarks · Sept 16, 2025 · 0:00:00

A Georgetown delegation, M.R., said some homes there had backed up five times since 2002, and that 47% of 83 homes surveyed backed up in 2024 and 69% in 2025. A storm overwhelming ditches and streets explains overland flooding. Repeated sewage backups point to rainwater getting into the sanitary sewer, which is meant to carry none; the city’s own 2026 by-law calls that a nuisance. See Finding 2.

2We’re already doing it, and fast

“The primary focus of The flood mitigation strategy, at least for 2026, includes mostly capital projects that had already been initiated well prior to this year.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Apr 7, 2026 · 1:03:28

“in government terms, this is lightning speed”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the 2026 flood plan · Mar 17, 2026 · 0:44:52

“This is something that will help every property in our city. It'll help, it'll facilitate more growth.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on the force main contract · Feb 17, 2026 · 0:29:08

“We are doing things and, um, we are moving forward and we are working on, on as much as we can.”

Mayor Earl Funk, answering residents’ request for a study · Jul 21, 2026 · 0:40:38
Context

The work is real and well priced: Lift Station 1’s design capacity rises from 482 to 843 litres a second, and the 2026 contracts came in under the engineers’ estimates. But the second force main was in the 2023 budget for 2024, was deferred for cost, had its design hired only after the 2024 storm, was approved in July 2025 and went to contract in February 2026, about 17 months after the first storm. Members described it as growth capacity before the 2025 storm and as flood mitigation after it. Bigger downstream capacity helps the whole system, but on its own it does not stop rainwater getting into a neighbourhood’s sewer.

3The engineers and the rules take care of it

“The requirement for development is the drainage from a building site cannot be any greater or more rapid than what was the case prior to development.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · May 21, 2019 · 0:25:06

“our staff do not sign off on development agreements if it's going to have— if it's going to cause direct flooding in a neighborhood, if it's going to impact these things.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · May 1, 2018 · 0:48:53

“I also hear the drainage concerns in that area. I have a lot of faith in our engineering department”

Councillor Jac Siemens, at the Williams Creek hearing · Aug 4, 2026 · 0:53:22
Context

The runoff rule is sound, and council did follow staff when they warned about drainage: it refused small variances and a rezoning in 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2023. But the rule only governs new building. Older areas were built before it, and neighbours of new projects repeatedly said their existing problems were never fixed (Williams Creek neighbours S.B. and G.W. in 2026 said water sits in their yards and garages; their claim that a nearby development caused it is unproven). In 2020 Councillor Siemens himself said development agreements can push water onto neighbours while the city “does wash their hands of it somewhat because it's all private property.” The developer’s servicing studies are written by consultants who, the City Manager said, are “under contract with the developer or landowner” and are reviewed by city staff; council did not adopt residents’ request for third-party review.
