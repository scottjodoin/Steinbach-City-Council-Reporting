Crime and policing · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for not doing more about crime and policing, such as adding officers, funding partners or setting targets, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given the same handful of reasons for a decade. Several are true as far as they go: RCMP pay is bargained in Ottawa, the RCMP was short of officers, and bail is not a city power. The weaker points are what the reasons leave out: council does choose how many officers to pay for, the province pays much of the bill, the shortage ended in 2026, and “it’s working” was never measured against a target.

1Ottawa sets the cost, not council

“In terms of the RCMP aspect, we recognize that that is out of our control in terms of the amount that we have to pay. We have the same number of officers and we're having to pay more.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the 90% cost share in the 2017 budget · Feb 21, 2017 · 0:16:03

“in the last round of collective bargaining, the federal government negotiated a 23.7% retroactive pay increase, which obviously has a significant impact on municipal budgets. … we should be getting a lot more say than what we're getting now.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jan 7, 2025 · 0:20:23

“We're happy to pay. We want to get the service. But we also want to have say in what that service is going to look like and what that bill is going to look like.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jan 7, 2025 · 0:23:30
Context

Much of this is accurate. The jump from a 70% to a 90% share when the population passed 15,000 is set by the federal-provincial policing framework, and the RCMP’s first union contract (2021) gave members about 23.8% over six years, retroactive to 2017, which municipalities had to pay (Global News). But the number of officers is council’s choice, as its own city manager said when it asked for a 17th: “the City of Steinbach does control the total RCMP officer complement” (Dec 1, 2020, 0:09:50). The province’s Urban Policing Grant ($2.61 million in 2025–26) covers well over half of an RCMP bill of about $4.0 million. Council took the 2023 AMM resolution on fair policing costs as information without supporting it, and wrote to Ottawa only in 2025. A 2011 task force chaired by Zwaagstra found Winkler’s own police force cost about the same per officer as the RCMP (SteinbachOnline); the model has not been reviewed in public since.

2The RCMP can’t fill the positions already paid for

“The solution is not simply adding, you know, RCMP officers because we're not getting the number that we're paying for now, that we're requesting now. So just saying we're going to go from 18 to 19 officers, we wouldn't.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jun 17, 2025 · 0:07:37

“In our meeting with Manitoba Justice, they made it pretty clear that RCMP shortages are a habitual challenge across the nation, not just in Manitoba and Steinbach specifically.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Jun 17, 2025 · 0:12:42

“We're often 2, 3, or 4 officers short of the amount that we have requested, and of course that affects the ability to do proper enforcement.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · May 6, 2025 · 1:13:46
Context

The shortage was real, long-running and shared. Council’s drug unit sat empty for three or four months in 2017 for the same reason. On May 15, 2025, Statistics Canada counted Selkirk’s RCMP at 14 officers against 18 authorized and Brandon’s own force at 90 against 111 (Statistics Canada). Council did act on it, with Ste. Anne police patrols paid by MPI and a private overnight patrol paid from the unspent money. But the argument against adding positions was strongest in 2022–2025: by September 2026 all 18 positions were filled, while the city had grown to about 21,000 people. See Finding 1.

3Whoever asks for extra policing should pay

“because they're making the request, this is their initiative, they should be responsible for 100% of the cost. We've already added an extra officer. We're not at this point planning to add another and impact our budget in that way.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the school division’s request for a school officer · Jul 5, 2022 · 0:26:09

“I also know that many of the students at SRSS do not come from the City of Steinbach, and I agree that the cost should be borne by the school division.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Jul 5, 2022 · 0:29:46

“My views all the time, or my goal for us, for a dedicated traffic person, was that MPI would eventually pay for that person. So hopefully we can get the accidents down and get MPI to sponsor that second person.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, after the RCMP suggested a second traffic officer · Apr 2, 2024 · 0:49:33
Context

The school division had proposed a split (the officer’s salary × 70% × 50%) that, by Zwaagstra’s reading, would have left the city paying two-thirds; council’s position that the division pay for an officer working mostly in a regional high school is defensible. But no later council item on the request was found, and in 2026 the RCMP said a special constable “spends a lot of time” in schools, with no public record of who pays. The second traffic officer the RCMP suggested in 2024 was never added; MPI-funded patrols by the Ste. Anne police came in 2025 instead. Swift Current, a similar-sized RCMP city, funds a school liaison officer itself (local news, not confirmed in its budget).

4The causes lie in addiction and the justice system

“And when you talk about the revolving door, the rotating door, I fully agree with you. … So, but that is the legislation that you have to work under.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, a former police officer, replying to the RCMP commander · Apr 2, 2024 · 0:50:02

“These past 10 years of catch and release have definitely made it more difficult for the police, made more work for prosecutors and judges, and foremost, causing the general public to be less safe and more at risk for property damage and serious violent crime.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, opening remarks · May 6, 2025 · 0:00:13

“we have this too— it's a catch-and-release program, right? We've got offenders get picked up, they get, they get incarcerated, they get, they get charged, and by the next day they're out back on the back on doing the same thing. Nothing's been fixed.”

Mayor Earl Funk, reporting on the cities’ bail-reform push · Apr 15, 2025 · 0:04:59
Context

Bail and sentencing are federal and provincial, and the RCMP has tied local break-ins to methamphetamine since 2016. But prevention is partly local. Headway, a program based at the detachment, told council in 2024 that about 90% of its young clients stopped being involved with the justice system (its own figure); the city’s grant to it was $8,000 in the 2018 grants list. In November 2024 council took no action on an outreach group’s request that the city apply for federal money for a mobile addiction outreach unit (only municipalities could apply); with no recording of that meeting, its reasons are not on record. Mental Health Act calls rose from 251 to 311 between the same months of 2025 and 2026. The city’s safety plan lists prevention actions (school programs, restorative justice, victim services, help for hospital transports), most with timelines “to be determined.” No Steinbach letter on bail reform appears in the minutes; the advocacy ran through the AMM’s cities caucus and the regional committee.

5Day-to-day crime is the RCMP’s job: call the police

“And bring it before our staff sergeant. And if you call in when it's happening, then they will be— they will send a car out.”

Mayor Earl Funk, to a resident reporting thefts · Mar 1, 2022 · 0:50:59

“If there are concerns about traffic disobeying the posted speed limit, this should be considered an enforcement issue and dealt with by the RCMP.”

City Manager Troy Warkentin, on Harmony Lane speeding · Feb 18, 2025 · 0:48:36

“Please tell them to call the police.”

Staff Sgt. M.M., RCMP, after the mayor said residents send him pictures of incidents · Sept 15, 2026 · 0:33:49
Context

Police operations do belong to the RCMP. But council sets the priorities and the number of officers, and it sends residents’ complaints (Whitby Crescent 2022, Harmony Lane 2025, First Street 2026) to its advisory group, which meets in private; outcomes were reported only in passing. Some residents say calling has not worked. Harmony Lane residents wrote in 2025 that they had called the RCMP “on several occasions and nothing helps because nobody shows up”; the city’s 2026 safety survey found “a strong perception that there is a lack of RCMP presence” and frustration with 911 calls going unanswered; and at an April 2026 hearing a resident, H.K., said police had dismissed his call about strangers in yards at night which drew no response from council. These are residents’ accounts and have not been verified. In the eleven years of this record, no council member asked about clearance rates or response times; Steinbach’s weighted clearance rate is the lowest of its Manitoba peers (Finding 3).

6What we have is working

“As you can see with the incident report, because of these priorities, many of these incidents are going down. So these priorities are working for us”

Councillor Earl Funk · May 15, 2018 · 0:13:15

“I think these are obtainable and they're measurable, and I think this is something to look forward to and it will make our community safer.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on the 2023–24 priorities · May 2, 2023 · 0:18:51

“although I'm not going to say that Steinbach doesn't have serious crime— every community does have instances of it— a lot of the crime that we are seeing is, is small-scale and petty.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Jun 17, 2025 · 0:12:42
Context

Some years did improve: thefts fell by more than half from 2014 to 2015, and break-ins fell in the RCMP’s 2018 and 2019 reports and again during COVID-19. But no priority ever had a number attached, so “measurable” was never measured in public. Statistics Canada’s crime severity index for Steinbach rose from 79.9 in 2015 to 108.7 in 2023 before falling to 82.9 in 2025, and the share of crime solved fell to 30.2%, the lowest in the decade. Most of Steinbach’s crime is indeed property crime (about 1,025 of 1,587 incidents in 2025), which matters to its victims; Zwaagstra himself said in 2017 that a break-in “matters a whole lot to the person whose home got broken into.”

7Only if it doesn’t add to the tax bill

“And so there's no city tax dollars that are being allocated here. The request is simply that we provide a limited amount of staff time so that way we can facilitate this.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on joining the Community Safety and Well-Being plan · Feb 6, 2024 · 0:12:01

“it's the money that we're spending on this is money we budgeted towards RCMP. So in terms of our budget, it's not like there's additional money coming out to pay for it.”

Councillor Susan Penner, on extending private security · Nov 4, 2025 · 0:09:19

“An additional RCMP officer would be about $200,000 a year. It's not an insignificant cost. It would impact our budget. But again, I think this is a problem, a significant problem, and we have to invest in order to address these types of problems.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, the one time council raised taxes to add an officer · Sept 7, 2021 · 1:00:47
Context

Council did pay when it chose to: the 2022 tax increase of 0.4 mill was mainly for the traffic officer. Otherwise new measures came at no new cost: the safety plan (provincially funded), the private patrol (unspent RCMP money), Ste. Anne patrols (MPI), the $200,000 grant (provincial). Steinbach’s policing is cheap by comparison: about $191 per resident before the provincial grant and about $66 after it, against about $420 in Brandon (about $217 from city taxes) and about $340 in Portage la Prairie (about $200 after its grant). With the detachment full from September 2026, there may be no unspent RCMP money left for the patrol, whose 2027 cost (about $150,000) will have to come from the budget itself. In 2023, $550,000 was drawn from reserves for “Policing” with no public explanation.
