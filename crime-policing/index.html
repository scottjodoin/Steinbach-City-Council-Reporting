Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Crime and policing

Crime and policing: what has council done?

What has Steinbach City Council done about crime and neighbourhood safety since December 2015, through its RCMP contract (how many officers it pays for, and what they cost), its yearly policing priorities, community safety officers, private security and partner groups? What reasons has it given for not doing more, how did each council member act, and how do Steinbach’s police staffing, costs and crime levels compare with similar cities?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026. This report replaces our October 2026 page on 2022–2026 and extends it back to 2015, with peer figures updated to 2025.

Executive summary

For eleven years council has run policing the same way: pay for a set number of RCMP officers, confirm the same four priorities, and add help when one problem gets loud enough. The city kept its policing cost low; it also has the thinnest police coverage of its Manitoba peers.

  • Few officers for a growing city. Council paid for 16 RCMP officers from 2015 to 2020, asked for a 17th in December 2020 (the first increase since 2015) and an 18th, dedicated to traffic, from 2022 after residents’ letters about street racing. With 18 officers for about 20,900 people, Steinbach had 86 authorized officers per 100,000 residents in 2025, against 139 to 191 in its Manitoba peers and 196 for Manitoba as a whole.
  • Costs rose for reasons council couldn’t control, and one it chose. When the population passed 15,000, the city’s share of RCMP costs rose from 70% to 90% (April 2017), and the whole 0.7-mill tax increase of 2017 went to policing with no new officers. The 2022 increase of 0.4 mill paid mainly for the traffic officer. The RCMP now bills about $4.0 million a year, and a provincial grant of about $2.6 million covers much of it.
  • The same four priorities, with no targets. Police visibility, drugs and organized crime, property crime and traffic were confirmed by council resolution every year from 2016 to 2025, without a single numeric target. In 2026 the RCMP set its priorities with council’s closed advisory board, and no council resolution was found. Crime severity (82.9 in 2025) is near Canada’s 75 and well below Manitoba’s 130, but only 30.2% of crime, weighted by seriousness, was solved, the lowest of the Manitoba peers; council never discussed that rate.
  • When the RCMP couldn’t fill positions, council hired private security. Council’s drug unit sat empty for months in 2017, and from 2022 to 2025 the detachment ran 15 to 25% short of the 18 officers paid for. Rather than add positions, council paid for an overnight private patrol from the unspent RCMP money from July 2025, and in October 2026 contracted it to the end of 2027 (about $150,000 a year). Feedback has been positive but anecdotal. The detachment reached full strength in September 2026.
  • Partners were heard more than funded. Crime Stoppers’ requests (almost every year since 2016), the Headway youth program’s 2016 request and Citizens on Patrol’s shrinking ranks were received as information; small grants continued, such as $8,000 for Headway in 2018. A school officer was offered only if the school division paid 100% (2022). Council joined the Community Safety and Well-Being plan on “no city tax dollars” terms; its policing actions have no dates. How a $200,000 provincial safety grant (2026) was spent has not been made public. Every policing vote was unanimous.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council kept 16 officers and absorbed the jump to a 90% cost share. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council added two officers, the second for street racing. His second council kept 18 positions it could not get filled, turned to private security, and joined a provincial safety plan. The council elected in October 2026 inherits a full detachment for the first time in years.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named; RCMP officers, delegations and residents are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts. The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every quote from a council member or staff is from a speaker identified with high confidence: from hand-checked speaker maps for meetings from January 2022, and for earlier meetings from how speakers were addressed and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. RCMP officers who presented to council, delegations and other members of the public are identified only by initials (first and last), including in quote citations. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain; on two dates in 2023–2026 the recordings mix the two Councillors Hiebert, and those turns are not attributed.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online), plus agenda packages where available. Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist; and the April 5, 2022 budget hearing, which has no audio. The RCMP Advisory Group (later called the Police Advisory Board), budget study sessions and committee meetings are closed and unrecorded, so what was said there is not covered.
  • What counts. 85 items: the RCMP contract (officer numbers, cost share, bargaining, grants), yearly priorities and RCMP reports, community safety officers and by-law enforcement, private security, cameras, Citizens on Patrol, Crime Stoppers and other partners, the Community Safety and Well-Being plan, residents’ safety complaints, and advocacy on bail, addiction and policing costs. Traffic engineering is in the traffic report. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so movers, seconders and who voted which way are taken from the recordings.
  • Figures. Officer counts, crime severity and clearance rates are from Statistics Canada (tables revised July–August 2026). The city’s budget shows police and fire together, so Steinbach’s RCMP cost is estimated from three quarterly invoices of $1,000,258 (July 2024 to March 2025; the fourth assumed equal). The RCMP’s year-end reconciliation can change the final bill. Statistics Canada counts officers on the books on May 15; councillors’ counts of officers actually working were lower.
  • Not confirmed. How the $200,000 provincial safety grant was allocated after the closed August 11, 2026 meeting; what the unbudgeted $550,000 drawn from reserves for “Policing” in 2023 paid for; whether the province answered the 2021 request for red light camera authority; whether the school division took up the 100% offer, and who pays for the special constable now working in schools; whether Steinbach’s two community safety officers hold provincial peace-officer designation; why Statistics Canada lists 19 authorized officers in 2022 when the city says 18; and the minutes of the October 20, 2026 meeting (the security contract is reported from the recording). No litigation about policing was found.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
