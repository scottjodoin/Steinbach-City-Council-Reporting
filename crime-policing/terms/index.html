Crime and policing · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council’s approach to crime and policing change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

The approach barely changed across three councils: an RCMP contract with officers added one at a time for a specific problem, the same four priorities every year, and residents’ complaints routed to a closed advisory group. What changed was the pressure. The cost share jumped in 2017; street racing brought an 18th officer in 2022; and when the RCMP could not fill the positions from 2022 to 2025, council turned to private security. Meanwhile the city grew by about a third, so police coverage per resident fell.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

Sixteen officers, a drug unit left empty for months, and the 90% cost share that took the whole 2017 tax increase.

21 items · 2 split budget votes, neither about policing

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

A 17th officer for growth, an 18th for street racing, red light cameras asked for, and a school officer only if the division paid.

24 items · every vote unanimous

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

Positions paid for but not filled, private overnight security, a letter to Ottawa, a safety plan and a $200,000 grant decided out of public view.

40 items · every vote unanimous

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, who chaired the RCMP advisory group from 2018 and moved most policing motions for a decade, and Jac Siemens did not run. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), and Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association, are new. Susan Penner, who topped the poll and said during the campaign that residents had made property theft and community safety a priority, is Deputy Mayor. Jake Hiebert, a former police officer, returns.

What the new council inherits:

  • A full detachment, for now. All 18 positions were filled from September 2026, for the first time in years. Council’s main reason for not adding positions, that the RCMP could not fill them, no longer applies; the city has grown to about 21,000.
  • The overnight patrol contract with Next Level Servicing Corp., from November 1, 2026 to at least the end of 2027 ($148,409 for 2027). Its results have been reported only anecdotally, and with the detachment full there may be no unspent RCMP money to pay for it.
  • The 2026–27 policing priorities, set by the RCMP with the advisory board and never adopted by council resolution, and an advisory group with no chair named in the public record after Zwaagstra’s departure.
  • The $200,000 provincial safety grant, to be spent by March 31, 2027. Staff proposed a second community safety officer truck and cameras at city buildings and parks; the decision went to a closed meeting in August 2026 and has not been made public.
  • The Community Safety and Well-Being Plan, whose policing actions (advocate for more RCMP and more community safety officers) have timelines “to be determined.”
  • Rising mental-health calls: 311 Mental Health Act calls by September 2026, up from 251 at the same point in 2025.

The new council’s inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026 made no policing decisions.
