Crime and policing · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s police staffing, policing costs, crime levels and crimes solved compare with other Manitoba cities and similar-sized RCMP cities in the West, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Cheap, and thin. Steinbach spends less per resident on policing than the peers whose costs we could confirm, and it has fewer officers per resident than any Manitoba peer.
  • Middling crime, low clearance. Its crime severity is near Canada’s and far below Manitoba’s, but well above Winkler’s and Morden’s, and it solves a smaller share of crime than every Manitoba peer.
  • Creative, but unmeasured. The private overnight patrol is unusual and quick; like the yearly priorities, it has no published targets or results.

Key findings

1Steinbach has the thinnest police coverage of its Manitoba peers

Council added two officers between 2015 and 2022 while the population it polices grew from about 15,800 to about 20,900, so authorized officers per 100,000 residents fell from 102 to 86 (Statistics Canada). That is well below Winkler (143) and Morden (139), which run their own forces, and RCMP-policed Selkirk (164) and Portage la Prairie (186), and less than half the Manitoba rate (196). Among similar-sized western RCMP cities it is low but not an outlier: Sylvan Lake, Alberta, has 92 and Swift Current, Saskatchewan, 119. Council itself said in 2020 that Steinbach had “far fewer officers on a per capita basis” than comparable communities. Its reason for not adding more since 2022, that the RCMP could not fill the positions (reason 2), no longer applies now that the detachment is full; the cost of one officer was put at $160,000–$180,000 (2020) and about $200,000 (2021) (reason 7).

2Crime is near the national level, but well above its closest neighbours’

Steinbach’s crime severity index, which weights crimes by seriousness, was 79.9 in 2015, rose to 108.7 in 2023 and fell to 82.9 in 2025 (Statistics Canada). That is about 10% above Canada (75.0) and far below Manitoba (130.0), as the RCMP commander told council in 2026. But Winkler (45.0) and Morden (56.2), the most similar Manitoba cities, have much less serious crime, with more police per resident. Brandon (99.7), Selkirk (155.3) and Portage la Prairie (211.0) have more. Most of Steinbach’s reported crime is property crime: about 1,025 of 1,587 incidents in 2025. See reason 6.

3Fewer crimes are solved, and council hasn’t asked why

Steinbach’s weighted clearance rate, the share of crime solved weighted by seriousness, was 30.2% in 2025: the lowest of the decade (it ranged from 31.7% to 43.9% between 2015 and 2024) and below Canada (36.6%), Manitoba (40.4%) and every Manitoba peer. Of the peers, only Sylvan Lake (27.5%) was lower. Many things affect clearance, including the mix of crimes and staffing, and in 2026 the commander said officers were too busy to set aside time for proactive patrol. In the eleven years of this record no council member asked about clearance or response times, and the priorities council approves have no targets to measure against. See reason 5 and reason 6.

4The private patrol was a creative stopgap; the evidence is still thin

Using the unspent RCMP money for an overnight patrol was quick and, at first, cost taxpayers nothing extra. Developers, city staff and the RCMP describe it positively, and we found no similar contracted patrol in the Manitoba peers. But no incident counts were ever reported in public, though a report with RCMP statistics was promised in July 2025. With the detachment full from September 2026, the patrol’s cost (about $150,000 a year to 2027) may have to come from the budget itself. See reason 2 and reason 7.

5The province pays more of the bill than council’s reasons suggest

Council’s main cost argument is that Ottawa sets RCMP pay (reason 1). But the province’s Urban Policing Grant rose from $1.78 million (2019) to $2.56 million (2024–25), $2.61 million (2025–26) and $2.67 million (2026–27), covering well over half of an RCMP bill of about $4.0 million. Net of the grant, policing costs Steinbach about $66 per resident, less than Brandon or Portage la Prairie after their provincial money. The grant letters were received as information each year, and council never discussed using the increases for more officers.

6Partners and plans got words more than money

Crime Stoppers asked almost every year from 2016 and was never funded; Citizens on Patrol fell from about 12 to 14 volunteers to 8 with its funding “in limbo”; an addiction outreach partnership was declined. The safety plan, built on a survey of more than 800 residents, was received as information, and its policing actions have no owners or dates. Portage la Prairie, which finished its plan in the same provincial program, sorted its actions into underway, short-term and long-term and has grown its community safety officers to 6.5. See reason 4 and reason 8.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Plan police strength for growth: set a target ratio of officers to residents and add positions now that the RCMP can fill them, starting with the second traffic officer the RCMP suggested in 2024.
  • Put numbers in the priorities: adopt the 2026–27 priorities in open council, with measurable targets for break-ins, clearance and response times, and review them each year.
  • Publish a yearly public-safety report: crime severity, clearance, officers paid for and working, safety officer activity, the private patrol’s results and the full policing cost, as Brandon does.
  • Open up the Police Advisory Board: publish its membership, agendas and minutes, recruit resident members publicly, and report back on the complaints council sends it.
  • Measure the private patrol before renewing it after 2027: incidents, calls and costs, by area, and how it will be paid for now that the RCMP is at full strength.
  • Grow the community safety officer program under the province’s framework, with peace-officer powers, as Portage la Prairie has done.
  • Give the safety plan owners and dates, and fund its small partners: Crime Stoppers, Citizens on Patrol recruitment and youth programs such as Headway.
  • Make the $200,000 grant decision public, and record future grant decisions in open council.
  • Press the province on mental-health calls (311 by September 2026, against 251 at the same point in 2025), for example institutional safety officers for hospital transports, as the safety plan suggests.
  • Revisit the policing model on cost and service at the next contract review, as the 2011 task force did, comparing the RCMP with a municipal force like Winkler’s.
