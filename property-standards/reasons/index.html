Property standards · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons have Steinbach City Council and its staff given since 2015 for not doing more to keep yards, garbage and rental buildings in decent condition, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has rarely explained why it does not do more on property standards, because it has rarely been asked: no member, staff report or delegation proposed a new yard rule, a derelict-building by-law or rental licensing. The reasons below come from the debates that did happen, mostly on secondary suites and at rezoning hearings. Some rest on real trade-offs, such as the risk of losing low-rent units. Others describe a choice: to rely on voluntary compliance and redevelopment rather than tickets, reports and standards for buildings.

  • 1. Tougher enforcement could cost low-rent housing
  • 2. Voluntary compliance comes first
  • 3. Redevelopment will fix neglected properties
  • 4. Upkeep is the owner’s business, not a land-use question
  • 5. It takes time, and some problems belong to others
  • 6. More enforcement costs money, so go step by step
  • 7. Rental licensing: no reason given, because it never came up

1Tougher enforcement could cost low-rent housing

“The downside— there is a downside to this, and that if there is substandard housing today in Steinbach, those same renters would be likely required to pay more to the landlord simply because upgrades to those rental units could be substantial in some cases.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, moving first reading of By-law 2157 · Sept 1, 2020 · 0:21:02

“I think one of the reasons that council has held off to put compliance and enforcement in play is potentially there will be a loss of many low-end rental units in Steinbach…”

Councillor Jac Siemens, who supported enforcing By-law 2157 · Aug 20, 2024 · 0:21:43

“However, during the review, the expectation for all rental suites in Steinbach to comply with the National Building Code would result in some significant degree of decommissioning of existing rental suites.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on why the by-law sets only basic standards · Aug 20, 2024 · 0:10:53
Context

The worry is real: Steinbach’s rental vacancy rate was “below 4%” in 2020, Councillor Siemens said, and closing suites would take homes off the market. Council already built that into the rule, choosing basic alarms and exits instead of the full building code. The mayor answered the 2024 version of the concern the same night: “But what does it cost to put in an egress? What does it cost to put in a fire alarm, a fire extinguisher? These are small, small costs that should not affect the cost of the rental that much” (0:24:32). The 2021 estimate was “plus or minus $3,000” a suite. The concern explains a slow start; it does not explain why no count of suites fixed, closed or lost has been reported, which is the only way to know whether low-rent units were lost. See Finding 2.

2Voluntary compliance comes first

“generally what we are hoping for, at least the majority of compliance will be voluntary.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, as By-law 2157 was adopted · Jan 5, 2021 · 0:27:56

“Well, like I said, the method of voluntary compliance is always much preferred.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Aug 20, 2024 · 0:16:41

“the history of the city has been, and I hope will continue to be, to first educate and then enforce and then penalize. … This isn't to have our inspectors going out looking for individuals to penalize, but rather to have the opportunity to encourage them stronger to get their work done.”

Councillor Damian Penner, moving By-law 2282 · Aug 4, 2026 · 1:25:40
Context

Peers say the same: Winkler’s 2025 enforcement policy makes education the first step. The difference is the next step. For yards and junk, Steinbach’s only tools after a notice are a city cleanup billed to taxes, after 21 days, or a court case; the “penalize” step does not exist because By-law 1663 is not on the ticket schedule. Brandon can issue an untidy-premises ticket ($100, or $200 after 15 days). For suites, three and a half years of voluntary compliance produced “several dozen” confirmations out of an estimated several hundred, and in 2024 Councillor Jake Hiebert said the fire chief had issued warnings years earlier with “still no compliance.” See Finding 1.

3Redevelopment will fix neglected properties

“And I think this is something that we're going to be getting rid of a property that is older, it's getting run down. Now there'll be a new property there that will attract new people”

Mayor Earl Funk, approving a nine-unit building on Maplewood Street · May 21, 2019 · 0:35:32

“That means those houses, derelict homes, are going to come out.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on three demolition permits · Dec 17, 2024 · 0:49:09

“I would imagine with the new garbage pickup, the dumpster, there wouldn't be garbage bags lying around. It would be in the dumpster. So that would be much cleaner that way.”

Mayor Earl Funk, answering C.M.’s concern about a Henry Street rental · May 6, 2025 · 0:15:57
Context

Redevelopment can end a problem at one address, and in several cases it did. But residents’ point was different: the city already has a rule against long grass and garbage, and the properties stayed that way for years until a rezoning came along. In the Maplewood (2018), Third Street (2023) and Henry Street (2025) cases, neighbours said run-down houses were rented out while owners waited to redevelop; the Henry Street applicant agreed the house had “fallen into a state of neglect.” Neighbours then use the rezoning hearing as the only place to raise upkeep. Where a rezoning is refused, as on Maplewood Street in 2018, nothing changes for the houses. Residents’ descriptions are their own and unproven. See Finding 3.

4Upkeep is the owner’s business, not a land-use question

“It wouldn't— it would be difficult to limit the number of vehicles because that would mean the city would need to enforce, and that is not something the city does on private property.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, asked about limiting vehicles per rental unit · Nov 18, 2025 · 0:32:50

“And the biggest one for me is that the lack of trust for the developer, and I feel that I don't know what the developer can do to gain trust of the neighborhood again, but that needs to happen before we can go forward”

Councillor Earl Funk, voting to deny the Maplewood Street rezoning · Aug 21, 2018 · 1:02:04
Context

Council is right that a rezoning is decided on land use, and it mostly kept upkeep out of those decisions. The 2018 Maplewood case was the exception: trust in an owner’s upkeep was a stated reason for denial, but no by-law tool was then used on the houses. Upkeep is not only the owner’s private business, though. By-law 1663 makes owners of any “residence, apartment building” or other premises responsible for junk, rubbish and long grass, and lets a city designate enter private property to check. A resident at the 2025 Henry Street hearing put it this way: “if the tenant doesn't do it, the onus is still on the owner to look after his property” (C.M., May 6, 2025, 0:17:18). Owners who live elsewhere get the notice by mail at their tax-roll address. Inside a rental, repairs are mainly provincial: landlords have an obligation to repair under the Residential Tenancies Act.

5It takes time, and some problems belong to others

“It'll take some time for our staff to go through this, and this won't happen overnight, and we won't have every property complying in a short time, but this will be something, this will be a long project…”

Mayor Earl Funk, on By-law 2157 · Sept 1, 2020 · 0:25:00

“the littering, the theft, um, that is something that will have to be taken up with the RCMP.”

Mayor Earl Funk, replying to N.P. about overflowing apartment dumpsters · Mar 1, 2022 · 0:46:47
Context

Theft is a police matter. Overflowing bins and rubbish on private land are not: By-law 1663 covers rubbish on an “apartment building” site and the lanes beside it, and the city’s own officers enforce it. The city’s property standards web page links weed control, graffiti and drainage pages and a general contact form, but names no by-law and has no page on junk or garbage on private property. A long timeline for suites is reasonable; the mayor expected “maybe a decade or two.” But a long project needs progress reports, and none has come to council since the 2024 update.

6More enforcement costs money, so go step by step

“So do these— would these CSOs, so to speak, would they— what kind of cost would they have to us?”

Councillor Earl Funk, on the province’s community safety officer program · Mar 7, 2017 · 0:30:08

“I think it is necessary, Mr. Mayor, that this is the first step of the process to approve this is first reading, and the next step is to listen to the public, what they have to say, and that could be from a variety of sources, and that will determine how we move forward.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on By-law 2157 · Sept 1, 2020 · 0:24:40
Context

Caution about cost is fair for a city of Steinbach’s size, and the step-by-step approach did produce By-law 2157. But the steps have been slow: the province’s 2017 letter on community safety officers got no report back, and the city ran with one community services officer for all by-laws from at least 2016 until a second was added by 2026. Staff estimated By-law 2157 alone would take about 500 staff hours in its first year. In July 2026 staff proposed $65,000 for a second officer’s truck from a $200,000 provincial grant; the decision was deferred to committee. Tickets also save money: Mayor Chris Goertzen backed the 2017 penalty by-law because it would cut the cost of court cases, yet yard and junk rules still go to court or to a city cleanup.

7Rental licensing: no reason given, because it never came up

No council member, staff report or delegation proposed rental licensing, a landlord registry or a suite registry at any recorded meeting from December 2015 to November 2026, and no member spoke against them either, so there is no stated reason to report. The concern about cost to landlords and rents (reason 1) would likely apply.

Context

Residents at hearings asked whether landlords would be “absentee landlords” and whether renters would look after new buildings, but did not ask for licensing. Staff said in 2020 that the city did not know where all secondary suites were, and in 2021 SteinbachOnline reported staff’s estimate of 300 to 600 suites; a register of suites that have passed inspection would address that. We found no Manitoba peer that licenses long-term rentals. Waterloo, London, Oshawa and Toronto do, with fees paid by landlords. See Finding 5.
