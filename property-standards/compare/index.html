Property standards · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s rules and enforcement for yards, buildings, garbage and rental housing compare with other Manitoba and Canadian cities, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • On par for yards, ahead on suite safety. Steinbach’s junk-and-weeds rule works much like Morden’s and Winkler’s, and its suite-safety by-law, with a $1,000 daily fine for every dwelling, is stronger on paper than most peers’.
  • Behind on buildings, tickets and derelict homes. Brandon and Portage la Prairie set standards for roofs, walls and windows, have derelict-building by-laws, and can fine owners; Steinbach can only clean up and bill, or go to court.
  • Little is measured anywhere, but some peers do better. Winkler has a written enforcement policy (2025) and Winnipeg publishes every by-law investigation. Steinbach has neither. No Manitoba peer licenses long-term rentals; four Ontario cities do.

Key findings

1The core yard by-law is 23 years old and has no ticket

By-law 1663 reaches almost every yard problem residents described: long grass, weeds, garbage, junk. But its only remedies are a city cleanup billed to taxes, which takes at least 21 days and staff time, or a court case. Steinbach already uses penalty tickets for derelict vehicles ($100), noise, animals, burning and suites, and amended the ticket schedule in 2018, 2021, 2022, 2025 and 2026, each time for by-laws it had just revised. Adding yard and junk contraventions would give officers a step between a warning and a cleanup, which is what “educate, then enforce, then penalize” needs (reason 2, reason 6). Brandon, by comparison, tickets untidy premises at $100, rising to $200 after 15 days.

2The right rental-safety rule, with unknown results

By-law 2157 is stronger than most Manitoba peers’ rules on paper: it covers every unit, carries a $1,000 daily fine and lets renters request an inspection. Portage la Prairie is the closest peer, with alarms required in every rental and fines up to $500 a day. Steinbach’s 2024 crackdown was a good step, and members backed it. What’s missing is a public count of suites found, inspected, fixed, closed and fined. A count like that would also show whether the low-rent units Councillor Siemens worried about were actually lost (reason 1, reason 5).

3Upkeep complaints end up at rezoning hearings

From 2018 to 2026 neighbours raised the condition of rentals, long grass or garbage at more than ten hearings. Rezoning can’t fix upkeep, and council has rightly decided land use on other grounds. But with no visible enforcement record and no ticket for yards, neighbours fall back on the hearing, and the answer they hear is that redevelopment will replace the problem (reason 3, reason 4). Published response times and enforcement figures would give them somewhere else to go.

4Peers regulate buildings and derelict properties; Steinbach doesn’t

Brandon (2023) and Portage la Prairie (2021) both rewrote their rules recently to cover roofs, walls, windows, pests and boarded homes, and both have derelict-building by-laws that require vacant buildings to be registered and kept secure and let the city take title to derelict ones under the Municipal Act. Portage said in 2024 it had 25 to 30 such properties and demolished its first. Steinbach’s answer to derelict houses has been to wait for redevelopment (reason 3).

5Licensing isn’t the only model, and data is the cheapest step

Manitoba cities have tackled problem rentals with maintenance-and-occupancy standards, derelict-building by-laws and fines, not licensing. Ontario licensing comes with fees landlords may pass on to tenants: in Waterloo a new licence for a one-bedroom rental costs $520 (2026), in London $165 plus a $176 fire inspection. The Ontario programs are also narrower than they sound: Waterloo covers low-rise rentals, London rentals of four units or less, Oshawa only the area near its college and university, and Toronto only buildings of 3+ storeys and 10+ units. The cheapest step is information: Winnipeg publishes every investigation, and Winkler wrote its priorities down (reason 7).

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Add yard, junk and garbage contraventions to the penalty-ticket schedule, so officers have a step between a warning and a city cleanup, as Brandon does.
  • Replace By-law 1663 with a community standards by-law that covers building exteriors, pests and boarded homes, as Brandon (2023) and Portage la Prairie (2021) did.
  • Pass a vacant and derelict building by-law, so empty houses awaiting redevelopment must be registered, secured and kept up, as in Brandon and Portage la Prairie.
  • Report on By-law 2157 every year: suites identified, inspected, fixed, closed and fined, and how many low-rent units were lost. A free list of suites with a Certificate of Compliance would help renters check a unit before they sign.
  • Adopt a council-approved enforcement policy with complaint priorities, as Winkler did, and add the response-time targets Winkler’s lacks (Winnipeg promises an inspection within 10 days).
  • Publish yearly enforcement figures: complaints, notices, cleanups and charges added to taxes, as Winnipeg does in far more detail.
  • Set garbage standards for multi-unit sites: bin size per unit, enclosure upkeep and collection frequency, written into development agreements.
  • Study rental licensing before deciding. Compare a light registry with Ontario-style licensing, including the cost per unit and who would pay it.
