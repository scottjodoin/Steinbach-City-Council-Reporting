Taxes and spending · By term

2022–2026: Mayor Earl Funk’s second council

How did Steinbach City Council set taxes and spending from November 2022 to October 2026, why did city taxes rise more in this term than in any other in this record, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • The biggest increases in this record. After a rate cut in the 2023 reassessment year, the rate rose 5.1% in 2024, then was held at 14.4 mills when values jumped in 2025, which raised city property-tax revenue 14.8%. 2026 was a zero-increase budget.
  • The event centre cost more, and reserves paid. The building rose from $61 million to $69.2 million; the city’s share, about $30.7 million, came mostly from reserves built over years, with $10 million borrowed on five-year terms.
  • The reasons given: construction inflation, interest rates up from about 2% to over 4%, RCMP costs set by Ottawa, reserves drawn down, and a refusal to stretch debt beyond five years.
  • One split: development fees were held to 5% a year, not the 7.5% staff advised; Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert voted against three times.
  • The bill ahead: a wastewater plant expansion with a city share of at least $65 million, and no financing plan made public by November 2026.

Council: the same seven as 2018–2022. Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert.

What happened

2023: a rate cut, and the event centre grows

In the 2023 reassessment year council cut the rate 2.8% to 13.7 mills and adopted a $67.3 million capital program. The mayor tied it to council’s stated priorities:

“And what I really like about this budget is how it reflects our priorities. 2 of our priorities are infrastructure renewal and expansion and recreation and cultural facility renewals. And this budget is loaded with that.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Mar 21, 2023 · 0:25:28

Construction prices and interest rates rose through the year. Council approved $5 million in extra borrowing authority as an event centre contingency, secured the Southeast Events Group’s $25.5 million donor pledge in writing, and in December approved an $8.15 million change order that took the building to $69.2 million. Members called it a good deal:

“the taxpayers are getting a $69+ million building for $30+ million.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert · Dec 19, 2023 · 0:38:28

The term’s only split came on development fees, the charges developers pay toward roads, sewers and other city costs of new lots. Staff proposed 7.5% a year; Councillor Zwaagstra amended it to 5%, and the by-law passed 5–2 at all three readings:

“A lot has been said about affordability, and the reality is if you're a citizen of Steinbach, you're either paying for it upfront on the development fees or you're going to be paying it through the mill rate.”

Councillor Damian Penner, voting against · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:14:48

“And I prefer to err on the side of affordability.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, voting for · Nov 7, 2023 · 0:11:52

2024: “our toughest budget”

The 2024 budget raised the rate 5.1% to 14.4 mills, the largest increase in this record. It passed unanimously with no public speakers:

“And I will say that in the years that I've been on council, I think this was our toughest budget. And the reason why it's so difficult is because we've been dealing with inflation, particularly in regards to construction costs.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Apr 2, 2024 · 0:18:11

“in the past we had a little bit of wiggle room, if that's the right word, is our supplementary property taxes, where those were added taxes on new properties, and that would be found money. And in order to hold taxes in past years, we've also started to budget for those supplementary property taxes.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Apr 2, 2024 · 0:22:06

Some renewal projects were pushed into later years. In December council arranged the $7.5 million event centre loan at 3.8% over five years.

2025: holding the rate in a reassessment year

Values rose about 15% in the 2025 reassessment. In earlier reassessment years council had cut the rate to offset that; this time it held the rate at 14.4 mills, so city property-tax revenue rose 14.8%, about $2.9 million. Members said reserves had been drawn down by the event centre and debt payments had risen. Councillor Zwaagstra asked whether borrowing over a longer term would reduce the increase; staff said yes for that year, but at a higher total cost, and council kept the five-year rule:

“We could change the borrowing policy, and we could borrow over 10, 15, or 20 years. But here is the harsh reality. … And so I don't think that changing that policy would make sense at this point.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Mar 4, 2025 · 0:31:04

“we've worked very hard over the last numerous years to hold the line on no net increases. … But eventually you see the reserves go to a point where we say this is, this is getting uncomfortable.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Mar 4, 2025 · 0:40:25

Councillor Susan Penner called it “a very significant tax increase” while seconding it, and asked that the event centre’s operating subsidy (half a mill, about $700,000 a year) be shown in the grants line and that the Chamber of Commerce’s automatically growing share of the business tax (about $225,000) be reviewed or capped. The mayor agreed it needed review; no review had come back in public by November 2026. In August council ended its $65,000-a-year economic development grant for lack of results.

In April 2025 council applied to the federal Canada Housing Infrastructure Fund for half of a $132.5 million wastewater expansion, leaving the city at least $65.4 million to pay. Councillor Zwaagstra had already warned that the project might not fit the five-year rule.

2026: no increase, sewer first, and the wastewater question

After basement backups in 2024 and 2025, the 2026 budget held the rate at 14.4 mills and put more than $20 million of its $35.8 million capital program into sewer and drainage work. The last event centre loan was arranged, leaving $10 million of debt on the building, all on five-year terms:

“there's also not too many communities can say that they can build a $76 million complex with $10 million worth of debt.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Feb 3, 2026 · 0:25:13

By May the wastewater project’s estimate had grown, by Councillor Susan Penner’s account, to more than $200 million; staff did not present that figure in a public meeting:

“Projected costs of this project have skyrocketed to over $200 million. It is entirely reasonable for us as stewards of our residents' dollars to ask, do the environmental requirements we're being asked to meet produce meaningful and measurable benefits?”

Councillor Susan Penner, opening remarks · May 5, 2026 · 0:00:12

Council also lent the event centre’s operator $400,000, to be repaid over four years. No federal decision on the wastewater application, and no plan for paying the city’s share, had been made public when the term ended.

Where members differed

Every budget, borrowing and reserve vote in this term was unanimous. The only recorded split on the topic was on development fees.
