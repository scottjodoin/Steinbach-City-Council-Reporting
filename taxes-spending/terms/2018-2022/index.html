Taxes and spending · By term

2018–2022: Mayor Earl Funk’s first council

How did Steinbach City Council set taxes and spending from November 2018 to October 2022, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • The rate barely moved. Frozen at 14.09 mills in 2019, cut to 13.70 in the 2020 reassessment year, held in 2021, and raised 0.4 mill in 2022 to pay for an extra RCMP traffic officer. Every budget passed unanimously.
  • Saving up for the event centre. Council committed $10 million plus up to half a mill a year (2019), borrowed only $7.5 million on a five-year term (2021), and in November 2022 took $13.2 million more from reserves and operating funds when the tender came in at $61 million.
  • The money fights were about who pays. Jac Siemens and Jake Hiebert voted against charging the Clearspring Greens neighbourhood most of a pathway’s cost; council then shifted costs to the city twice, ending at a 50/50 split capped at $1,610 a lot.
  • COVID-19 barely changed the budget on paper. The pool renovation was moved up and paid from reserves, and federal restart money was banked.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert.

What happened

2019: a freeze in the year of the biggest loan

The new council promised no mill-rate increase for 2019 and kept it, even though repaying the $5.375 million water supply loan, the largest in city history, made it a heavy year for debt payments:

“As a council, we made some tough choices and drafted a budget that will freeze property taxes at last year's level. … Yes, we are borrowing money for some of our capital projects, but all of our debt will be paid off over a 5-year term.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, opening remarks · Mar 19, 2019 · 0:00:46

“And I think it would be easy for council to have a good legitimate excuse or reason to raise taxes this year. We could easily have defended saying, look, our debt payments are going up significantly this year, we need to raise taxes.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Mar 19, 2019 · 0:23:40

Revenue grew instead through fees: public swim fees rose for the first time since 2008, sewer rates 8.5%, garbage about 3% and most other fees 2.5%. Council refused a developer’s request for a $10,000-a-lot demolition grant, citing a policy older than any member:

“In the 1980s, we, the city of Steinbach, developed the developer pay policy, and we've been following it since.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Mar 19, 2019 · 0:52:18

In October 2019 council signed the event centre agreement the night it was presented:

“I want you to know that tonight's resolution is to commit $10 million plus $500,000 a year. We're tying it to a mill rate.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Oct 1, 2019 · 0:51:37

2020: who pays for a pathway, and COVID-19

The Clearspring Greens pathway was planned as a local improvement, charging each of 146 lots about $4,280 for a $625,000 path. Councillors Siemens and Jake Hiebert tried to cut its scope and voted against the levy, which passed 5–2:

“But this is the first time that we're asking residences from any area to pay the majority of the cost of a pathway to connect them to our community.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Jan 7, 2020 · 1:39:10

Two weeks later, after meeting the neighbourhood, council moved the $208,000 lighting cost to a city reserve (6–1, Siemens against). In January 2021, after 78 owners objected, Councillor Susan Penner rebuilt it as a 50/50 split capped at $1,610 a lot, the same as the sidewalk fee on a new lot, and objections fell to 6. In December 2020 council kept a road levy on a disability-services charity, 5–1 (Siemens against), on the grounds that exempting one property would set a precedent.

The 2020 budget, drafted before the pandemic, cut the rate 2.8% to 13.70 so the reassessment would not raise city taxes:

“One thing I'm especially liking is that our overall debt's going down. We are paying off $2 million worth of debt this year, and our total balance for 2020 will be $7.8 million, which is fairly low for a city our size.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Apr 7, 2020 · 0:39:15

With the pool closed, its $1.479 million renovation was moved up a year and paid from the recreation reserve. The federal Safe Restart grant, $947,679, was put into a reserve for a later project. We found no public record of which 2020 items staff actually deferred.

2021–2022: steady rates, a traffic officer, and the event centre bill

The rate stayed at 13.7 in 2021. Funding requests that arrived after the budget was set went to the next study session:

“our budgets are balanced. There's actually, once our budget is planned, there's no room to pull around with it, right? So this will have to go to our next budget planning, which is July.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Mar 16, 2021 · 0:27:34

In September 2021, after complaints about street racing, council asked for a dedicated RCMP traffic officer at about $200,000 a year. That officer was the stated reason for the only increase of the term:

“There is a small tax increase of 0.4 mills, and that is mainly because of the extra RCMP officer that we're putting in place for this year in order to deal with traffic issues in our city.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · May 3, 2022 · 0:04:56

Council borrowed $7.5 million over five years toward an event centre then estimated at $42.5 million, and paid for its land and design from the recreation reserve, built up by about $1.1–1.2 million a year since a 10% tax increase in 2012 that went mostly into it. When the tender came in at a $61 million guaranteed maximum price in November 2022, council approved $11.4 million more from reserves, $1.8 million from operating funds and up to $5 million more debt:

“We're obviously going deeper into the reserve funds than we would like, and certainly deeper than administration would like.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Nov 1, 2022 · 1:53:27

The Hanover School Division asked the city to share the cost of a school RCMP officer; council agreed only if the division paid 100%.
