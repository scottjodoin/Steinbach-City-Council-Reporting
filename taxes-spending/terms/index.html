Taxes and spending · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council’s approach to taxes and spending change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

The rules stayed the same across all three councils: keep the mill rate as steady as possible, put surpluses into reserves, pay off every loan within five years, and wait for other governments’ money before big projects. What changed was the pressure on those rules. Policing costs ended a long freeze in 2017; the event centre drew down reserves; and inflation, interest rates and a reassessment produced the largest increases in 2024 and 2025. The next council faces the largest bill yet: wastewater.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

Years of freezes ended by the RCMP cost jump; the multiplex and arts centre dropped; a 4–3 budget over the golf club land deal.

43 items · 16 split votes, more than the next two terms combined

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

Rates frozen or cut, one small increase for a traffic officer; saving and borrowing for the event centre; fights over who pays for local improvements.

55 items · every budget unanimous

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

The event centre’s overrun, a 5.1% increase, the rate held in a reassessment year, development fees below staff advice, and the wastewater bill ahead.

54 items · every budget unanimous

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, who explained most budgets for a decade, and Jac Siemens, the member most often against charging costs to one group of residents, did not run. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), brings long experience of setting school budgets; Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association, holds elected office for the first time. Susan Penner, who topped the poll, is Deputy Mayor.

What the new council inherits:

  • The 2027 budget in a reassessment year. Values rose about 9% in the 2027 reassessment. Whether to cut the rate to offset it, as councils did in 2016, 2020 and 2023, or hold it, as in 2025, is the first big choice.
  • The wastewater plant. At least $65 million for the city on the 2025 estimate, possibly much more. The five-year debt rule has never been applied to anything this large.
  • Development fees, due for review in 2027 under the 2023 by-law.
  • Promised reviews that have not come back in public, including the Chamber of Commerce’s share of the business tax.

The new council’s inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026 had no budget decisions.
