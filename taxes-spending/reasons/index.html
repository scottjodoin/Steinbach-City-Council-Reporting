Taxes and spending · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for keeping taxes low, borrowing little, and not spending more, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given the same eight reasons for a decade, in nearly the same words. Most of them are sound practice and have kept the city’s tax rate and debt low. The weaker points are where a reason was applied unevenly (fairness and precedent), where it shifted costs out of sight (supplementary taxes, study sessions), or where it is about to meet a project larger than it was built for (the five-year debt rule and wastewater).

  • 1. People have limited budgets
  • 2. Infrastructure comes first
  • 3. Pay every loan off in five years
  • 4. Save first, and wait for partners
  • 5. Growth should pay, but not too much
  • 6. It isn’t ours to control
  • 7. Fairness and precedent
  • 8. Decide it at budget time

1People have limited budgets

“we are leaving the mill rate unchanged, so we are not raising taxes, and that is something that we've been consistent on as a council, is that to the greatest degree possible, we want to keep the mill rate as steady as we can because we recognize that people have limited budgets.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Apr 6, 2021 · 0:31:07

“Honestly, I don't like tax increases. I don't like them.”

Mayor Earl Funk, at the 2025 budget hearing · Mar 4, 2025 · 0:44:48
Context

The rate stayed low, but holding it had costs that members named themselves. In 2016 the mayor said four years without an increase came “sometimes to a fault”; in 2024 Councillor Siemens said earlier freezes had relied on counting supplementary taxes from new construction in advance, leaving no cushion when costs jumped. And a steady rate is not a steady bill: in the 2025 reassessment year the rate was held while values rose about 15%, so city property-tax revenue rose 14.8%.

2Infrastructure comes first

“It's not the fancy stuff, but this is what the majority of our work seems to be about, our infrastructure and our roads and everything under our roads.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Feb 4, 2025 · 0:10:03

“If our basic infrastructure isn't working in city, nothing else really matters.”

Councillor Susan Penner, on the 2026 budget after two years of basement backups · Mar 17, 2026 · 0:42:27
Context

Infrastructure did come first in most budgets, and the city’s asset management was praised by its auditors. But the largest project of the decade was recreation: the event centre, whose overrun drew down the reserves that would otherwise have cushioned infrastructure. In 2024 some renewal projects were pushed into later years, and the asset management policy was put in writing only in 2023, when the province required it. After the 2024 and 2025 storms, council moved flood and sewer work up in the 2026 budget.

3Pay every loan off in five years

“The city continues to use a 5-year amortization term for all of its debt, allowing us to borrow at lower interest rates than if a longer term was used.”

Brian Hrehirchuk, Director of Finance, at the 2018 budget hearing · Mar 20, 2018 · 0:00:58

“And in particular, our 5-year debt policy. We're continuing with that policy even now, that policy that's been in place for 25, 30 years, and it has paid off in, in a big way over the years because it means that we are not hamstringing future councils with the, uh, with projects.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Mar 21, 2023 · 0:21:40

“Will we be able to keep to this policy forever? I suspect there are some projects coming up, thinking along wastewater, which will make it difficult to actually do that.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, opening remarks · Mar 18, 2025 · 0:00:17
Context

The rule is a long-standing practice, not a by-law, and it was restated at almost every borrowing in this record. It kept total debt low (about $6–14 million through the decade) and interest costs small. Its cost is bigger yearly payments: debt payments rose from about 13.5% of the city tax rate in 2025 to about 17% in 2026, and in 2025 staff confirmed a longer term would have lowered that year’s increase, at a higher total cost. The wastewater expansion, with a city share of at least $65 million, is several times larger than anything the rule has carried.

“And I think what we'll do is we will refer this to our study session for July. That's where we build our budget for 2026, and then it'll be finalized in November.”

Mayor Earl Funk · May 6, 2025 · 0:37:53
Context

Weighing every request against the whole budget is sound. The problem is visibility: the study sessions where the budget is built are not recorded, and most referred requests never got a public answer (see the advocacy report). Public budget hearings drew almost no speakers in any year of this record.
