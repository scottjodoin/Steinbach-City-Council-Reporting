Taxes and spending · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s tax rate, debt, reserves, growth fees and budget process compare with other Manitoba cities and a fast-growing Alberta town, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong on the numbers. Steinbach’s city tax rate is low for a Manitoba city, its debt is small and short, and its reserves are large for its size.
  • Weak on the rules and the openness. Its five-year debt rule and its reserves have no written policy, its growth fees are low by peer standards, and its budget is built in unrecorded sessions with no public deliberation day, survey or tax calculator.
  • The test ahead. Peers facing big water and wastewater bills have published how they will pay (Portage la Prairie: $75 million over 30 years). Steinbach has not.

Key findings

1Low rate, rising bills

Steinbach’s rate is among the lowest of Manitoba cities, though Winkler and Niverville are lower before their flat levies. But a low rate is not a frozen bill. The typical home’s city tax rose from about $1,161 in 2016 to about $1,282 in 2022, then to about $1,620 in 2025 and 2026, mostly from the 2024 increase and from holding the rate when values rose in 2025. Both facts are true, and residents should hear both. See council’s reason 1.

2The five-year rule is distinctive, and unwritten

No peer we checked repays all its debt in five years. The rule kept interest low and left room for the next project, and the 2025 auditor praised it. But it lives only in practice and in what members say, and it makes yearly payments larger: in 2025 staff confirmed a longer term would have lowered that year’s increase. The wastewater plant, at several times the size of any past loan, will decide whether the rule bends. A written policy would say in advance when an exception is allowed. See reason 3.

3Big reserves, no targets

Reserves grew from $13.8 million in 2015 to $47.9 million in 2025, paid for most of the city’s share of the event centre, and dipped to $38.6 million in 2024 before recovering. Selkirk and Okotoks set targets for how large each reserve should be and report against them; Steinbach decides transfers year by year. See reason 4.

4Growth fees are low

Councillors themselves noted in 2023 that Steinbach’s development fees were below Winkler, Morden, Brandon and Niverville, and that even staff’s 7.5% a year would not cover growth. Council chose 5% for housing affordability. The fee schedule is due for review in 2027, and no study of what growth actually costs the city has been presented in public. See reason 5.

5Much of the real budgeting is off the record

Councillors describe weekends of study sessions where priorities are set. Those meetings are not recorded, so residents see the result at the hearing but not the trade-offs, and public hearings drew almost no speakers in any year of this record. Brandon and Portage la Prairie show that a growing Manitoba city can deliberate on its budget in public. See reason 8.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Publish a wastewater financing plan: the city’s share, whether it will be borrowed beyond five years, and a fallback if federal money doesn’t come.
  • Put the debt and reserve rules in writing: the five-year rule and when an exception is allowed, and target sizes for each reserve, as Selkirk and Okotoks do.
  • Base the 2027 development-fee review on a growth cost study, and publish the current fees.
  • Deliberate in public: record the budget study sessions or hold a public deliberation day, as Brandon and Portage la Prairie do.
  • Report study-session decisions, including a yearly list of grant requests with the amount and outcome of each.
  • Show residents their bill: the typical household’s city tax beside the mill rate in each budget, and a tax calculator, so the effect of reassessments is visible.
  • Itemize the long-range capital plan publicly; staff keep a multi-year look-ahead, but only yearly totals are presented.
