Earl Funk: taxes, budgets and spending

What did Earl Funk say and do about Steinbach's taxes, budgets, debt and spending between December 2015 and November 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor 2010–2018 · Mayor since November 2018 (re-elected by acclamation in 2026)

In short

As a councillor, Earl Funk was one of the members most willing to vote no on money. He voted against the 2017 budget over the borrowing planned for a $24 million performing arts centre, and against the 2018 budget and its tax by-law because, he said, the small tax increase matched the first payment on the golf club land deal. He also moved council's 2016 request that the school division freeze school taxes.

As mayor since November 2018 he has chaired every financial plan, and every one has passed unanimously. He speaks for low taxes and the five-year debt rule, and he has backed the big projects that reserves made possible, above all the event centre. He sends most funding requests from groups to council's unrecorded budget study sessions. In 2025 he said plainly that he dislikes tax increases, and backed one anyway.

“Honestly, I don't like tax increases. I don't like them.”

Mayor Earl Funk · On the 2025 budget, which held the rate in a reassessment year · Mar 4, 2025 · 0:44:48

Term by term

2014–2018

A frequent critic of how money decisions were made. He moved the 2016 request that the Hanover School Division freeze school taxes (school taxes were 55% of the bill, he said), opposed reaffirming the city's $7.5 million arts-centre share when the estimate reached $24.3 million (2016), voted against the 2017 budget over arts-centre borrowing, and voted against the $500,000 golf club land purchase (2017) and then the 2018 budget and every reading of its tax by-law. He argued that if taxes must rise, it should be for something most residents support, and contrasted the golf deal with the seniors' centre, which he said followed the rules and got 'a pat on the back.'

Took part in 16 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2014–2018 story.

2018–2022

Elected mayor in 2018. He chaired budgets that froze the rate (2019, 2021), cut it to offset reassessment (2020) and raised it 0.4 mill for a traffic police officer he championed (2022). He praised falling debt, proposed banking the federal COVID-19 restart money in a reserve, and committed the city to the event centre ($10 million plus up to half a mill a year). On the Clearspring Greens pathway he first said the 2019 budget had no room, then backed moving the lighting cost to the city after meeting the neighbourhood.

Took part in 26 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

He framed budgets around council's priorities (infrastructure, recreation, safety, relations with other governments). In September 2023 he called for a 'rethink' as interest rates and construction costs rose, and then backed the 5.1% increase in 2024 and the larger revenue increase in 2025. He supported holding development fees to 5% a year for entry-level housing while admitting council had waited too long to review them. He agreed the Chamber of Commerce's share of the business tax needed review, but no review came back in public. He has consistently sent groups' funding requests to study sessions.

Took part in 38 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

From critic to steward: as a councillor he voted against the spending choices of the majority, often on grounds of process; as mayor he leads a council that decides money matters by consensus, mostly in study sessions, and defends the long-standing rules (low mill rate, five-year debt, reserves first) that he once invoked against the arts centre.
