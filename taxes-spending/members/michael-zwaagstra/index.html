Michael Zwaagstra: taxes, budgets and spending

What did Michael Zwaagstra say and do about Steinbach's taxes, budgets, debt and spending between December 2015 and November 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor in all three terms covered · did not run in 2026

In short

Michael Zwaagstra was the member who explained the budget most often. He moved most financial plans, debt by-laws, reserve transfers and fee changes, and he gave the same reasons year after year: keep the mill rate steady because 'people have limited budgets,' put surpluses into reserves, charge what services actually cost, and pay off every loan within five years so that council is 'not hamstringing future councils.'

He was also the sharpest critic of the old council's big spending choices: he voted against the 2017 budget over the $24 million arts centre and called the golf club land purchase 'a bailout that is dressed up as a land deal.' In 2023 he amended staff's proposed 7.5% yearly rise in development fees down to 5%, citing housing affordability, while saying even 7.5% would not cover the cost of growth. In 2025 he warned that the wastewater expansion may make the five-year rule hard to keep.

“And in particular, our 5-year debt policy. We're continuing with that policy even now, that policy that's been in place for 25, 30 years, and it has paid off in, in a big way over the years because it means that we are not hamstringing future councils with the, uh, with projects.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · On the five-year debt rule, at the 2023 budget · Mar 21, 2023 · 0:21:40

Term by term

2014–2018

Moved the 'net zero' 2016 budget and explained it; argued for development fees so new growth pays its impact; refused a dog-licence increase without a stated purpose. Seconded dropping the multiplex as unaffordable on a $13 million tax base, voted against the 2017 budget and tax by-law over the arts centre, led the case against the golf land deal (December 2017) and voted against the 2018 budget, saying the increase would 'disappear' without the golf payment. Still moved most of the infrastructure borrowing, all on five-year terms.

Took part in 32 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2014–2018 story.

2018–2022

Council's main spokesman on money: framed the 2019 freeze against federal deficits, moved the 2020 and 2021 plans, refused a developer's demolition grant and a charity's exemption from a road levy as unfair to other taxpayers, and was 'not in favour' of spreading the Clearspring Greens pathway cost citywide (he later moved the compromise). Initiated the traffic RCMP officer and the 0.4-mill increase that paid for it; refused the school division's cost-share for a school officer. Candid that the event centre tender took the city 'deeper into the reserve funds than we would like.'

Took part in 47 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

Moved both reassessment-year and increase-year budgets and called 2024's 5.1% increase 'our toughest budget.' Cut staff's development-fee proposal from 7.5% to 5% a year for affordability. In 2025 he set out why the rate was held in a reassessment year (infrastructure first, reserves drawn down by the event centre, RCMP costs set federally), rejected stretching debt beyond five years, said growth does not pay for itself, and warned that wastewater may break the five-year rule. Did not run in 2026.

Took part in 47 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

Consistent across all three councils: the same fiscal rules, argued the same way. What changed was his position relative to the majority: a frequent dissenter under Mayor Goertzen, he became the majority's main explainer under Mayor Funk.
