Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Taxes and spending

Taxes, spending and priorities: how does Steinbach pay its way?

How has Steinbach City Council set taxes, borrowed and spent since December 2015? What reasons has it given for holding the line, how did each council member act, and how does the record compare with other Manitoba cities?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026.

Executive summary

Over eleven years and three councils, Steinbach kept to the same few money rules. They kept the city’s tax rate among the lowest in Manitoba, and they are about to meet their biggest test.

  • A low, steady rate. The city’s mill rate went from 13.23 in 2016 to 14.4 in 2026. Councils cut it to offset reassessments in 2016, 2020 and 2023, and raised it for policing in 2017, 2018 and 2022. The largest rise was 5.1% in 2024.
  • Steady rate, rising bills. In 2025 council held the rate while property values rose about 15%, so city tax revenue rose 14.8%. The typical home’s city tax rose from about $1,282 in 2022 to about $1,620 in 2026.
  • Five-year debt and reserves first. Every loan is paid off within five years, and surpluses go into reserves. That let the city build a $76 million event centre with $10 million of debt. The coming wastewater expansion, with a city share of at least $65 million, is far larger than anything the rule has carried.
  • Divided in 2018, unanimous since. The 2018 budget passed 4–3 over the golf club land deal. Every budget since 2019 has been unanimous; the few split votes since were about who pays: a neighbourhood pathway (2020) and development fees kept below staff advice (2023).
  • Much of the real budgeting is out of sight. Budgets are built in unrecorded study sessions, public hearings drew almost no speakers, groups’ funding requests are referred there and rarely answered in public, and promised reviews have not come back.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council kept taxes frozen for years, then divided over big recreation projects and a land deal. Mayor Earl Funk’s councils, with the same six councillors for eight years, decided money matters by consensus, saved for and built the event centre, and faced the steepest cost pressures. The council elected in October 2026 inherits the wastewater bill.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts. The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence. Speakers were matched to names with AI assistance, from how they were addressed in each meeting and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations and other members of the public are identified only by initials. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain. Where we trim a remark, leave out a personal passage, or identify a speaker by initials, the full remarks can be watched at the linked moment in the official meeting video.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched, including the separately recorded budget hearings of 2016, 2017 and 2018: transcripts of 243 meeting videos and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist; and the April 5, 2022 budget hearing, which has no audio, so the 2022 budget is from the minutes. Budget study sessions are closed and unrecorded.
  • What counts. 152 items: each year’s financial plan, borrowing, reserves, fees and utility rates, development fees, local-improvement levies, grants policy, council pay, provincial and federal money, and the budget process. Individual funding letters from groups are covered in the advocacy report. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so who voted which way is taken from what members said. The April 2016 multiplex vote (5–2) and the December 2016 arts-centre vote (4–3) are confirmed by SteinbachOnline’s reports; the December 2017 golf-club vote (about 4–3) is inferred from members’ stated positions.
  • Figures. Mill rates and budget totals are from staff presentations at the financial plan hearings and the minutes. Typical-home tax figures are from SteinbachOnline (2023) and the 2026 Financial Plan presentation. The “over $200 million” wastewater figure is Councillor Susan Penner’s statement (May 5, 2026); staff did not present it in a public meeting. Not confirmed: the outcomes of the 2019 federal infrastructure applications, the 2025 federal wastewater application and the outdoor pool grant applications; which 2020 items were deferred for COVID-19; and what happened to the difference when 2023 supplementary taxes came in far above budget.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
