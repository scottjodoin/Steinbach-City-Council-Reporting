Steinbach City Council · Jan 2022 – Sept 2026 · Chamber forum topic: priorities and how to pay for them

Taxes, spending and priorities: how does Steinbach pay its way?

What have council's priorities been, how has it paid for them, and how has it kept taxes reasonable while the city grew? How does its record compare with other Manitoba cities?

Quotes link to their moment in the official meeting videos; budget documents and peer sources are linked. Last updated Oct 13, 2026.

Bottom line

  • Council's stated priorities for 2022–26 were infrastructure renewal and expansion, recreation and cultural facilities, safety, and relations with other governments. Infrastructure came first in every budget debate.
  • How it pays: reserves built over years, a rule of paying off all debt within 5 years, development fees, and partner money (provincial and federal grants, plus $25.5M in private donations for the Event Centre).
  • The tax rate is low for Manitoba: $648 in city tax per $100,000 of home value in 2026, against $815 in Brandon, $948 in Selkirk and $1,253 in Thompson.
  • Bills still rose. The typical home's city tax rose from about $1,282 (2022) to $1,620 (2026), because of a 5.1% rate increase in 2024 and holding the rate steady in the 2025 reassessment year. Every budget passed unanimously.
  • The next test: a wastewater expansion with a city share of at least $65M, and much of the budget work happens in study sessions the public can't watch.

Context

The five-year debt rule is a long-standing practice, not a by-law. The Southeast Event Centre shows how the funding mix works: about $74M in total, of which the city paid about $30M from reserves, operating funds and short-term borrowing, governments $18.5M, and donors $25.5M. Debt payments take 2.427 mills, about 17% of the 2026 city tax rate (2026 Financial Plan).

Context

Holding the rate at 14.4 mills in the 2025 reassessment year meant property-tax revenue rose 14.8%, about $2.9M. Staff estimated about 11% of that came from rising assessments. Reserves fell from $44.3M (2023) to $38.6M (2024) as the Event Centre went over budget, then recovered to $47.9M by the end of 2025. The 2026 budget held the rate again and was presented as a zero-tax-increase budget.

Context

Many requests from outside groups were sent to unrecorded budget study sessions or simply “received as information”. Key findings

1Low rate, rising bills

Steinbach's city tax rate is among the lowest of Manitoba cities, and the commercial and residential rates are the same. But the typical home's city bill rose about 26% from 2022 to 2026, from about $1,282 to $1,620 (SteinbachOnline; 2026 Financial Plan). That came from the 2024 rate increase and from keeping the rate flat when assessments jumped in 2025. Both statements are true, and residents should hear both.

2The five-year rule meets its biggest test

Paying off debt in five years has kept interest low and left room for the next project. The coming wastewater expansion is far larger than anything paid for that way: $132.5M in the 2025 funding application, with the city's share at least $65M. Councillor Zwaagstra has said himself that it may be hard to keep the rule. Whether, and how, to bend it is a decision the next council will face in public.

3Much of the real budgeting is off the record

Councillors describe weekends of study sessions where priorities are set. Those meetings aren't recorded, so residents see the result at the hearing but not the trade-offs: which requests were turned down and why. Publishing study-session decisions and a list of grant requests and outcomes would make the record match the effort.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Publish a wastewater funding plan: the city's share, whether it will be borrowed beyond five years, and a fallback if federal money doesn't come.
  • Itemize the long-range capital plan publicly; staff keep a 7–10 year look-ahead, but only yearly totals are presented.
  • Report study-session decisions, including a yearly list of grant requests with the amount and outcome of each.
  • Show typical-household bills alongside the mill rate in each budget, so residents can see the effect of reassessments.
  • Base the 2027 development-fee renewal on a cost study of what growth actually costs the city.
  • Adopt a written debt policy setting out the five-year rule and when an exception is allowed.
