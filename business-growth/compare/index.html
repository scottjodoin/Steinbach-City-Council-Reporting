Business growth and investment · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s industrial land supply, economic development effort, business incentives, business tax and downtown programs compare with other Manitoba cities and two fast-growing Prairie cities, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong results, light tools. Steinbach’s 2025 building permits ($118.0 million) were well ahead of Winkler’s and Morden’s, with low property taxes and rules that council has repeatedly eased. It does this with fewer tools than its peers: no economic development staff or office, no published incentives, and no published land prices.
  • Land is the binding limit. Like Morden and Brandon, Steinbach has run out of serviced industrial land. Morden and Winkler bought large blocks ahead of demand in 2022; Selkirk keeps a land-bank reserve. Steinbach has the same tool, a development corporation, but has added land a few lots at a time.
  • The business tax arrangement is unusual. Most peers levy no business tax; Selkirk levies one and keeps it. Steinbach levies 0.5% and passes it to the Chamber of Commerce without a written agreement.

Key findings

1Land and servicing are the real limits

Demand is not Steinbach’s problem: lots sell “as soon as they came on the market,” the economic development director told council that investors want serviced land, and the city ran out again in 2026. Its wastewater system has room until about 2032; the expansion is estimated at $132.5 million. Morden faced the same shortage and its development corporation agreed to buy 142.5 acres in 2022 (about $20,350 an acre); Winkler bought 113 acres the same year. Steinbach’s development corporation could do the same, but no land purchase ahead of demand has been discussed in public, and its holdings and prices are not published. See reason 2 and reason 3.

2The office failed partly because success was never defined

Council ended the economic development grant because it could not see results, but it had set no targets in 2021, and the office’s reports came twice in five years. Peers that keep economic development going put it in writing: Portage la Prairie’s agency has a four-year city–RM agreement (the city’s share $274,952 in 2026; city and RM together about $387,000, though the RM’s share after 2024 is unconfirmed) and publishes audited statements. Whatever, if anything, replaces Steinbach’s office would start better with written measures. See reason 7.

3Steinbach spends least on economic development, by choice

From 2026 the city budgets nothing for an economic development office of its own; peers budget from $184,000 (Niverville, 2025) to $1.14 million (Brandon, 2026). Council’s view is that land, low taxes and partners do the work (reason 6), and the results so far are strong. The risk is that nobody is assigned to the work in between: answering investors, tracking leads, and planning land years ahead.

4The Chamber’s business tax needs a written deal

Steinbach is the only city we checked that levies a business tax and hands it to the Chamber of Commerce. The amount grew about 66% from 2015 to 2025 while the rate stayed at 0.5%. Councillors questioned it in 2019, 2025 and 2026, and businesses asked to keep it; what is missing is any public agreement on what the money buys. Selkirk shows the other model: a higher business tax kept as city revenue. See reason 8.

5Tax breaks matter less than land and speed

Research on local business incentives finds they tip only a small share of location decisions: a median of 12.7% across 34 estimates, and 3.4% in the estimates with no obvious bias (Upjohn Institute). Steinbach’s lack of incentives is therefore less of a gap than its lack of land. If council ever adds one, peers show the form: written, rule-based and time-limited, like Niverville’s grant or Martensville’s 2026 by-law with a built-in review. Winkler’s well-known credit applied to projects started within five years of 2017 and may have lapsed; we could not confirm whether it was renewed.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Keep a rolling supply of serviced industrial land, for example two to three years ahead of demand, using the development corporation to buy land early, as Morden and Winkler did.
  • Publish lot prices, inventory and the development corporation’s yearly statements, as Camrose does, so investors and residents can see what is for sale and what it costs taxpayers.
  • Decide openly what replaces the economic development office: an in-house officer, a regional agency with Hanover and La Broquerie, or formal delivery through the Chamber. Whatever is chosen should have written targets and a yearly public report.
  • Put the business-tax grant under a service agreement with the Chamber, setting out what it pays for and an annual report, and decide in public whether to cap its growth.
  • Keep the wastewater expansion on schedule, since industrial growth stops when servicing does.
  • Publish permit processing times and a yearly permit report.
  • Consider small, rule-based tools only once land is available, such as a time-limited credit on new municipal tax with a built-in review, and a low-cost downtown storefront grant like Selkirk’s or Portage’s.
