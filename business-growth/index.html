Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Business growth and investment

Business growth and investment: what has council done?

What has Steinbach City Council done since December 2015 to help local businesses grow and to attract new investment, through industrial and commercial land, economic development partnerships, downtown, business taxes and fees, and regulation? What has it stopped doing, what reasons has it given, how did each council member act, and how do other councils compare?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 9, 2026.

Executive summary

Over eleven years and three councils, Steinbach’s approach to business stayed the same: supply land, keep taxes and rules light, and leave promotion to partners. It has worked well enough that the city keeps running out of industrial lots.

  • Land is council’s main business tool. The city and its Steinbach Community Development Corporation service industrial lots and sell them at or near cost. They sold out in 2019, ran short in 2021–2022 and were gone again by early 2026; 11 more on August Cove were approved in 2025. Council also assembled a downtown block (eight lots, 2016–2018), and it consistently keeps retail out of the subsidized industrial park.
  • Rules were mostly eased for business. Mobile-sign rules were rewritten at businesses’ request (2016), businesses on gravel roads no longer have to pave (2018), most parking and sign variances were granted, and the 2026 zoning by-law cut required downtown parking by a third. The few refusals protected neighbours, the city’s look or the industrial park.
  • An economic development office was tried and dropped. From 2021 to 2025 the city matched private money for a Chamber-led office, up to $65,000 a year, without setting any targets. Council ended it in 2025 for lack of results, and the city now funds no economic development office or staff of its own, while peers budget $184,000 (Niverville) to $1.14 million (Brandon) a year.
  • The business tax goes to the Chamber. A 0.5% business tax is passed to the Chamber of Commerce less a 5% fee: about $216,000 in 2025, with the revenue up 66% since 2015, and no written list of what it pays for. Councillors questioned the arrangement in 2019, 2025 and 2026; promised reviews have produced no public result.
  • Members mostly agreed. The sharpest splits were the 2017 golf club land purchase (4–3), the 2018 Sunday-shopping ballot question (defeated 2–5), retail in the industrial park and development fees (5–2), and a 2025 request for a cannabis plebiscite (refused 6–1).

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council rewrote sign rules for businesses, bought land downtown and split over a golf club land deal. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council sold out its industrial lots, opened more, and started an economic development office with the Chamber. His second council ended that office, kept guarding the industrial park and simplified its zoning rules. The council elected in October 2026 starts with no city industrial lots on the market and no economic development office.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

Sign rules loosened at the Chamber’s request, a downtown block assembled, paving relief on gravel roads, a 4–3 golf land deal and no Sunday-shopping vote.

36 items · 20 split votes, most of any term

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

Industrial lots sold out and Acres Drive extended; a $65,000-a-year economic development office; the Chamber grant first questioned; COVID-19 letters but no local relief.

26 items · 7 split votes

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

The economic development office ended; August Cove lots; retail kept out of the industrial park; a shorter zoning by-law with less downtown parking; the Chamber’s share questioned again.

28 items · 7 split votes

2026–2030

The new council

Michael Zwaagstra and Jac Siemens are gone; Ron Falk and Greg Carlos are new. First up: industrial land, wastewater capacity and what, if anything, replaces the economic development office.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents and people speaking for organizations are identified by initials. Businesses and organizations are named. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts. The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every council quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence: from hand-checked speaker maps for meetings from January 2022, and for earlier meetings from how speakers were addressed and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations and other members of the public, including staff of the Chamber of Commerce and Steinbach Economic Development, are identified only by initials. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain. Councillors Jake and Bill Hiebert’s August 2025 remarks on ending the economic development grant are given from news coverage, because the transcript cannot tell their voices apart with confidence.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched (243 meetings): transcripts of the meeting videos, and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (not yet transcribed) and four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist. The April 5, 2022 video has no audio, and there are no transcripts of July 15 and August 19, 2025, so those decisions are from the minutes alone. Budget study sessions and committee meetings are closed and unrecorded.
  • What counts. 90 items: industrial and commercial land, the Steinbach Community Development Corporation, downtown, the business tax and the Chamber of Commerce, economic development, signs, parking, paving and other business rules, development fees, Sunday shopping, cannabis retail and relations with neighbouring municipalities over fringe development. One further item, a councillor’s remarks about her own business, is left out. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so who voted which way is taken from what members said. The December 2017 golf club vote (4–3) is from the transcript.
  • Figures. Building permit totals are the city’s figures as reported by SteinbachOnline (the city does not post an annual permit report). Business tax revenue is from the audited financial statements; from 2019 the statements show one line that appears to include business fees. The Chamber’s 2025 share (about $216,000) is our arithmetic: 2025 business tax revenue of $227,838 less the 5% fee. Peer figures are from each city’s own budget, by-laws and news reports, checked November 7–8, 2026; see How Steinbach compares.
  • Not confirmed. Prices and sizes of the city’s industrial lots and the development corporation’s finances (accepted by council each year but not posted); the Chamber’s grant by year after 2022; the outcome of the promised reviews of the business tax (2019, 2025) and of Steinbach Economic Development’s 2025 request to double its city grant, all handled in unrecorded study sessions; the result of the 2023 provincial grant application for Hespeler Street; the result of the 2018 cannabis plebiscite; and whether 2023 ($204.7 million) or 2024 ($104.9 million) was the city’s record permit year, as news reports conflict. A councillor’s 2025 statement that Winkler’s chamber gets $65,000 from its city could not be confirmed and is not repeated as fact.
  • Claims. Figures given by others are theirs and unverified: Steinbach Economic Development’s claim of more than $12 million a year in economic impact, the mayor’s statement that the Steinbach Chamber is the second-fastest-growing in Canada, and a councillor’s report that business owners feared being bought out downtown.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote on these pages was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026, with no business-growth items. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
