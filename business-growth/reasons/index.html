Business growth and investment · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for how it helps businesses grow, for the limits it keeps, and for not doing more, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given the same eight reasons for a decade. Most describe a coherent approach: let businesses operate, let the market lead, and keep the city’s subsidized land for industry. The weaker points are where a reason was never tested against results (the economic development office, the Chamber’s grant), where a promised review never came back in public, and where “the market decides” sits beside the fact that the city itself is the main supplier of industrial land.

  • 1. Businesses should be free to advertise and operate
  • 2. The market leads; council clears the way
  • 3. Subsidized land is for industry, and one rule for all
  • 4. Protect neighbours and the city’s look
  • 5. Keep the cost of building down
  • 6. Economic development is a partnership
  • 7. Show us results first
  • 8. We’ll review it

1Businesses should be free to advertise and operate

“I think we have to go back and look at the spirit of the sign bylaw is to allow advertising.”

Councillor Earl Funk, rewriting the mobile sign rules · Sept 6, 2016 · 0:54:13

“I can’t recall a single time that we’ve denied an electronic sign. … it makes sense that a business should be able to advertise its services.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jul 7, 2026 · 0:44:42

“The fact is we want to encourage business and growth in our community and we want to be reasonable as well. Starting up a business is already very difficult and very costly.”

Councillor Susan Penner, on a one-stall parking variance for a new grocery · Sept 21, 2021 · 0:22:07
Context

Council acted on this reason consistently: signs, parking and paving relief were granted far more often than refused, often against management’s recommendation. The counterpoint is whose view counted. In 2015 a Councillor Penner objected that only sign users had been heard, not the wider public consulted on the city’s appearance. “Businesses support it” was also council’s reason for keeping the business tax that funds the Chamber of Commerce and for not putting Sunday shopping to voters in 2018, when a former councillor, D.B., argued that shift workers drive to Winnipeg on Sundays. In both cases the business view came largely through the Chamber or through members’ own contacts; no survey of businesses or residents was presented in public.

2The market leads; council clears the way

“the best projects aren’t governments always coming up with ideas. It’s the private developers who do it, and we certainly see that here.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the Millbrook Market plan · Mar 3, 2020 · 0:21:57

“The, the best way that government can create jobs is not by actually creating jobs, but creating an environment for it, for private entities to create jobs.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Apr 20, 2021 · 0:27:49

“We’ve always said, council, that the developers do know what the market is saying”

Mayor Earl Funk, as council let a developer drop most of a building’s commercial space · Oct 20, 2026 · 0:12:33
Context

Growth has been strong by any measure: building permits passed $100 million in most years since 2021, and the population was put at 17,806 in the 2021 census, up 32% in ten years. But the market did not deliver everything council wanted. Downtown density waited years for a developer; main-floor commercial space was waived twice when tenants could not be found; and the industrial land market is largely the city’s own, since the city and its development corporation build and sell most of the industrial lots. Council’s choices about when to service land shape what the market can do.

3Subsidized land is for industry, and one rule for all

“So the city builds these industrial parks, we’re using tax dollars and granting, and we sell the lots at cost or just above cost.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Feb 18, 2025 · 0:33:15

“So they’re investing in our commercial areas, spending a lot, and then, you know, there’s this retail space in a subsidized area, and it really is— makes it not a level playing field.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Oct 4, 2022 · 0:21:19

“It creates a precedent.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, against a lower road standard for one private commercial park · May 3, 2016 · 0:22:37

“This might be perceived as unfair to other business owners as we are using tax dollars to help the golf course out in their financial position.”

Councillor Earl Funk · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:11:37
Context

This is council’s most consistent line: of the requests for retail in the industrial park, broad ones were refused and only narrow, tenant-tied ones allowed. Applicants had their own case: Adult & Teen Challenge’s D.E. said no suitable commercial space existed for its thrift store, and the landlord of 110 Industrial Road pointed to retail already on the street. A resident, G.S., asked in 2025 whether cheaper industrial land draws retail away from commercial zones. One thing residents cannot check is the size of the subsidy: the city publishes no lot prices, and the development corporation’s financial statements, accepted by council each year, are not posted. “Fairness” was also invoked on both sides of the golf club deal and of the 2023 development fees.

Context

Some reviews did arrive, slowly: the downtown parking review asked for in 2018 came through the new zoning by-law, which cut required parking by a third in 2026, after years of case-by-case variances. Others did not: the Chamber’s share was to be reviewed in 2019 and again in 2025, and no result, cap or agreement has been made public; lit digital mobile signs, deferred in 2016 to a future zoning review, were never decided on the record. Because study sessions are not recorded, residents cannot tell whether a review happened and was rejected, or never happened.
