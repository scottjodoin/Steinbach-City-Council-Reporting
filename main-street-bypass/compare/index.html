A Main Street bypass · How Steinbach compares

Highways through town: how Steinbach compares

How have other Manitoba and Canadian cities handled provincial highways through town, by bypass, truck route or rebuild, and how does Steinbach’s approach to through traffic and trucks compare?

In short

  • Steinbach’s path is the usual one in Manitoba. Winkler, Morden and Niverville are also rebuilding the provincial highway through town with the province. The Manitoba cities that were bypassed, Portage la Prairie, Brandon, Selkirk and Dauphin, got their bypasses decades ago in provincial highway programs, not at a city’s request.
  • Where it falls short is evidence. Cities that weighed a bypass more recently measured first: Yorkton with a licence-plate survey, Brandon with an origin–destination study in its road plan, Lloydminster with a traffic model. Steinbach has counted neither its through traffic nor its trucks, has no dangerous-goods route, and the highway access plans it helped pay for are unpublished.
  • A bypass is mostly a truck question. Research and recent examples agree that bypasses divert trucks far more than cars, and that the old route usually stays a truck route unless the by-law changes. The truck movement a Steinbach bypass would serve, north to west, has never been measured.

Key findings

1The bypass was dismissed, not measured

Council’s only stated reason against a bypass, that traffic comes to Steinbach rather than through it, rests on a count of intersections, not trips. Its numbers make a large north–south through flow unlikely and suggest the west-to-north movement is modest, at most about 550 vehicles an hour in the evening peak and probably far fewer. But that is an upper limit worked out from turning counts, not a measurement. See reason 1.

Yorkton is the closest match: a regional service centre of about 16,000 on several provincial highways. In 2016 its province, city and neighbouring RMs recorded licence plates on all seven highways into the area for 12 hours. “On average, 11,300 vehicles enter the study area on a highway each day, including 1,460 trucks. Of that number 1,700 vehicles (15%) including 475 trucks (32%) continued through the City”. The study concluded “it does not appear the through traffic demand on Highway 16 will warrant a high-speed through route” (Yorkton Regional Transportation Study, 2018). That is Steinbach council’s argument, settled with data, and it still found a third of trucks passing through. In Moosomin, Saskatchewan, a short provincial count in 2016 found “out of 83 vehicles, only 28 were through traffic”; the town questioned how short the count was and asked for a fuller study (World-Spectator, Sept 7, 2016).

2Steinbach’s approach matches its closest Manitoba peers

The province controls the highways, and no Manitoba city of Steinbach’s size has had a bypass built in decades. Winkler, Morden and Niverville, like Steinbach, are getting the highway through town rebuilt or its intersections upgraded with the province. Portage la Prairie (1970) and Brandon (1959) were bypassed as part of the Trans-Canada; Selkirk and Dauphin also have older provincial bypasses whose dates weren’t confirmed. The province’s 2025/26 plan lists intersection improvements in Steinbach, not a new route. See reason 2.

3A bypass alone doesn’t take trucks off Main Street

Bypassed cities usually keep their old highway as a truck route. Portage la Prairie’s 2025 traffic by-law designates Saskatchewan Avenue, the old PTH 1, as a truck route and a dangerous-goods route from one city limit to the other, fifty-five years after its bypass opened. Okotoks, Alberta, bypassed by the Highway 2 expressway, still lists Highway 2A through town as a truck route (2025). Where trucks did move, it was quick and large: weeks after Cornwall, PEI’s Trans-Canada bypass opened in 2019, the province “measured 80 per cent of truck traffic being diverted around the town,” while only about 40% of westbound traffic at the North River roundabout used the bypass (CBC). Steinbach routes all through trucks on Main Street and Brandt Street by design, and has no truck counts to say how many would use a bypass.

4The real bypasses are city streets, built without a plan

Choosing to fix the highway and let development build the edge roads (reason 3) moved cross-town traffic onto Old Tom Road, Park Road West, Loewen Boulevard and, residents say, Friesen Avenue. That is a reasonable way to serve local trips, the kind the counts suggest dominate. But no plan says which streets are meant to carry that traffic, the 2020 promise of one for the edge roads is unmet, and traffic studies aren’t required on city streets (reason 4). For how much the city is expected to grow, see Planning for the future.

5Elsewhere, bypasses follow trucks, and the research is mixed

In the Canadian cities that built one recently, the province planned and paid, sometimes with federal money, and the trigger was trucks: Yorkton’s West Truck Route served new canola plants (2012), and Estevan’s bypass (2015) came after truck traffic in the area rose more than 90% from 2007. A 2005 review for New Jersey’s transportation department found rural bypasses “generally achieved their goal of diverting truck traffic out of downtown areas, but did not necessarily reduce overall downtown traffic volume,” and that “the building of a bypass road has little economic impact on the bypassed community” (Rutgers, 2005). Wisconsin’s 1998 study of 17 bypassed communities found origin–destination surveys showing “an average of 76% of all vehicles having an origin or destination within the city or village” (WisDOT). A Texas study warned that “the better a relief route works from a traffic standpoint, the greater its adverse impact on local per capita sales” (Srinivasan & Kockelman, 2002). Florida’s guidance says a bypass “may be appropriate where heavy truck traffic causes continuing unacceptable impacts” or “where a high percentage of trips are just passing through the community” (CUTR, 2014). No Canadian or Manitoba standard sets a through-traffic share that justifies a bypass. Together this supports council’s view that a bypass wouldn’t cure congestion caused by local trips, and suggests the case for one here would rest on trucks, which nobody has counted.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Ask the province for an origin–destination survey of PTH 12 and 52, on weekdays and a Saturday, with truck counts. It would replace an inference from turning counts with a measurement of through traffic, as Yorkton and Brandon did.
  • Publish the provincial access plans for PTH 12 (2013) and PTH 12 and 52 (2018) and their update, which shape where the highways can be accessed and whether a perimeter route was ever sketched.
  • Publish the yearly traffic count on the city website, not only in agenda packages, and keep sending it to the province, as the 2026 resolution didn’t.
  • Settle the corridor question in the development plan. Either map land for a future ring road or highway corridor as the city grows north and west, as Lloydminster did, or record a decision that the city doesn’t want one.
  • A plan for the edge roads, the one promised in 2020, naming which city streets are meant to carry cross-town traffic, so streets such as Old Tom Road and Friesen Avenue don’t become bypasses by accident.
  • Review truck routes with counts: put the route list in the by-law, check residents’ reports of trucks on McKenzie and Friesen avenues, and consider a dangerous-goods route as Portage la Prairie and Brandon have.
