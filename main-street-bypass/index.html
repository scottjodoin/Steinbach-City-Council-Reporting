Steinbach City Council · Jan 2022 – Sept 2026 · Highways PTH 12 and PTH 52

A bypass around Main Street? What council says and what the traffic study shows

Has Steinbach City Council considered a highway bypass that would take through traffic off Main Street (PTH 52) and Brandt Street (PTH 12)? What reasons has it given, does the city's own annual traffic study support them, and how have other Manitoba and Canadian cities handled provincial highways through town?

Quotes link to their moment in the official meeting videos; the traffic study, news and peer sources are linked. Last updated Oct 20, 2026.

Bottom line

  • Nobody is planning a bypass. Both highways are provincial, so a bypass would be the province's project. In 2018 the province called the idea “just a hypothetical concept”. Council has never moved, budgeted or asked for one at any meeting from 2022 to 2026.
  • Council's reason is its traffic study. In February 2026 Councillor Jac Siemens said the yearly reports show “traffic comes to Steinbach, it doesn't go through Steinbach. So a bypass Doesn't make sense to me.” No other council member has argued for or against a bypass on the record.
  • The study's numbers partly support him. In the 2024 evening peak, two-way traffic on Brandt St at the south end of the city was about 725 vehicles an hour. Mid-city on PTH 12 it was about 2,200. At Brandt St and Main St, only about 225 drivers an hour turned between PTH 52 West and PTH 12 North. That limits how much traffic can be passing through.
  • But the study can't measure through traffic. It counts turns at 20 highway intersections for a few weekday hours. It doesn't record where trips start or end, and it doesn't report truck numbers. No origin–destination survey of Steinbach was found.
  • Manitoba peers haven't built recent bypasses. Winkler, Morden and Niverville rebuilt their highways through town with the province, as Steinbach did at Loewen Blvd. Bypasses at Portage la Prairie, Brandon, Selkirk and Dauphin came with major provincial highway projects decades ago. Where Steinbach-sized cities have recently got one, such as Estevan in Saskatchewan, the province built it for heavy truck traffic. Yorkton measured its traffic with a licence-plate survey, found only 15% passing through, and put a bypass off. Steinbach has counted neither its through traffic nor its trucks.

Part 1 · The council record

Whose decision?

Steinbach's two through routes are provincial highways: PTH 12 runs north–south (PTH 12 North, then Brandt Street south of Main), and PTH 52 runs east–west (PTH 52 West, then Main Street). Manitoba Transportation and Infrastructure (MTI) owns them and runs every signal on them. A new highway around the city would be a provincial project; the city's part would be land-use planning, lobbying and possibly a cost share. For more on who controls what, see Traffic in Steinbach.

The only public bypass discussion by the province found for this report is from 2018. A highway access plan prepared by the province with the city and the RM of Hanover mentioned a perimeter highway or bypass. A provincial official said “the comment in that study is just a hypothetical concept being considered”. Asked whether anything was on the horizon, he said: “No, I don't see that happening anytime soon.” (SteinbachOnline, Mar 20, 2018). The province says that 2018 access management study of PTH 12 and PTH 52 led to a later design study for PTH 52 through Mitchell (EngageMB). Neither that study nor its September 2025 update, which the 2025 traffic study cites, is published online.

The city's 2025 development plan describes PTH 12 as “an Expressway north of PTH 52 and a Primary Arterial south of PTH 52”, and both highways as heavy-truck routes to be “preserved as major highway corridors”. The province's current five-year highway plan lists intersection improvements at PTH 12 and PTH 52 in Steinbach. It lists no bypass, realignment or twinning through the city (Manitoba 2025/26 infrastructure strategy, 2026–2030 list).

What the traffic study shows

Every year since 1997 the city has paid WSP Canada to count traffic on the two highways. The 2024 study, presented on March 18, 2025, counted 20 intersections on four weekdays in late September and early October 2024, from 3 to 6 p.m., plus a.m. counts at four sites (study, pp. 1–4). It is the main evidence council points to on the bypass question.

The 2025 study

The next study (WSP, January 2026), presented on February 17, 2026, shows the same shape. In its Table 2.1, two-way PTH 12 traffic at Hanover Rd at the south end was about 810 vehicles an hour, against about 2,330 at Park Rd. Two-way flow on PTH 12 now peaks “on the link south of the Superstore Access / Co-op Access intersection”. On PTH 52, “Traffic volumes west of Industrial Road have decreased slightly each consecutive year”. Traffic grew again overall: combined growth at all intersections was 3.3% over one year, 1.4% a year over two and 1.9% a year over five. The busiest intersection is still Brandt St at Main St, at about 3,465 vehicles an hour. The 2025 study's recommendations are about signal timing at Golfair, Park Rd and Superstore/Co-op, plus Giesbrecht and Lund Rd. Like the 2024 study, it says nothing about through traffic or a bypass (Feb 17, 2026 agenda, item 8A).

What the numbers can and can't say

A vehicle passing through Steinbach has to enter at one end of a highway and leave at another. On Brandt St, two-way traffic at the south end (about 725 an hour) is a third of the flow at Park Rd (about 2,200). So at most about 725 vehicles an hour in the evening peak could be travelling right through on PTH 12 from north to south. The real number is likely lower, because many of those trips start or end in the south of the city. Traffic also builds toward the middle of both corridors and falls off toward the ends, and westbound PTH 52 is heavier than eastbound in the evening, the pattern of people leaving jobs in Steinbach. Both fit Councillor Siemens's point that most traffic is coming to Steinbach.

The west end (about 1,800 an hour) and the north end (about 1,880) carry more traffic, and the obvious bypass route would link them. The study's turning counts help here. At Brandt St and Main St, 140 drivers an hour turned from PTH 52 West onto PTH 12 North and 85 turned the other way in 2024: 225 in all, under 7% of the 3,430 vehicles through that junction. In 2025 it was 180. Some drivers may cut through on Industrial Rd and Park Rd West instead. Even if every turn on that route were a through trip, the total stays below about 550 an hour in either year, and many of those trips are local. The 2024 study shows evening traffic heading out of the city in both directions: westbound on PTH 52 at Westland Dr (1,075) outnumbered eastbound (725), and southbound on Brandt St at Hanover Rd (485) outnumbered northbound (240).

The counts still can't follow a vehicle from one end of the city to the other. Only an origin–destination survey, which matches licence plates or tracks trips at points around the city, can. None was found for Steinbach.

Trucks

“there will be more truck traffic on Main Street. And trucks generally go south on 12 or north on 12.…generally they're after the number 1 highway, they're going south to the border.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on selling city land to a trucking company · Jan 16, 2024 · 1:31:02

Trucks are the usual case for a bypass, and the city's study doesn't report them. The province's most recent truck estimates (2019) put about 750 to 1,800 trucks a day on the PTH 12 sections around Steinbach, and about 500 to 1,235 on PTH 52 (MTI truck traffic layer). Those figures don't say how many trucks pass straight through. The city's designated truck routes are PTH 12 (Brandt St), PTH 52 (Main St), McKenzie Ave, Acres Dr and Industrial Rd (City of Steinbach), so through trucks are routed along Main St and Brandt St. In the 2024 discussion, staff confirmed that trucks serving that site would have to use PTH 52.

Their reasons for not pursuing a bypass

1“Traffic comes to Steinbach, it doesn't go through”

Councillor Siemens's argument (above) is the only stated reason on the record. A bypass only helps if a meaningful share of traffic is passing through, so the reasoning is sound in principle.

Context

The study's counts are consistent with it for north–south traffic, as shown above. But the study is a peak-hour intersection count, so it can't show how many drivers pass straight through. It doesn't count weekends, when shoppers from the region come in, or report trucks. Counts in recent years were also taken during construction, as Siemens himself noted. The claim may well be right, but the study wasn't built to prove it.

2It's the province's highway, and the province has no plans

“MTI, who is the authority who manages all the traffic signalization in Steinbach.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Feb 17, 2026 · 0:14:17
Context

Jurisdiction is real; the city can't build a provincial highway. But a city can still shape the option. It can ask the province for a corridor study, and it can keep a right-of-way open in its development plan so homes and businesses aren't built where a future route would go. Steinbach's new development plan (By-law 2244, adopted September 2025) has a general policy that “Land shall be protected for … any future highway system enhancements, such as interchanges, highway realignments, or new corridors”. But the only specific item it names is a realignment of PTH 52 at Bushfarm and Keating Rds. Its road map shows no bypass or ring-road corridor, and the words “bypass” and “perimeter” don't appear (Official Community Plan, pp. 13–16 and Reference Map 1).

3Build city connector roads and fix the intersections instead

“We've talked about that at our study sessions, and we continue to do that as part of our long-term planning process, is how do we manage our traffic flows within Steinbach.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Sept 6, 2022 · 1:18:26

“I would hate to have 3 sets of traffic lights in one block”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on possible signals at PTH 52 and Lund Rd · Mar 18, 2025 · 1:33:18
Context

This is the approach council has actually taken. It has added connector roads (Park Road West, Acres Drive), rebuilt the Loewen intersection, and lobbied for signal timing and the Giesbrecht lights. These steps serve local trips, the kind the study suggests dominate. The trade-off is that traffic moves onto residential streets such as Old Tom Rd, Loewen Blvd and Friesen Ave. Those streets don't get a traffic study unless a development affects PTH 12 or 52.

Key findings

1The bypass was dismissed, not studied

Council's only stated reason (reason 1) rests on a study that measures intersections, not trips. Its numbers make a large north–south through flow unlikely. Its turning counts suggest the west-to-north movement, the one a bypass would most likely serve, is also modest: at most about 550 vehicles an hour in the evening peak, and probably far fewer. But that's an upper limit worked out from turning counts, not a measurement. An origin–destination survey would answer the question directly, as Yorkton's did.

2Steinbach's approach matches its closest peers

The province controls the highways (reason 2), and no Manitoba city of Steinbach's size has had a bypass built recently. Winkler, Morden and Niverville, like Steinbach, are getting their highways rebuilt through town with cost-sharing. The bypassed cities, Portage la Prairie, Brandon, Selkirk and Dauphin, got them decades ago as part of larger provincial highway projects, not at a city's request.

3The real bypasses are residential streets

Choosing connector streets over a highway bypass (reason 3) has moved some traffic onto Old Tom Rd, Loewen Blvd and Friesen Ave, and residents there have complained. With the population projected to roughly double in 20 years (see Steinbach in 10 to 20 years), the city has no transportation master plan saying which streets are meant to carry that traffic.

4Elsewhere, bypasses follow trucks and crashes, and are measured first

In the Canadian cities that built one, the province planned and paid, sometimes with federal money. The trigger was usually trucks or safety rather than general congestion. Yorkton's truck route came with new canola plants and Estevan's bypass after truck traffic nearly doubled. Kenora's followed crashes on a Trans-Canada main street. Where through traffic was in doubt, the province measured it.

Yorkton is the closest match to Steinbach: a regional service centre of about 16,000 on several provincial highways. In 2016 its province, city and neighbouring RMs recorded licence plates on all seven highways into the area for 12 hours. “On average, 11,300 vehicles enter the study area on a highway each day, including 1,460 trucks. Of that number 1,700 vehicles (15%) including 475 trucks (32%) continued through the City”. The study concluded “it does not appear the through traffic demand on Highway 16 will warrant a high-speed through route” and recommended better regional connector roads instead (Yorkton Regional Transportation Study, 2018). That is Steinbach council's argument, settled with data, and it still found that a third of trucks were passing through.

In the small town of Moosomin, Saskatchewan, a two-hour provincial truck count at three points in 2016 found that “out of 83 vehicles, only 28 were through traffic”. The ministry “concluded that most Highway 8 traffic was destined for Moosomin, not passing through”. The town questioned how short the count was and asked for a fuller study (World-Spectator, Sept 7, 2016). That is the same argument Steinbach council makes, but tested by counting where vehicles went, and still disputed.

The research on effects is mixed. A 2005 study for New Jersey's transportation department reviewed earlier US and Canadian bypass studies. It found that rural bypasses “generally achieved their goal of diverting truck traffic out of downtown areas, but did not necessarily reduce overall downtown traffic volume.” It also found that “the building of a bypass road has little economic impact on the bypassed community.” One explanation it gives: once through traffic moves to the bypass, drivers who had been using parallel side routes return downtown (Rutgers Voorhees Transportation Center, 2005, executive summary and literature review). That supports council's view that a bypass wouldn't cure congestion caused by local trips. It also suggests the main case for one here would be trucks, which the city doesn't count.

Wisconsin's 1998 study of 17 bypassed communities points the same way. Origin–destination surveys in five of them found “an average of 76% of all vehicles having an origin or destination within the city or village”. In towns over 5,000 people, the old route lost only about 30% of its traffic in the long run, and “nearly all of the medium and larger communities have more traffic on their old highway route than on the bypass” (WisDOT summary). A Texas study found the reverse risk: “the better a relief route works from a traffic standpoint, the greater its adverse impact on local per capita sales” (Srinivasan & Kockelman, 2002). Florida's planning guidance says a bypass “may be appropriate where heavy truck traffic causes continuing unacceptable impacts” or “where a high percentage of trips are just passing through the community” (CUTR for Florida DOT, 2014). No Canadian or Manitoba standard sets a through-traffic share that justifies a bypass.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Ask the province for an origin–destination survey of PTH 12 and 52, on weekdays and a Saturday, with truck counts. This would replace the current inference from turning counts with an actual measure of through traffic.
  • Publish the provincial access management plan (2018) and its September 2025 update, which shape where PTH 12 and 52 can be accessed and which roads may close.
  • Publish the yearly WSP study on the city website, not only inside agenda packages, and keep sending each year's study to the province, as the 2026 resolution didn't.
  • Settle the corridor question in the development plan. Either reserve land for a future ring road or highway corridor as the city grows north and west, or record a decision that the city doesn't want one.
  • A transportation master plan that names which city streets are meant to carry cross-town traffic, so residential streets such as Old Tom Rd and Friesen Ave don't become bypasses by accident.
  • Review truck routes using counts, and act on complaints about trucks on streets that aren't truck routes.
