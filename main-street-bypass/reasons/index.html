A Main Street bypass · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons have Steinbach City Council and its staff given since 2015 for not pursuing a bypass or truck route around Main Street and Brandt Street, and does the city’s own traffic data support them?

In short

Council never debated a bypass, so its reasons come in passing: one councillor’s verdict in 2026, and staff explaining who owns the highways and who builds the roads. The first reason is the one that matters, and the city’s counts partly support it: most traffic does seem to start or end in Steinbach. But the counts were never designed to answer the question, they say nothing about trucks, and nobody has asked for data that would. The other reasons explain why council worked on the highways through town instead, and why city streets ended up doing a bypass’s job without a plan.

  • 1. Traffic comes to Steinbach, it doesn’t go through
  • 2. It’s the province’s highway, and the province has no plans
  • 3. Roads follow development
  • 4. It’s part of long-term planning
  • 5. There’s no room in the budget

1Traffic comes to Steinbach, it doesn’t go through

“One, one of the things I always look at on this report is the amount of traffic coming into Steinbach and the amount of traffic leaving Steinbach, because I always get that, why isn't Steinbach building a bypass to help with the traffic? But we see when in this, these reports, we see every year when we do these, we see traffic comes to Steinbach, it doesn't go through Steinbach. So a bypass Doesn't make sense to me.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on the 2025 traffic count · Feb 17, 2026 · 0:24:02

“The other thing is, this is always done in September or early October, this traffic study, and we're always under construction. The last quite a number of years we've been under construction at one of the intersections.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, same remarks · Feb 17, 2026 · 0:24:02

“there's a lot of vehicles coming in from the west, coming in every morning to work, and it really causes congestion in that area.”

Mayor Earl Funk, at the same meeting · Feb 17, 2026 · 0:20:30

This is the only stated reason against a bypass on the record from 2015 to 2026, and nobody responded to it. A bypass only helps if a meaningful share of traffic is passing through, so the reasoning is sound in principle. The question is whether the yearly count can show it.

Context

The count partly supports it. Traffic thins out toward the edges of the city, and few drivers turn between the two highways at Brandt and Main, the move a north-to-west through trip would make. But the count records turns at about 20 intersections for a few weekday hours in the fall. It can’t follow a vehicle across the city, doesn’t count weekends, when shoppers from the region come in, and doesn’t report trucks, the usual case for a bypass. Construction has also distorted recent counts, as Councillor Siemens said. The claim may well be right; the study wasn’t built to prove it, and no one has asked for one that is. The details are below.

What the city’s traffic counts show

Every year since 1997 the city has paid for a count of traffic on the two highways (WSP Canada in recent years). The 2024 study, presented on March 18, 2025, counted 20 intersections on four weekdays in late September and early October 2024, from 3 to 6 p.m., plus morning counts at four sites. The 2025 study

The next study (WSP, January 2026), presented on February 17, 2026, shows the same shape. Two-way PTH 12 traffic at Hanover Rd at the south end was about 810 vehicles an hour, against about 2,330 at Park Rd. On PTH 52, “Traffic volumes west of Industrial Road have decreased slightly each consecutive year”. Traffic grew again overall: 3.3% over one year, 1.4% a year over two and 1.9% a year over five. The busiest intersection is still Brandt St at Main St, at about 3,465 vehicles an hour. Like the 2024 study, it says nothing about through traffic or a bypass (Feb 17, 2026 agenda, item 8A).

What the numbers can and can’t say

A vehicle passing through Steinbach has to enter at one end of a highway and leave at another. On Brandt St, two-way traffic at the south end (about 725 an hour in 2024) is a third of the flow at Park Rd (about 2,200). So at most about 725 vehicles an hour in the evening peak could be travelling right through on PTH 12 from north to south, and probably far fewer, because many of those trips start or end in the south of the city. Traffic also builds toward the middle of both corridors and falls off toward the ends, and westbound PTH 52 is heavier than eastbound in the evening, the pattern of people leaving jobs in Steinbach. Both fit Councillor Siemens’ point.

The west end (about 1,800 an hour) and the north end (about 1,880) carry more traffic, and the obvious bypass would link them. At Brandt St and Main St, 140 drivers an hour turned from PTH 52 West onto PTH 12 North and 85 turned the other way in 2024: 225 in all, under 7% of the 3,430 vehicles through that junction. In 2025 it was 180. Some drivers may cut across on Industrial Rd and Park Road West instead; even if every turn on that route were a through trip, the total stays below about 550 an hour in either year, and many of those trips are local.

The counts also show how much construction moves traffic around, which is the caveat Councillor Siemens raised:

“One of the things that I did notice was that with Park Road being closed, Acres Drive has more than doubled in traffic.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on the 2022 traffic count · Mar 21, 2023 · 0:45:46

Trucks are the usual reason to build a bypass, and the city’s counts don’t report them. The province’s most recent truck estimates (MHTIS, 2019) put about 1,800 trucks a day on PTH 12 north of Main Street and 1,235 on PTH 52 west, but only about 755 on PTH 12 south of Main. So no more than about 755 trucks a day could be passing straight through north–south. Trucks are heaviest on the north and west legs, the pair a bypass would link, but no data says how many go from one to the other. See the truck table.

Only an origin–destination survey, which matches licence plates or tracks trips at points around the city, can settle the question. None has been done for Steinbach by the city or the province. Yorkton, Saskatchewan did one in 2016 and found 15% of vehicles, and 32% of trucks, passed through (see How Steinbach compares).

2It’s the province’s highway, and the province has no plans

“I mean, we're talking about provincial highways. It is under provincial jurisdiction, but as a municipality, we can advocate on behalf of our city, and this data makes it possible for us to do that.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, chairing, on the 2023 traffic count · Mar 5, 2024 · 0:17:31

“MTI, who is the authority who manages all the traffic signalization in Steinbach.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Feb 17, 2026 · 0:14:17

“the Highway Access Management and Transportation Plan, the one has not yet been adopted by Manitoba Infrastructure. So until it's formally adopted by Manitoba Infrastructure, the city will not be adopting it until they have adopted it.”

Lacey Gaudet, City Planner, on the access plan that mentioned a perimeter highway · Jul 17, 2018 · 0:56:06

Staff gave the same answer on trucks: signs on a provincial highway need the province and a council resolution (Adam Thiessen, July 2022). Council’s response to the highways being provincial was to advocate for specific upgrades with its own data, and to share the cost: the Loewen Boulevard and PTH 12 rebuild cost about $11.2 million, the city’s share about $6.1 million.

Context

The jurisdiction is real: PTH 12 and 52 are the only provincial roads in the city, and a bypass would be the province’s project. The province’s only public word on one was in March 2018, when an official called the perimeter highway in the new access plan “just a hypothetical concept being considered” and said he didn’t see it happening “anytime soon” (SteinbachOnline). Its 2025/26 plan lists only intersection improvements in Steinbach.

But a city can shape the option. It can ask the province for a corridor or origin–destination study, which council never did, and it can keep a route open in its development plan. Steinbach’s 2015 and 2018 plans had no corridor policy. The 2025 plan (By-law 2244) says land “shall be protected for … any future highway system enhancements, such as interchanges, highway realignments, or new corridors”, but names only a PTH 52 realignment at Bush Farm and Keating roads, and its maps show no bypass; the words “bypass” and “perimeter” appear in none of the three plans. Lloydminster’s 2016 plan, by contrast, told the city to secure a bypass right-of-way with both provinces before either had committed (peers). The 2018 access plan itself, and its later update, have never been published.

3Roads follow development

“As a rule, any road or servicing improvements is developer-driven.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on gravel Park Road West · Jun 7, 2016 · 0:14:51

“By and large, when it comes to accommodating traffic, the…it is the responsibility of the developer to develop any…generally any roads that are fronting proposed developments.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on Bush Farm Road · Mar 3, 2020 · 0:38:25

“And some of these things take time because as development comes, we let the development happen and then we can work on the road system.”

Mayor Earl Funk, at the Old Tom–Loewen hearing · Sept 6, 2022 · 0:24:23

This is the rule for the city’s own edge roads, the ones that do a bypass’s work: developers build the road that fronts their land, and the city fills gaps when it must.

Context

The result is a ring road built piecemeal and by accident. Old Tom Road was paved in 2016, and by 2019 Clearspring Greens residents said Park Road, Old Tom and Hespeler had become “a perimeter road around Steinbach.” G.H. said waiting for development to pave Bush Farm Road “seems a little bit regressive” (2020). K.P. said drivers avoiding the Loewen queue were “using Maplewood, Old Tom Road, and crossing over our yard” (2021). R.F. said the Old Tom and Loewen roads were “being used as a city bypass and speedway and not designed to handle this” and asked for a traffic study first (2022). In 2026 P.H. said Friesen Avenue “has become like a major hub for truck transport to come down, although it is not a truck route.” These are residents’ observations; no counts were taken on those streets.

When the city did act, it paid: Park Road West was finished to Industrial Road in 2023 from reserves ($1,970,097), “another way to go north,” in the mayor’s words. In 2020 the mayor said council needed “a plan for how we will mitigate these” edge roads; none has come to a public meeting.

4It’s part of long-term planning

“In regards to transportation, we note that their comments are definitely something we should be looking into, and within the future, we do want to take on a transportation plan. But that's not something we would be doing immediately.”

Lacey Gaudet, City Planner, on the province’s comments on the development plan · Jul 17, 2018 · 0:56:06

“We've talked about that at our study sessions, and we continue to do that as part of our long-term planning process, is how do we manage our traffic flows within Steinbach.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Sept 6, 2022 · 1:18:26

“But specifically to Old Tom and Lowen Boulevard, it's been talked about, it's part of our long-term planning strategies. There's not a definitive plan.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Sept 6, 2022 · 1:19:40
Context

Study sessions are closed and unrecorded, so what was discussed there can’t be checked. Eight years after the planner said a transportation plan was wanted “within the future,” the city has none (see Traffic in Steinbach). Staff also told a resident in December 2025 that traffic impact studies are required only for developments affecting PTH 12 or 52, so the edge roads and collector streets carrying cut-through traffic are not studied when homes are added along them.

Notes on the traffic data and the map
  • Traffic study. Study figures are from the 2024 Steinbach Annual Traffic Counting Study (WSP Canada, dated March 3, 2025), in the March 18, 2025 council agenda package, and the 2025 Steinbach Annual Traffic Count and Analysis Program (WSP, January 2026), in the February 17, 2026 agenda package. Page numbers are the studies’ own. The two-way flow figures were added up for this report from the studies’ Table 2.1. They are approach volumes, counted on different days, so they show the pattern, not exact traffic on each block. The 2025 Table 2.1 totals were added up from the approach columns because the PDF’s table text is scrambled.
  • Turning counts. The studies publish turning movements only as pictures (Figures 2.3 and 2.4: 2024 study pp. 8–9; 2025 study pp. 13–14). The numbers were read from those pictures and every approach was checked against Table 2.1; all match within rounding except one: in 2025 the northbound movements at Woodhaven Ave / Friesen Ave add to 585, while Table 2.1 gives 605. The map shows both. The 2025 study’s p. 14 figure is captioned “2024”, but its numbers are 2025’s. The PTH 52 West to PTH 12 North figures are upper limits worked out for this report, not WSP findings.
  • Map. Highway lines come from Manitoba Transportation and Infrastructure’s highway centreline layer; intersection positions from OpenStreetMap streets. North Gate Dr isn’t named in OpenStreetMap, so it was placed using the spacing on the study’s own map, and the Superstore/Co-op access was placed between the two stores. Background map © OpenStreetMap contributors; map library Leaflet.
  • Truck estimates. MHTIS 2019 annual average daily truck traffic, by highway section, from the Manitoba Highway Traffic Information System layer (hosted by the University of Manitoba). These are estimates from short counts with assumed truck shares, and the sections are long.
