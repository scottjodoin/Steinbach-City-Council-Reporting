Serving Steinbach well: an action plan for any council candidate

You want to earn residents' trust and serve them well if you're elected to Steinbach City Council. Based on what the public record shows, what concrete steps should you commit to and carry out?

Part 1 starts with how council deliberates and how much of its work happens out of public view (the Council deliberation and Behind closed doors? reports). Part 2 covers the process and policy gaps in the other reports. Each action says what the gap is, what to do, who has to agree, when it can happen, and how residents can check that it was done. It is written for any candidate, new or sitting, and endorses no one. Prepared Oct 14, 2026.

How to read the marks

  • You: things you control yourself: what you say, what you disclose, what you ask in open council.
  • Motion: you can move it, but it needs a seconder and a council majority: a by-law, budget line or policy.
  • Province/other: it needs the province, the federal government, a neighbouring municipality or a partner organization.
  • When: Campaign (before election day), First 100 days, Year 1, or Term.

Before you use any of this: what the record does and doesn't say

These limits protect your credibility. Opponents and reporters will check your claims against the same reports.

  1. There is no evidence that developer votes are arranged in advance. All three vote tests in Behind closed doors? point the other way:
    • Development is where council disagrees most: 26 of the 29 split votes and failed motions involved development.
    • When residents objected to a developer's application, a median of 3 councillors explained their vote.
    • Every time council overruled its staff, it debated the matter in public.
    Don't claim “backroom deals”. The real issue is process and openness, not corruption (Finding 1).
  2. The deliberation scores are provisional. They were coded by AI and no human has reviewed them. Sitting councillors haven't yet had a chance to correct errors, and 77 speech turns couldn't be tied to one person. Use the council-wide patterns. Don't use individual councillors' scores against them (limits).
  3. The legal points are a plain reading of the Municipal Act, not a ruling. No court or Ombudsman has ruled on Steinbach's practice. Say “appears inconsistent with the Act” and “should be brought into line”, not “illegal”.
  4. Credit what already works:
    • Every regular meeting is public and on YouTube.
    • Recorded votes and failed motions are minuted.
    • Agendas are posted 96 hours ahead.
    • Councillors mention committee discussions openly.
    • The mayor disclosed a meeting with a developer on the record.
    Residents tend to trust candidates who acknowledge what's good.

Part 1 · Open, reasoned decision-making

What the record shows

From Council deliberation, 527 substantive decisions:

  • On a typical item, about 2 of the 6 councillors said anything on the merits.
  • On 35% of items only one councillor spoke, and on 5% none did.
  • On 11% of items the only public reason was the staff report.
  • Budget items (77%) and governance items (71%) were the least often explained in public.
  • Every councillor scored lowest on intellectual flexibility (1.5–2.2 out of 4). Councillors rarely said what would change their view or admitted uncertainty.
  • When residents or delegations took part, more councillors spoke: 2.7 per item, against 1.7 without public input.

From Behind closed doors?:

  • Much of the discussion happens elsewhere: at the Strategic Priorities Committee (SPC, the whole council sitting as a committee), at multi-day budget study sessions and at “committee meetings”. These came up in 30 of 103 meetings.
  • None of these sessions has a public notice, agenda or minutes.
  • By-law 1809 closes every study session automatically. The Municipal Act requires committee meetings to be public, unless closed for a listed reason that is recorded in the minutes.
  • None of 113 sets of minutes records a resolution to close a meeting.
  • Staff have described items as “generally agreed to by council” at committee.
  • A $200,000 decision was deferred to SPC, and no public resolution was found afterwards.
  • Five major agreements reached council on documents delivered that same day.

How to win these votes: frame them as bringing Steinbach into line with the Act and with what Selkirk, Brandon and the RM of Ste. Anne already do. Don't frame them as a criticism of colleagues. Several sitting councillors have already argued for more openness in public. Councillor Susan Penner, for example, asked that a study-session item be shown in the grants line “so we have a clear idea how much that is costing” (Reason 2).

C. How you and residents will know it worked (by the end of year 1)

  • Every SPC and study session has a posted agenda and minutes.
  • Every closed session appears in the minutes with its legal ground.
  • Budget deliberations are on video.
  • No major agreement is decided on same-day documents without a stated reason.
  • On the deliberation index, the share of items where three or more councillors speak rises from 25%, and the share where the only reason is the staff report falls from 11%. Offer to have your own record scored, with human review, after year 1.

Part 2 · Process improvements that run through every report

The same five process problems appear in almost every report. Fixing them helps on every topic at once.

  1. Plans without owners, dates or costs.
    • The wellbeing plan, the community safety plan and the climate plan (presented Aug 2026, not adopted) all lack owners, timelines or costs.
    • The recreation master plan was promised in 2019 and never adopted.
    • There is no strategic plan with targets.
    Action (Motion, Year 1): adopt a council strategic plan with measurable targets and quarterly public progress reports, as Brandon does (“On Track”/“At Risk”). Give every existing plan an owner, a date and a cost (future planning).
  2. Results that aren't published. None of these is reported publicly:
    • police clearance rates
    • by-law enforcement counts
    • permit processing times
    • drainage complaint outcomes
    • suite-safety inspections
    • results of the private security patrol
    • traffic counts
    Action (You, First 100 days): request a yearly public report on each, then (Motion) make them a standing schedule. This costs almost nothing and makes every later debate fact-based.
  3. Residents hear late and see little.
    • The first notice is the hearing notice: there is no early letter and no sign on the property.
    • Developers' studies aren't posted.
    • There was no “what we heard” report after the plan review.
    • There is no public participation policy.
    Action (Motion, Year 1): see the public notice item in Part 3.
  4. Development terms are settled out of sight. Council approves subdivisions “subject to a development agreement” whose terms aren't published. Action (You, starting immediately): on each subdivision, ask that the main terms be read into the record. Then (Motion) require them in the report council votes on, as Brandon does.
  5. Outside money left on the table.
    • No Housing Accelerator Fund application.
    • No housing needs assessment, which is the entry ticket for most federal and provincial housing money.
    • No application to the federal Rural Transit Solutions Fund.
    • Active transportation projects are unfunded.
    Action (Motion, Year 1): a standing grants-pursuit list reviewed each budget.

Part 4 · A practical sequence

Campaign (now to election day, Wednesday, Oct 28, 2026)

  • Make 4–5 specific, checkable pledges rather than broad promises. Suggested set:
    1. I will explain my vote on every decision (A1, A2).
    2. I will disclose any meeting with a developer (A4).
    3. I will move to open study sessions, committee meetings and budget deliberations to the public (B1–B3).
    4. I will report back after every meeting (A8).
    5. One topic pledge from Part 3 that matters most to your neighbourhood.
  • When residents raise an issue, point them to the relevant report and timestamp. It shows you've done the homework and it keeps claims accurate.
  • Speak about colleagues and opponents with the same fairness the reports use. Credit good practice by name.

First 100 days

  • From the first meeting: A1–A9.
  • Ask the city solicitor for an opinion on By-law 1809, s. 12.1 against Municipal Act s. 152. Then bring B1, B2 and B4 together as one “open meetings” package.
  • Ask that the November 2026 budget study sessions be held in public (B3). If that isn't possible this year, ask for a public summary of each decision.
  • Request the yearly reports (Part 2, item 2) and the 2026–27 policing priorities with targets.
  • Before winter: the homelessness extreme-cold protocol.

Year 1

  • Strategic plan with targets and quarterly reporting.
  • Housing needs assessment, public notice reforms, and development-agreement terms published with subdivision reports.
  • Budget: sewer subsidy, coordinator, grants-pursuit list.
  • Year-end: publish your own scorecard of these pledges: done, in progress, or failed and why.

Rest of the term

  • Wastewater funding and servicing plan.
  • Transportation master plan with the province.
  • Recreation master plan, community standards by-law, and economic development model.

Sources

All findings come from the Steinbach City Council Reporting site. Each report links every quote to its moment in the meeting video and lists its own unverified items under “About this page”. Check those before quoting a figure in campaign material.

Notable unverified or qualified items:

  • Whether an SPC or special meeting was held on Aug 11, 2026, and what it decided.
  • Whether Steinbach posts signs on application sites.
  • Steinbach's annual RCMP cost, which is estimated from invoices.
  • Winkler's and Morden's policing costs and meeting practices.
  • Whether the federal Rural Transit Solutions Fund is open in Oct 2026.
  • The By-law 2282 (Aug 2026) text, which the property standards report didn't review.
