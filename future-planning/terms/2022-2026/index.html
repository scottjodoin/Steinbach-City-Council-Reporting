Planning for the future · By term

2022–2026: Mayor Earl Funk’s second council

What long-term decisions did Steinbach City Council make from November 2022 to October 2026, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • A longer horizon on paper. The mayor opened the term calling for a 20–30-year plan. The new Official Community Plan (September 2025) has a 25–30-year vision and a projection of 34,085 residents by 2041, and a new zoning by-law followed in January 2026. Neither the plan nor the term’s four priorities has measurable targets.
  • Wastewater moved to the front. Sewer capacity works began in 2023. In April 2025 council chose its own treatment plant over the regional co-op and applied for half of a $132.5 million project. By May 2026 the cost had passed $200 million, with no funding announced.
  • Growth paid less than staff advised. Development fees rose 5% a year, not the 7.5% staff recommended, though members said even 7.5% would not cover the cost of growth (5–2).
  • Plans tied to grants and storms. The asset management policy (2023) was a grant condition; the climate plan was grant-funded after the 2024 storm, presented in August 2026 and not adopted. A first active transportation plan was accepted “in principle.”
  • The edges defended, the region kept at arm’s length. Council objected to a Hanover rezoning (2024) and La Broquerie’s plan (2026) to keep land for annexation, joined a regional lobby committee, and ended regional library talks.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert. All six councillors and the mayor were re-elected in 2022.

What happened

2022–2023: a call for a longer view

At the first meeting of the term, with all members returned, the mayor asked council to plan further ahead:

“I think we need to be looking 20, 30 years into the future and we need to start looking and setting up the councils that are going to come before us with capital plans, with, with, and, and projects and, and infrastructure repair.”

Mayor Earl Funk, at the first meeting of the term · Nov 1, 2022 · 0:02:41

No 20–30-year plan followed; the term’s priorities were set in unrecorded sessions. Council upgraded Lift Station 2 so east-side subdivisions could go ahead, and adopted an asset management policy in October 2023, which staff said was a condition of a federal grant. On development fees, staff recommended 7.5% a year; council chose 5%, with Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert against:

“But when the city grows, there are unique costs that come with that. So for example, expansion of the lagoon, potential of having to have mechanical treatment for wastewater eventually, the eventuality of a second fire station.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:08:47

2024: density, the edges and a storm

A Holm Street infill passed 4–3. Councillor Damian Penner made the case for density, then voted against with the neighbours:

“We are looking at our official community plan right now in depth, and the 20-year projection is we need to double our population. If we double our population, our population outwards, the city will fail. It's just that simple. We cannot double our population sprawling outwards.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:43:10

Council asked the province to fund half the design of a second sewer line to the lagoon, the first wastewater capacity work on the lagoon route. A September storm dropped about six inches of rain and flooded drains and basements; in December council applied for a federal grant for a climate risk assessment and adaptation plan. It also set the direction of the community plan rewrite:

“So the city's official community plan provides the long-term vision and goals for the city as to how administration and council want to see the city develop over the next 25 to 30 years.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Dec 3, 2024 · 0:14:32

When the RM of Hanover proposed rezoning land at the city’s northern edge, council objected:

“And in this case here, we have to think in the long term. We have to think about what is in the best interests of our city, and especially as the city is eventually going to continue to grow. Eventually our borders will change and will expand.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Dec 17, 2024 · 0:39:11

2025: the community plan and the wastewater application

The new Official Community Plan passed its hearing in April and third reading in September, after the minister approved it over objections, some from neighbouring municipalities. In April council ratified an application to the federal housing infrastructure fund for half of a $132.5 million wastewater project. The City Manager explained why the city would not join the Red-Seine-Rat co-op:

“the RSR co-op option was projected to be somewhere between the neighborhood of 25 to 30% greater in cost than if the city were to proceed independently. Secondly, the RSR co-op model would have required the city to cede ownership of portions of its existing wastewater treatment facility lands”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Apr 15, 2025 · 0:15:25

“we as a council are looking forward now, even though we've got, you know, another, you know, 5 years of growth left, that we're looking at this now.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Apr 15, 2025 · 0:24:27

Council accepted its first active transportation plan in principle, funded the climate plan, and asked the province to raise Manitoba Water Services Board funding to $100 million a year:

“It is not feasible for municipalities and smaller cities to be able to afford that magnitude of project. And these projects need to be done because if you can't get them done, your growth literally will stop. You cannot build what you cannot service.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Oct 7, 2025 · 0:30:40

2026: costs rise, the term ends

The zoning by-law took effect in January. Council awarded contracts for a new force main, a larger sewer main and a Lift Station 1 upgrade that nearly doubles its pumping capacity, and ended regional library talks. In May, Councillor Susan Penner gave a new figure for the treatment plant:

“The new water treatment facility Steinbach is planning will be the most significant investment the city has ever made. It's exactly the kind of project where costs can escalate quickly based on regulatory requirements. Projected costs of this project have skyrocketed to over $200 million.”

Councillor Susan Penner, opening remarks; she means the wastewater facility · May 5, 2026 · 0:00:12

The climate plan was presented on August 18, about four months late, as a planning document; it was not adopted. In September council objected to La Broquerie’s plan, which would drop the transitional area at the city’s edge:

“we are going through a time of unprecedented growth, and we don't know when it's going to end or if it's ever going to end. And so we need to make sure that we are saving land for future development. And if one— 'cause once land is developed, it's gone. It can't be developed twice.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 15, 2026 · 0:49:15
