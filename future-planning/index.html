Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Planning for the future

Steinbach in 10 to 20 years: what has council planned?

What long-term decisions has Steinbach City Council made since December 2015 to steer the city over the next 10 to 20 years, on growth, land and annexation, water and wastewater, neighbouring municipalities, long-range capital and climate? What reasons has it given for not planning further ahead, how did each council member act, and how do other fast-growing municipalities plan for that horizon?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026.

Executive summary

Across three councils, Steinbach secured land and water ahead of growth, and its planning horizon lengthened from five years to thirty. Its biggest long-range decision, wastewater, is still unfunded, and much of its planning remains unpublished.

  • The planning horizon grew, on paper. In 2018 a councillor called the new community plan “specifically a 5-year planning document.” The 2025 Official Community Plan sets a 25–30-year vision and projects 34,085 residents by 2041, up from 17,806 in 2021. It has no measurable targets, and council has never published a strategic plan: its priorities are set in unrecorded sessions.
  • Land and water came first, and mostly worked. About 2,800 acres annexed from the RM of Hanover in 2018, a second water supply and treatment plant (about $11 million, roughly half from other governments) and 25 years of downtown land buying that became the event centre site were all put in place before they were needed.
  • Wastewater was flagged in 2018 and is now the decision that will shape the next 20 years. The mayor called the lagoon the “next major hurdle” in February 2018; the first capacity works came in 2023–2026. The treatment project was $132.5 million in the April 2025 funding application (city share at least $65.4 million) and “over $200 million” by May 2026. No funding has been announced.
  • Plans arrive one project at a time, and neighbours are handled by objection. Long-range capital plans are published only as yearly totals; the asset management policy (2023) was a grant condition; a climate plan presented in August 2026 has not been adopted. Council filed at least seven objections to neighbours’ plans and rezonings, opposed provincial planning regions, and chose its own wastewater plant over the regional co-op.
  • Members agreed on the big decisions and split on land use and who pays. Every recorded vote on water, wastewater, neighbours’ plans and the two community plans was unanimous. Splits came on infill (First Street 4–3 in 2019, Holm Street 4–3 in 2024), industrial land, and development fees kept below staff advice (5–2 in 2023). Jac Siemens was the steadiest voice for plan-led land use; Michael Zwaagstra explained most long-range decisions. Both left council in 2026.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council annexed land, built a second water supply and adopted a five-year community plan, while recreation megaprojects fell through. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council treated water as settled, guarded local control and planned site by site. His second council rewrote the community plan for a 25–30-year horizon and turned to wastewater and storms. The council elected in October 2026 inherits the wastewater bill.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents and delegations are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts (“Arm of Hanover” means the RM of Hanover). The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence. Speakers were matched to names with AI assistance, from how they were addressed in each meeting and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations and other members of the public are identified only by initials. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain. Where we trim a remark, leave out a personal passage, or identify a speaker by initials, the full remarks can be watched at the linked moment in the official meeting video.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist; meetings in late 2019 and some in 2024–2025 that have minutes but no transcript; and the April 5, 2022 meeting, which has no audio. Strategic Priorities Committee meetings and council study sessions, where priorities are set, are closed and unrecorded. The August 18, 2026 climate plan presentation is known from the minutes and news coverage.
  • What counts. 136 items: strategic priorities, community plans and zoning by-laws, growth figures, annexation and land, neighbours’ plans and regional bodies, water and wastewater capacity, long-range capital and asset management, transportation and active transportation, and climate and drainage. Individual rezonings count only where council debated long-term growth through them. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so who voted which way is taken from what members said. The April 2016 multiplex vote (5–2; the video ends before the vote) and the December 2016 arts-centre vote (4–3) counts and opponents are from SteinbachOnline’s reports; the December 2017 golf-club land vote (about 4–3) is inferred from members’ stated positions.
  • Figures. Population figures differ by source, and we show where they come from: Statistics Canada censuses (16,022 in 2016, revised; 17,806 in 2021), the Official Community Plan projection (34,085 by 2041), and figures members cited (20,011 or 22,000 today; 36,936 by 2045 or 44,000 by 2046). The $132.5 million wastewater application and the city’s share of at least $65.4 million are from the April 15, 2025 minutes. “Over $200 million” is Councillor Susan Penner’s statement (May 5, 2026); the $215 million total and a city share of about $72 million are the mayor’s figures as reported by SteinbachOnline (September 2026), not presented at a recorded meeting. The City Manager dated the last lagoon expansion to 2007; other sources say 2009–2010.
  • Not confirmed. The outcome of the 2025 federal wastewater application (no award found; Steinbach was not among the November 2025 provincial-stream recipients); whether the engineers’ report on long-term water supply requested in January 2022 was ever prepared; the outcomes of the 2017 and 2024–2025 Municipal Board hearings on neighbours’ plans; whether the August 2026 climate plan has been adopted or published; and the city’s water rights licence limit, which is not published.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
