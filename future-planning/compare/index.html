Planning for the future · Part 2

How Steinbach compares

How do other fast-growing and comparable municipalities plan 10 to 20 years ahead, and where does Steinbach match, lead or fall short of them?

In short

Steinbach is strong where it acted early: it has land for decades of growth and a second water supply. It is on par with peers in having a modern community plan. It falls short in what it publishes and shares: no strategic plan with targets, capital plans shown only as yearly totals, a climate plan not adopted, and no joint plan with its neighbours. Its wastewater project is where the comparison matters most: Selkirk built ahead of growth, Winkler shared with its neighbour, and Morden ran out and stopped issuing permits.

Key findings

1Land and water were secured ahead of need

This is Steinbach’s strongest record. The 2018 annexation and the second water supply were both in place before growth required them, and decades of downtown land buying made the event centre possible. Both relied on partner money and patience (reason 5), and the annexation was smaller than requested. Niverville’s experience shows the province now expects a land case of about 25 years.

2One project will define the next 20 years

Every growth path runs through wastewater. The mayor flagged the lagoon in 2018, the City Manager then gave it “a dozen to 20 years” from 2007, and the first capacity works came in 2023. The cost has risen from $132.5 million to over $200 million, the federal application has no reported result, and financing it will likely break the five-year debt rule (reason 4, reason 5). Peers show the range of outcomes: Selkirk built ahead, Winkler shared with its neighbour, and Morden, which ran out, stopped accepting building-permit applications in 2023 and in 2025 switched to a cheaper design after its plant’s cost outran the funding.

3The horizon lengthened; the measures didn’t

The community plan went from “specifically a 5-year planning document” (2018) to a 25–30-year vision (2025), but it has no targets, and council members still cite different growth numbers (reason 1, reason 3). Brandon ties growth phases to its capital plan and commits to reporting indicators; Steinbach’s own 2014 growth study is not mentioned in the council record.

4Steinbach plans more than it publishes

Staff keep a 7–10-year capital look-ahead, members speak of 10- and 20-year capital plans, and priorities are set each term (reason 2). Residents see none of it: four yearly capital totals, no strategic plan, an unpublished asset management plan and an engineers’ report that never came back. A resident asked at the 2025 budget hearing how the city knows it isn’t falling behind. Brandon, Morden and Martensville publish plans; Brandon reports on its plan every quarter.

5Neighbours are handled by objection, not agreement

Steinbach has good reasons to protect its edges, and the regional wastewater co-op was costlier (reason 8). But objections are decided case by case by others, and the joint action committees produce nothing public. Southern Manitoba has a working model: Winkler, Morden and two RMs share one development plan, and Winkler and Stanley share a wastewater system.

6Climate and drainage planning arrived through grants and storms

The asset management policy (2023) and the climate plan (2024 application) came when federal grants required or paid for them, and members framed climate planning as federal “downloading” (reason 6). After the 2024 and 2025 storms the urgency changed, but the climate plan is still not adopted. Brandon and Selkirk adopted theirs years earlier and report on them.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • A published wastewater funding and servicing plan: the capacity date, the city’s share, how it will be financed (including any exception to the five-year rule), and a fallback if federal money does not come.
  • A council strategic plan with targets and public progress reports, as Brandon, Morden and Martensville publish, set early in the 2026–2030 term.
  • One official population forecast, with capital and servicing phased to it, as Brandon’s growth strategy does.
  • An itemized 10-year capital plan and a yearly asset management report, as the 2023 policy already assigns to administration.
  • Adoption of the climate plan, with funded actions for its first phase (2026–28) and a drainage master plan.
  • The long-term water supply report requested in 2022, sized for the 40,000–45,000 people the wastewater plant is being planned for, and the city’s withdrawal limit made public.
  • Joint fringe-area plans with the RMs of Hanover and La Broquerie, with joint action committees that report in public, in place of case-by-case objections.
  • Indicators in the community plan when it is reviewed by 2030, so residents can see whether growth is following the plan.
