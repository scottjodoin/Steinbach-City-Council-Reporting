Housing and development · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council’s approach to housing, infill and paying for growth change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

The approach stayed much the same across all three councils: approve what developers propose in new neighbourhoods, decide infill one application at a time under an administrative infill policy, make developers pay for servicing, and leave affordable housing to non-profits and other governments. What changed was the scale. Building went from 50 new homes in 2016 to more than 300 a year from 2024, mostly apartments; rents rose and vacancy stayed low; and the cost of growth, above all wastewater, outgrew the fees that pay for it. The 2025 community plan lists new housing tools, but none has been put in place.

Downtown apartments and seniors’ housing approved, parking and setback rules held, a seniors’ incentive that lapsed, and an early warning about the lagoon.

Apartments in established neighbourhoods

Infill is where council most often divided. It approved more than 20 apartment projects on lots that had held houses, and refused 7. Most refusals came where a site sat mid-block in a single-family area; most approvals were on main roads, near downtown or beside existing apartments. The line between the two was drawn case by case, under an infill policy that staff described in 2016 as “administrative in nature” and not of the same weight as the zoning by-law.

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, who moved more housing files than anyone and led the 2023 fee decision, and Jac Siemens, the most frequent lone vote against particular infill sites, did not run. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), and Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association in his first elected office, have no record on this topic yet. Susan Penner, council’s most consistent vote for density and affordability exceptions, topped the poll and is Deputy Mayor.

What the new council inherits:

  • Development fees. By-law 2233 sets fees only to the end of 2027 ($4,727 a house that year). The next council decides what new homes pay from 2028, after members of the last council agreed that fees do not cover the cost of growth.
  • Housing tools on paper. The 2025 Official Community Plan says the city “should consider” fee relief and less parking for affordable housing, city land for housing, faster approvals for rental and affordable projects, and more density near downtown and main roads. None has been adopted.
  • The wastewater expansion. About $132 million on the 2025 estimate, said in May 2026 to exceed $200 million, with no federal decision reported. Council has said the goal is never to stop issuing building permits.
  • Infill without a map. Residents at the 2026 hearings asked whether the city has a plan for where density goes. Large proposals are pending, including a six-storey, 100-unit rezoning on First Street (first reading 2024) and 153 lots at Williams Creek awaiting third reading.
  • Seniors’ housing. The “Rooted in Community” task force set up in October 2026 has not reported, and The Bridge on Third is seeking provincial and federal funding with the city’s letter of support.

The new council’s inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026 had no housing items.
