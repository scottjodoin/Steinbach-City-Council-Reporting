Housing and development · By term

2022–2026: Mayor Earl Funk’s second council

How did Steinbach City Council handle housing, infill and the cost of growth from November 2022 to October 2026, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • Record building, mostly apartments. Permits reached a record 344 new homes in 2024 and 365 in 2025, about three-quarters of them multi-family. Council approved every new subdivision and most apartment rezonings, often over petitions.
  • Fees kept low for affordability. In 2023 council chose 5% yearly fee increases for 2024–2027 instead of staff’s 7.5% (5–2), while agreeing that neither would cover the cost of growth. In 2025 a councillor said “growth pays for itself is not true”.
  • Split on which streets are ready. Home Street passed 4–3; Third Street, 189 Hanover and 330 McKenzie were denied as neighbourhoods “not ready to transition”, the last two against staff advice.
  • Parking became the most divided issue. Council granted less parking for studios (5–2), a seniors’ building (5–2) and Bridgepark (6–1), and cut downtown parking by a third in the new zoning by-law, but kept 1.5 stalls per apartment elsewhere.
  • Affordable housing: policies, not programs. The new community plan lists tools council “should consider”; none was adopted. The city endorsed The Bridge on Third with a letter, and no needs assessment or federal housing application came to council.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert (the same six re-elected in 2022).

What happened

2023: fees below staff’s advice, and a denial on Third Street

Fees had risen 2.5% a year for about a decade while construction costs rose faster. Staff proposed 7.5% a year for 2024–2027. Councillor Zwaagstra moved 5% instead, and it passed 5–2, with Councillors Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert against at every reading:

“basically the opposition here is that we’re making it too affordable to build homes and develop within Steinbach. And I prefer to err on the side of affordability.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Nov 7, 2023 · 0:11:52

“A lot has been said about affordability, and the reality is if you’re a citizen of Steinbach, you’re either paying for it upfront on the development fees or you’re going to be paying it through the mill rate.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:14:48

Under By-law 2233 a single-family home pays $4,083 in 2024 rising to $4,727 in 2027, and an apartment unit $3,112 in 2025. Water and sewer impact fees apply only to non-residential development. In August council unanimously denied an apartment building on Third Street after a 40-plus-name petition, saying the site lay outside the transitional zone and refusing the developer’s mid-hearing request to adjourn and consult:

“public hearings do matter. It actually does make a difference when people come to these hearings.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Aug 1, 2023 · 1:08:42

Council also borrowed $2 million to upgrade a lift station so east-side subdivisions could be serviced.

2024: Home Street, 4–3

The longest hearing of the term was on 32 apartments at 268/274 Home Street, near the hospital. A 43-name petition and many neighbours opposed it; others spoke for it. Residents put affordability on both sides:

“In terms of affordable housing, I am a homeowner today because of this neighbourhood.”

E.D., resident, against the rezoning · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:13:01

“I believe this notion that this area is affordable, it’s no longer affordable. … So renting is my only opportunity”

C.S., resident, for the rezoning · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:16:15

Council split 4–3. Mayor Funk and Councillors Siemens, Susan Penner and Bill Hiebert voted for it; Councillors Zwaagstra, Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert against:

“If we double our population, our population outwards, the city will fail. It’s just that simple. We cannot double our population sprawling outwards. Having said that, there is also a lot of value of having young families in affordable single-family housing within accessibility to the downtown.”

Councillor Damian Penner, voting against · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:43:10

“But please don’t fear multifamily. You can live harmoniously with multifamily and single-family dwellings. It does work.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:49:17

Later that year council approved a six-storey building on Main Street and backed enforcement of basement-suite safety rules, though Councillor Siemens warned of “a loss of many low-end rental units”. In December it accepted a review of the community plan and zoning by-law:

“one of the recommended changes as it pertains to land use includes adding policies on affordable housing. And this is something we want to be proactive about and has changed significantly since 5 years ago, given how much costs have rapidly been rising over the past few years.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Dec 3, 2024 · 0:14:32

2025: a new plan, “growth pays for itself is not true”, and studios

The new Official Community Plan (By-law 2244, third reading September 16, 2025) says the city “should consider” affordable-housing tools: more density near downtown and main roads, city land for housing, faster approvals for rental and affordable projects, reducing or waiving fees and parking for affordable housing, and easier secondary suites. None had been adopted by November 2026. At the 2025 budget, a councillor said growth costs the city more than it brings:

“And so what this does tell us is that the old maxim that growth pays for itself is not true, because obviously if the city was not growing, we wouldn’t be having to do a lot of these things.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Mar 4, 2025 · 0:31:04

Council applied for federal help with half of a roughly $132 million wastewater expansion, so the city would never have to stop issuing building permits. It denied a sixplex at 189 Hanover Street that staff had recommended:

“But I think you’re 10 years too early.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Mar 18, 2025 · 0:36:35

In November it approved 36 studio apartments under 500 sq ft on Main Street, aimed at rents under $1,000, with 45 parking stalls instead of 59 (5–2, Councillors Zwaagstra and Jake Hiebert against):

“Requiring what we do for, again, bigger units for this will likely make this unaffordable and defeat exactly what we’re trying to accomplish.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Nov 18, 2025 · 0:56:39

2026: seniors’ buildings, a new zoning by-law, and another denial

The new zoning by-law (January 2026) cut downtown parking by a third and allowed detached “granny” suites, but only on rural residential lots. Council approved 48 apartments on McKenzie Avenue despite a petition, a seven-storey, 84-suite Steinbach Housing seniors’ building with one stall per suite (5–2), and a six-storey Bridgepark building (6–1). It endorsed The Bridge on Third, a new supportive-housing project, with a letter but no money:

“And that was a prime example of how something good can happen from government funding, where A need is identified, the government provides funds, the local government then decides which organization is best suited to deal with those, and we got the Bridge, which has been remarkably successful.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · May 5, 2026 · 0:19:28

At a September hearing on 12 units on Giesbrecht Street, a neighbour, T.N., asked whether the city had “a plan instead of infill”. In October council unanimously denied 20 townhouse-style units at 330 McKenzie Avenue that staff had recommended, months after approving 48 units a few blocks away:

“Now, when a neighborhood’s not ready to transition, it won’t work.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Oct 6, 2026 · 0:59:40

Council also set up the “Rooted in Community” task force on keeping seniors who await care-home beds in Steinbach.

Where members differed

This term had the most split votes on housing of the three: 19. They fell into three groups: development fees (Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert against 5%), particular infill sites (Jac Siemens most often alone), and parking (Michael Zwaagstra and Jake Hiebert, with Bill Hiebert once). Every denial of an apartment project was unanimous.
