Housing and development · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s housing supply, affordable-housing tools, use of federal housing money, zoning, parking and growth fees compare with other Manitoba cities and two fast-growing Prairie cities, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Strong on supply and mix. Steinbach is building more homes than ever, three-quarters of them multi-family, and already had a larger share of apartments in 2021 than Winkler, Morden, Niverville or Portage la Prairie.
  • Behind on the tools. Brandon, Winkler and Morden have done housing needs assessments; Brandon won federal Housing Accelerator Fund money and pays incentives for affordable units; Winkler and Brandon allow garden suites in town and require less parking. Steinbach has none of these, and its 2025 community plan lists such tools only as options.
  • Low growth fees. A new house pays less in Steinbach than in Winkler, Morden or Niverville, and nothing separately for water and sewer, while the wastewater bill growth brings keeps rising.

Council approved most of what builders proposed, and building reached records. But rents kept rising: the average two-bedroom rose about 32% from 2015 to 2025 and the average bachelor about 56%. In the 2021 census, 21.7% of Steinbach households spent 30% or more of their income on shelter, the highest of the Manitoba cities compared (Winkler 21.0%, Selkirk 18.8%, Portage la Prairie 16.9%, Morden 16.4%, Brandon 16.3%, Niverville 16.2%), and 38.5% of renters did. Supply alone, the approach council relies on, has not closed that gap. See council’s reason 3.

2The community plan names the tools; none has been adopted

The new Official Community Plan (By-law 2244, adopted September 16, 2025) says the city “should consider strategies to promote the creation and retention of affordable housing”. Its list includes more density and height, mixed-use and high-density housing as of right near downtown and along main roads, city land for housing, faster approvals for rental and affordable projects, “reviewing development charges and fee schedules with consideration for waiving or reducing fees for affordable housing”, less or no parking, easier secondary suites, and pre-approved plans for “missing middle” homes. As of November 2026 we found none adopted as a by-law or program. The zoning by-law that followed in January 2026 cut parking downtown but kept the old defaults elsewhere. See reason 4.

3No needs assessment, no federal application

Steinbach’s affordable-housing record rests on one provincial grant, passed to Steinbach Community Outreach in 2021–22. That model worked, but it depends on someone else offering money. Peers that did their own groundwork reached more of it: Brandon, with a 2023 needs assessment and a plan to allow fourplexes, cut parking and add incentives, won $6.25 million from the Housing Accelerator Fund, and three smaller Manitoba municipalities (the RMs of Ritchot and Lorne and St-Pierre-Jolys) shared $4.05 million. Winkler and Morden applied and were not funded, which shows an application is no guarantee. Red Deer, which won $12 million but then refused the four-units-as-of-right condition, had not returned the money as of January 2026 and was seeking a meeting with the federal minister. See reason 4.

4The rules lag the votes

Council regularly approves apartments, height and less parking through one-off variances, and decides which streets are “ready” at each hearing. The results are hard to predict: 330 McKenzie was denied in October 2026, months after 48 units were approved a few blocks away, and both 189 Hanover and 330 McKenzie were denied against staff advice. Writing the exceptions council already grants into the zoning by-law (as it did for downtown parking), and mapping where apartments are expected, would make outcomes predictable for neighbours and builders. See reasons 1 and 6.

5Growth’s biggest bill isn’t in the fee

Council members said in 2023 that even staff’s 7.5% a year would not cover growth, and in 2025 that “growth pays for itself is not true”. Yet the 2024–2027 fees rise 5% a year and include no residential charge for water or sewer, while the wastewater expansion growth requires was put at about $132 million in 2025 and over $200 million in 2026. Niverville and Morden charge new homes more than twice as much, much of it for wastewater. The fee by-law expires after 2027; a study of what growth costs would let the new council decide openly how much new homes pay and how much existing taxpayers carry. See reasons 5 and 7.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Commission a housing needs assessment, as Winkler and Brandon did, so the city knows how many and what kind of homes it needs, and can apply for programs that ask for one.
  • Turn the community plan’s list into decisions: adopt or reject each affordable-housing tool it says council “should consider”, in public, with a date.
  • Adopt an affordable-housing incentive by-law (a grant offsetting development fees, a tax rebate for new affordable units, city land where it has some), as Brandon did, so non-profit projects like The Bridge on Third can count on local support.
  • Map where apartments are expected and write the parking and height relief council already grants into the zoning by-law, so hearings are about design rather than whether a street is “ready”.
  • Consider gentle density: three or four units on corner lots or lots with a lane, and detached suites on urban lots, as Brandon and Winkler allow.
  • Base the post-2027 development fees on a growth cost study, including whether new homes should pay toward wastewater as they do in Niverville, Morden and Winkler.
  • Apply for any future federal housing round and report the outcome of the 2025 wastewater application in public.
