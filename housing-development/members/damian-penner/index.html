Damian Penner: housing, apartments, infill and paying for growth

What did Damian Penner say and do about housing and development in Steinbach (affordable housing, apartments and infill, new neighbourhoods, and who pays for growth) between December 2015 and November 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor since November 2018 · re-elected 2026

In short

Damian Penner named affordability earlier and more directly than most members: one-bedroom suites 'we are desperate for' (2019), and a 2021 warning that housing had 'escaped the grasp' of some young families. He supported every greenfield subdivision, saying the market should decide the mix, and argued that the city 'cannot double our population sprawling outwards'. He moved the 48-unit McKenzie rezoning and backed reduced parking for studios and seniors and detached suites for families.

On paying for growth he was the clearest dissenter: with Jake Hiebert he voted against the 2024–2027 development fees at every reading, saying residents pay either through fees or through the mill rate. On particular infill sites he weighed neighbours heavily: he voted against the Home Street rezoning, seconded the Third Street denial ('leapfrogging' the transitional zone) and joined the unanimous 330 McKenzie denial. Re-elected in 2026.

“A lot has been said about affordability, and the reality is if you're a citizen of Steinbach, you're either paying for it upfront on the development fees or you're going to be paying it through the mill rate.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Arguing for staff's 7.5% yearly development-fee increase · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:14:48

Term by term

2018–2022

He supported 44 First Street because one-bedroom suites are something 'we are desperate for' and density reduces urban sprawl, called 126 Maplewood 'a good option for transitioning this neighborhood', and spoke against refusing 85 First Street before a public hearing. He backed every greenfield approval, citing slim lot inventory, and said 'the best thing we can generally do is move out of the way and let the market do what it dictates'. In September 2021 he said housing was becoming unattainable for young families, but tied it to federal policy and proposed nothing local. He seconded the 205 McKenzie fourplex.

Took part in 24 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

He voted against the 5% development fees at every reading, saying 'there has been subsidization into the mill rate since 2012'. He argued the city cannot double its population by sprawling, supported detached suites to help families, lower parking for studios and the seniors' building, and moved the 48-unit McKenzie rezoning ('if you're not going to put high density to be close to major intersections, I don't know where it's going to fit'). He opposed bending subdivision policy for a developer's financing. But he voted against the Home Street rezoning (though for its fourth storey), seconded the Third Street denial and joined the 330 McKenzie denial on neighbourhood character.

Took part in 39 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

A steady voice for supply and for making growth pay for itself, who treats the community plan's transitional areas as the line for infill. Inside them he has backed dense projects; outside them he has sided with neighbours.
