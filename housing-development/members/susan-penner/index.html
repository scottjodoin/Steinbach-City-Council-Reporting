Housing and development · Council member

Susan Penner: housing, apartments, infill and paying for growth

What did Susan Penner say and do about housing and development in Steinbach (affordable housing, apartments and infill, new neighbourhoods, and who pays for growth) between December 2015 and November 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor in all three terms covered · Deputy Mayor from November 2026 (topped the 2026 poll)

In short

Susan Penner cast the only recorded vote against the 2016 Hanover Street apartments, over height. From 2017 on she became council's most consistent vote for density: the only vote against denying the Maplewood townhouses (2018), for 44 First Street (2019), and the mover of Home Street, the six-storey Main Street building and the studio apartments. She argued that old rentals cannot be replaced without density and that 'not everybody can afford a house'.

She pushed exceptions that lower the cost of building: fewer stalls at The Bridge because low-income tenants own fewer cars, and fewer at the Main Street studios because standard parking 'will likely make this unaffordable'. She moved the 2024 community plan review that added affordable-housing policies, the first detached secondary suite and the endorsement of The Bridge on Third. She also backed 5% rather than 7.5% fees, said 'developers generally know best', and made transitional housing a conditional use so neighbours get a hearing. Re-elected at the top of the poll in 2026, she is now Deputy Mayor.

“Requiring what we do for, again, bigger units for this will likely make this unaffordable and defeat exactly what we're trying to accomplish.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Moving reduced parking for 36 studio apartments on Main Street · Nov 18, 2025 · 0:56:39

Term by term

2014–2018

She voted against the 2016 Hanover Street apartments: 'I think 3 stories is too high for that particular area', while saying council wanted more density downtown. From 2017 she was the most consistent pro-density vote: she welcomed affordable seniors' options at Stonebridge, moved the First Street rezoning and its shorter setback to keep downtown 'vibrant', was the only vote against denying the Maplewood townhouses, and called Lexington Village entry-level housing. Some 2016–2017 remarks by 'Councillor Penner' cannot be told apart from Cari Penner's and are not attributed.

Took part in 11 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2014–2018 story.

2018–2022

She argued that 'either we keep these old dilapidated homes, or we have to allow an increase in density' (44 First Street, 2019), welcomed new lots when inventory was low, and seconded the suite safety by-law: 'everybody has a right to safe housing'. She rebuilt the Clearspring Greens pathway levy at $1,610 a lot, the same sidewalk fee a new lot pays. She moved The Bridge's parking variance, saying lower-income tenants are less likely to own a car, said rent is costly so entry-level lots matter, and argued high density should be 'spread throughout the city'.

Took part in 25 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

She moved Home Street and its fourth storey, the six-storey Main Street building, the 2024 community plan review ('adding policies on affordable housing'), the 36 studio apartments and their parking cut, the seniors' building's height and parking, the first detached secondary suite and the endorsement of The Bridge on Third. She supported 5% fees so as not to price buyers out of the market, said 'developers generally know best where the needs are', and argued 'you cannot build what you cannot service'. She made transitional housing a conditional use so neighbours are heard, and moved the 2026 seniors' task force.

Took part in 43 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

From a lone vote against three storeys on Hanover Street in 2016 to council's leading advocate for density and for rule changes that lower the cost of affordable units. She treats developers as the main source of supply and the city's role as clearing the way, with neighbourhood hearings kept for sensitive uses.
