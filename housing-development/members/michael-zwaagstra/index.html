Housing and development · Council member

Michael Zwaagstra: housing, apartments, infill and paying for growth

What did Michael Zwaagstra say and do about housing and development in Steinbach (affordable housing, apartments and infill, new neighbourhoods, and who pays for growth) between December 2015 and November 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor in all three terms covered · did not run in 2026

In short

Michael Zwaagstra moved most of the decade's rezonings, subdivisions and fee by-laws. He defended renters and density against traffic objections ('I wasn't riffraff when I rented'), argued that medium density is the only economic way to redevelop big old lots, and stated the developer-pay rule when council refused a builder's demolition grant. He steered the whole $1.34 million provincial grant to Steinbach Community Outreach in 2021, calling it a model in which 'the government provides funds' and the city picks the organization.

He also set limits. He said council encourages infill but will not 'mandate' it against neighbourhoods, voted against the Home Street building, moved the Third Street denial and said rents are 'completely out of our purview'. In 2023 he moved the 5% yearly fee increase instead of staff's 7.5%, preferring 'to err on the side of affordability' while saying even 7.5% would not cover growth; in 2025 he said 'growth pays for itself is not true'. He voted against the reduced parking for the Main Street studio apartments (2025) and the seven-storey seniors' building (2026), though he moved the six-storey Bridgepark building with 1.25 stalls a suite. He did not run in 2026.

“basically the opposition here is that we're making it too affordable to build homes and develop within Steinbach. And I prefer to err on the side of affordability.”

Term by term

2014–2018

The most active mover on housing: the Hanover Street apartments, Bridge Park's seniors' rezoning, the Terrace on 3rd variance and the 2016 fee renewal at 2.5% a year, 'approximately the rate of inflation'. He turned neighbours' requests into conditions on rezonings, cautioned against city grants for regular 55+ housing ('we need to be targeted'), and defended the 1.5-stall parking rule on Lowen Boulevard (2016) and Chapel Drive (2017). He supported the First Street rezoning and its shorter setback but moved to deny the 44 Maplewood townhouses as too large and unclear, and said at the 2018 plan hearing that council encourages infill but is 'not going to mandate' it.

Took part in 29 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2014–2018 story.

2018–2022

He defended renters at 126 Maplewood, carried 44 First Street 4–3, argued that redevelopment of large lots is only 'economically feasible' at medium density, and stated the developer-pay rule when refusing a $10,000-a-lot demolition grant. He said rents are 'completely out of our purview' (2020), led denials on infill-policy grounds (14 Ellice, 85 First Street), and said new lots are 'not a financial windfall' but let 'our kids and grandkids' buy homes. He steered the full provincial grant to Steinbach Community Outreach and said The Bridge's reduced parking did not mean lower parking for 'other multifamily regular buildings'.

Took part in 32 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

He moved the 5% development-fee amendment in 2023, noting Steinbach's fees were below Winkler, Morden, Brandon and Niverville and that 'even 7.5% wouldn't cover the cost of growth'; in 2025 he said 'the old maxim that growth pays for itself is not true'. He supported density in designated areas (330 First Street, Main Street, 205 McKenzie beside a school) but voted against Home Street ('density in general, in theory, is good. But so are single-family homes') and moved the Third Street denial. He voted against the studio apartments' parking cut, Home Street's fourth storey, a duplex setback and the seniors' building's one-stall-per-suite parking, and moved the 2025 federal wastewater application.

Took part in 46 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

Consistent throughout: more housing is good and new development should pay, but neighbourhoods and the parking by-law should not be overridden lightly, and affordable housing is funded by senior governments. His fee position, affordability first with the gap left on the mill rate, shaped the 2024–2027 fees.
