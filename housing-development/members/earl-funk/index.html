Housing and development · Council member

Earl Funk: housing, apartments, infill and paying for growth

What did Earl Funk say and do about housing and development in Steinbach (affordable housing, apartments and infill, new neighbourhoods, and who pays for growth) between December 2015 and November 2026, and how did that change over time?

Councillor 2010–2018 · Mayor since November 2018 (re-elected by acclamation in 2026)

In short

As a councillor, Earl Funk said little on housing in this record. He welcomed a seniors' building because he lived beside two like it, voted for a shorter setback on First Street townhouses so tenants would have green space, and moved one of the 2018 purchases of downtown houses, saying the lots could later hold multi-family housing. He voted to deny the Maplewood townhouses in 2018, citing distrust of the developer and drainage.

As mayor since November 2018 he has chaired every housing decision and supported nearly every subdivision and most apartment rezonings, often citing his own years living beside apartments: 'You can live harmoniously with multifamily.' He voted for the 32-unit Home Street building when council split 4–3. But he voted against a fourplex on McKenzie Avenue in 2022 and joined the unanimous denials of Third Street, 189 Hanover and 330 McKenzie, judging those neighbourhoods 'not ready to transition'. He backed 5% rather than 7.5% fee increases to protect entry-level buyers, described affordable housing as 'usually in the province's basket', and pushed for wastewater capacity and a seniors' task force.

“But please don't fear multifamily. You can live harmoniously with multifamily and single-family dwellings. It does work.”

Mayor Earl Funk · On the 32-unit Home Street building, which passed second reading 4–3 · Mar 19, 2024 · 1:49:17

Term by term

2014–2018

Little on housing in the record before 2018. He welcomed the Haven Group's 61-unit seniors' building in 2016, saying he lived on Lumber Street with two buildings like it behind him. In 2018 he voted for a 15-ft setback on the First Street townhouses so tenants would have more green space (defeated 3–4), moved one of the Hanover Street house purchases saying the lots could hold mixed-use or multi-family housing, voted to deny the 44 Maplewood townhouses over 'the lack of trust for the developer' and drainage, and voted against the Harmony Lane redesign out of fairness to a homeowner who had relied on the approved plan.

Took part in 10 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2014–2018 story.

2018–2022

Elected mayor in 2018. He supported infill where developers had consulted neighbours (126 Maplewood, the 72-unit Maplewood project) and argued that on costly older lots 'a side-by-side or a single-family dwelling just doesn't pay the bills' (78 First Street, 2020). He joined unanimous denials of plans he called the wrong process or too aggressive (216 Hanover, 14 Ellice), cautioned in 2021 that First Street was 'getting reasonably saturated' with apartments, and called The Bridge 'a good start' in 2022. In November 2022 he voted against the 205 McKenzie fourplex: 'I just don't feel at this point I'm ready to go with that kind of development in this area.'

Took part in 24 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2018–2022 story.

2022–2026

He backed 5% rather than staff's 7.5% yearly fee increases because entry-level housing would be hit hardest (2023), voted for the Home Street building (4–3, 2024) and its fourth storey (3–2, 2025), and supported the studio apartments' parking cut, the seven-storey seniors' building and the six-storey Bridgepark building. He joined the unanimous denials of Third Street (2023), 189 Hanover (2025) and 330 McKenzie (2026), saying 'when a neighborhood's not ready to transition, it won't work', and voted against a duplex setback after the 2025 flooding. He described affordable housing as a provincial responsibility being handed to municipalities, championed the wastewater expansion, and set up the 'Rooted in Community' seniors' task force in 2026.

Took part in 44 of the items in this report during this term. Read the 2022–2026 story.

The trajectory

From a councillor who said little on housing to a mayor who speaks for density in principle and decides it street by street. He backs apartments where he sees a neighbourhood 'transitioning' and joins denials where he does not, without a map that tells residents or builders which is which. On affordability he has relied on developers and other governments rather than city programs.
