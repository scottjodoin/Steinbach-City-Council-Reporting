Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Housing and development

Housing and development: how has Steinbach handled growth?

As Steinbach grew from about 16,000 to nearly 18,000 people and kept growing, what has city council done since December 2015 to expand affordable and supportive housing, keep a healthy mix of single-family homes and apartments, handle infill in established neighbourhoods, and pay for the infrastructure growth needs? What reasons has it given for not doing more, how did each council member act, and how do other councils compare?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026.

Executive summary

Over eleven years and three councils, Steinbach said yes to most housing and left affordability to builders and other governments. Homes are being built faster than ever, mostly apartments, but the city’s own housing tools are still on paper.

  • Record building, mostly apartments. Permits for new homes went from 50 in 2016 to a record 344 in 2024 and 365 in 2025. Apartments and other multi-family homes swung between 12% and 66% of new units from 2015 to 2023, then held at about three-quarters in 2024–2026. Council approved nearly every new subdivision and most apartment rezonings, often over petitions.
  • Affordability is still strained. The average two-bedroom rent rose about 32% from 2015 to 2025 ($749 to $991), rental vacancy fell to 0.3% in 2017 and 0.4% in 2024, and in 2021 about 22% of households spent 30% or more of their income on shelter, the highest of the Manitoba cities compared.
  • The city’s affordable-housing role: approvals and passing on others’ money. Its only affordable-housing money was a $1.34 million provincial grant it passed to Steinbach Community Outreach for 24 units (2021–22). There is no city incentive, land offer, housing needs assessment or federal Housing Accelerator Fund application. The new community plan (2025) lists tools council “should consider”; none has been adopted.
  • Infill decided street by street. Council approved more than 20 apartment projects in established neighbourhoods and refused 7. It split 4–3 on 44 First Street (2019) and Home Street (2024), denied two mid-block sites against staff advice (2025, 2026), and granted parking relief case by case. There is still no map of where apartments are expected.
  • Growth doesn’t pay its way, by council’s own account. Development fees rose 2.5% a year to 2023, then 5% a year (passed 5–2, below staff’s 7.5%): $4,502 per house in 2026, with no residential water or sewer charge. The wastewater expansion growth requires, about $132 million and later said to exceed $200 million, awaits federal help.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council welcomed density downtown and seniors’ housing but held firmly to parking and setback rules. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council approved apartment after apartment on First Street and passed on the city’s first affordable-housing grant. His second council saw record building, set development fees below staff’s advice, and split repeatedly over which streets were ready for apartments. The council elected in October 2026 inherits fees due for renewal, a community plan full of untried tools, and the wastewater bill.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words and false starts. The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every council and staff quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence. Speakers were matched to names with AI assistance, from how they were addressed in each meeting and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations, applicants and other members of the public are identified only by initials, in quotes, context boxes and the data. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner, and from 2018 the two Councillors Hiebert, often cannot be told apart in the recordings; their remarks are not attributed unless certain. Where we trim a remark, leave out a personal passage, or identify a speaker by initials, the full remarks can be watched at the linked moment in the official meeting video.
  • Residents’ claims. Neighbours at hearings made claims about property values, tenants, crime and sewer backups. These are reported as what residents said, not as fact; claims about particular people or properties are unproven. City staff told council in 2016 that no verified effect on property values had been found from multi-family development.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist (so the outcome of an October 2017 narrow-lot rezoning is unknown); and the November 2019 meetings that passed the 2020–2023 fee by-laws, which have minutes but no recording. Closed study sessions and committee meetings are not recorded.
  • What counts. 126 items: affordable, supportive and seniors’ housing; secondary suites; apartment and infill rezonings; parking and height variances for housing; large subdivisions (routine ones grouped); development fees and developer-pay rules; wastewater capacity for growth; and land at the city’s edges. Homelessness and shelters are covered in a separate report. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; both are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice, so who voted which way is taken from what members said; some 2016 vote counts were not announced.
  • Figures. New-home counts are city building-permit counts (permits, not completions): 2015–2024 from the city’s statistics page, 2025 and 2026 from SteinbachOnline reports of the city’s monthly permit reports. The city page shows only 20 multi-family units in 2023, which looks low but is what it reports. Rents and vacancy are CMHC’s October survey of purpose-built rentals of three or more units, which leaves out basement suites and rented houses; 2025 is the latest year. Household figures are from the 2016 and 2021 censuses; the 2026 census results are not yet out. Development fees for 2024–2027 are from By-law 2233.
  • Not confirmed. Whether the city ever applied to the federal Housing Accelerator Fund (none found in CMHC’s lists, council motions or news; not confirmed with the city); the city’s residential development fees year by year from 2015 to 2023 (only the 2.5% yearly increase and a 2020 news figure of about $3,611 a house were found); when The Bridge opened and its current rents; the outcome of the federal wastewater application; whether the six-storey, 100-unit First Street rezoning (2024) went to a hearing; and how much the earlier seniors’-housing incentive paid.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote on these pages was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026, with no housing items. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
  • Earlier version. This report replaces a one-page report on 2022–2026 (October 2026). Its quotes, findings and peer sources are carried over, with corrections: Brandon’s incentive by-law pays a grant equal to development charges rather than waiving them, and allows fourplexes on corner lots or lots with a lane; Red Deer had not returned its federal housing money as of January 2026.
