Steinbach City Council · Jan 2022 – Sept 2026 · Election forum topic: Housing & Development

Housing and development: keeping up with growth

As Steinbach grows, how is council expanding affordable housing, keeping a healthy mix of single-family homes and apartments, and paying for the infrastructure that growth needs? Who ends up paying, and how do other councils compare?

Quotes link to their moment in the official meeting videos; news and peer sources are linked. Last updated Oct 13, 2026.

Bottom line

  • Steinbach is building more homes than ever, and most are now apartments. The city issued permits for a record 344 homes in 2024 and 365 in 2025, about three-quarters of them in multi-family buildings, up from about half in 2022.
  • Affordability is still strained. In 2021, 21.7% of Steinbach households spent 30% or more of their income on shelter, the highest of the Manitoba cities compared. Rental vacancy fell to 0.4% in 2024.
  • Council's affordable-housing role is mostly approving projects and passing on others' money. It routed a $1.34M provincial grant to The Bridge (24 units), endorsed other projects, and granted parking relief for seniors' and studio buildings. It has no incentive program of its own, no housing needs assessment, and no application to the federal Housing Accelerator Fund on record.
  • Density is decided case by case. Most infill rezonings are approved, but proposals deep inside single-family blocks are often denied for “neighbourhood character”. The new zoning by-law doesn't allow 3–4 homes per lot as of right, as Brandon and Winnipeg now do.
  • Who pays for growth: developers pay fees of $4,288 per house and $3,112 per apartment (2025), rising 5% a year. Council chose 5% over staff's recommended 7.5% to protect affordability (5–2 vote). The biggest growth cost, a $132.5M wastewater expansion, has no matching charge on new homes.

Context

The Bridge opened in late 2023, with rents of $583–$801 including utilities. Since then council has endorsed The Bridge on Third (supportive housing) and approved seniors' buildings with long waitlists: Steinbach Housing about 200, Bridgepark Manor about 220. It has not put city money, land or fee waivers into affordable housing, and no further city affordable-housing grant followed the 2022 provincial one. Secondary suites are a sensitive point: when staff proposed enforcing suite safety standards in 2024, Councillor Siemens warned of “a loss of many low-end rental units” (Aug 20, 2024).

“So we do not want to ever be in a situation where the city is not able to issue building permits.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, applying for federal help with a $132.5M wastewater expansion · Apr 15, 2025 · 0:22:58

“as a matter of practice, sidewalks are generally not required by developers at time of development of subdivision, and that is a targeted and specific strategy of the city.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Dec 16, 2025 · 0:15:16
Context

By-law 2233 (2023) sets the fees through 2027, at $4,083 per single-family home in 2024 rising to $4,727 in 2027, and $3,112 per apartment unit in 2025. Water and sewer impact fees apply only to non-residential development (By-law 2233). The vote was 5–2, with Councillors Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert against because they wanted developers to pay more. Meanwhile the city has committed to its $65.4M share of a $132.5M wastewater project that growth makes necessary. No result of its federal funding application has been reported.

Their reasons for not doing more

1Some neighbourhoods aren't ready for density

“And so to turn this into a 6-plex, looking at the size of that lot, even though it would technically fit, it does not fit the overall character of the neighbourhood.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on denying a sixplex at 189 Hanover St · Mar 18, 2025 · 0:40:39

“But I think you're 10 years too early.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Mar 18, 2025 · 0:36:37

“I just don't feel at this point I'm ready to go with that kind of development in this area.”

Mayor Earl Funk, voting against a fourplex on McKenzie Ave · Nov 1, 2022 · 1:26:41
Context

Council decides each rezoning on its own, guided by an infill policy and the Official Community Plan's “transitional” area, but there is no map saying where apartments are expected. In October 2026 it unanimously denied 20 townhouses at 330 McKenzie, with the mayor saying “This is maybe not the time.” Five months earlier it had approved 48 units a few blocks away. Residents and builders can't predict outcomes, and each hearing becomes a neighbourhood fight.

2Higher fees would make housing less affordable

“But too big an increase would hurt growth rate.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Nov 7, 2023 · 0:11:36

“The reality is even 7.5% wouldn't cover the cost of growth.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:18:06
Context

Both sides agreed that fees are passed on to buyers, and that keeping them low shifts costs to existing taxpayers through the mill rate. Niverville charges $10,100 per house, including $4,600 for its lagoon (Niverville). Morden shows the cost of running out of wastewater capacity: it stopped accepting new building-permit applications in 2023 (CBC).

3Affordable housing is for other governments to fund

“And that was a prime example of how something good can happen from government funding, where A need is identified, the government provides funds, the local government then decides which organization is best suited to deal with those, and we got the Bridge, which has been remarkably successful.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · May 5, 2026 · 0:19:57
Context

Provincial and federal money for affordable housing exists, but much of it now rewards cities that change their own rules. Brandon received $6.2M from the federal Housing Accelerator Fund in 2024 after committing to allow four homes per lot and adding financial incentives for affordable housing (CMHC). The RM of Ritchot, the RM of Lorne and St-Pierre-Jolys, all smaller than Steinbach, shared $4.05M from its small and rural stream. No Steinbach application appears in council records.

4Parking rules should hold

“There's nothing unusual about requiring 139 stalls. That is what our bylaw requires.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, voting against 84 stalls for 84 seniors' suites · Jun 2, 2026 · 0:54:24

“I have not been convinced on the need to, to make an exception to our parking requirements.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Nov 18, 2025 · 0:49:17
Context

Council has repeatedly granted parking relief anyway: 30 stalls instead of 40 at The Bridge, one stall per suite for seniors, and 45 instead of 59 for studios. The new zoning by-law still requires 1.5 stalls per apartment plus 10% for visitors. Brandon has cut its requirement to one per unit.

5Developers know the market best

“And developers generally know best where the needs are in terms of housing our community.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Dec 16, 2025 · 0:12:19

Sources: 2024 permits, 2025 permits, CMHC rents, CMHC vacancy. Asking rents are higher: a typical listed 2-bedroom was $1,407 in July 2026 (Rentals.ca). A starter home costs “$300,000 and upwards,” a resident told council (Mar 18, 2025), and the average single-family sale was about $406,000 in early 2025.

2The rules haven't caught up with the votes

Council regularly approves apartments, height and reduced parking through one-off variances, yet the new zoning by-law (2026) keeps the old defaults. These are 1.5 stalls per unit, no 3–4 homes per lot, and detached suites only in rural areas. Writing the exceptions council already grants into the by-law, and mapping where apartments belong, would make outcomes predictable for neighbours and builders. It would also make Steinbach eligible for programs that reward those changes.

3Growth's biggest bill is wastewater

Development fees bring in roughly $1.25 million a year at 2025 volumes (estimate). The city's share of the wastewater expansion is $65.4 million, and a councillor said in May 2026 that the projected cost had “skyrocketed to over $200 million.” Fees will be reset when By-law 2233 expires in 2027. A study of what growth actually costs would let council decide how much new homes should pay and how much existing taxpayers should carry.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Commission a housing needs assessment. It is the entry ticket for most federal and provincial housing money.
  • Map where apartments are expected (the transitional area) with design standards for height, setbacks and parking, so hearings stop being case-by-case fights.
  • Write the variances council already grants into the zoning by-law: lower parking for seniors', affordable and studio housing, and detached suites on urban lots.
  • Consider 3–4 homes per lot on wider lots with a lane, as Brandon did, and apply for any future Housing Accelerator Fund round.
  • Adopt an affordable-housing incentive by-law (fee offsets, small per-unit grants, tax rebates, city land) that stacks with Manitoba's forgivable loans of up to $70,000 per affordable unit.
  • Rebuild development fees from a cost study when By-law 2233 expires in 2027, including a residential share of wastewater costs and different rates for infill and greenfield areas.
  • Keep pushing for wastewater funding so Steinbach never has to stop issuing permits, as Morden did.
