Housing and development · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for refusing particular housing projects, for leaving affordable housing to others, and for not charging growth more, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given the same eight reasons for a decade. Several are sound on their own terms: listening at public hearings, keeping fees affordable, and building sewer capacity before it runs out. The weaker points are where a reason is applied case by case with no written test (which streets are “ready”, when parking can bend), where it hands the job to someone else (the market, the province), or where council itself says it doesn’t add up (fees that don’t cover growth).

Councillor Jake Hiebert, on six units at 14 Ellice Avenue · Aug 18, 2020 · 1:02:19

“I think when you look at the neighborhood, the previous request for rezoning, now that area might not have been ready yet. It wasn’t ready to transition, but this area is transitioning.”

Mayor Earl Funk, approving 12 units at 288 Henry Street the night 330 McKenzie was denied · Oct 6, 2026 · 1:22:58
Context

Whether a street is “ready” is decided at each hearing. The community plan’s transitional area, which Councillor Damian Penner said “ends at 2nd Street” when Third Street was denied, did not settle it: council approved Home Street (2024) outside it, and denied sixplexes and townhouses at 189 Hanover (2025) and 330 McKenzie (2026) where staff had recommended approval. Residents on both sides asked for clearer rules. T.N., objecting to 12 units on Giesbrecht Street, asked whether the city has “a plan instead of infill”; K.B., objecting at 330 McKenzie, told council “Your plan and zoning mean nothing if you rezone this property.” No map of where apartments are expected has been adopted.

2Public hearings matter

“I’ll take the position of the people that live in that area and know that area.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on the 205 McKenzie fourplex · Nov 1, 2022 · 1:24:53

“This development does not fit the neighbourhood, and I believe council needs to listen to the concerned citizens of this area.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, moving the 330 McKenzie denial · Oct 6, 2026 · 0:47:10

Other members pushed back on letting objections decide:

“We can’t stop developing the city in perpetuity until we’ve satisfied the timeline of whatever it arbitrarily may be that residents start to— are feeling, feeling okay with it.”

Councillor Damian Penner, on the Bridgepark seniors’ building · Jul 21, 2026 · 1:01:16
Context

Council often approved projects over petitions: 9 units at 126 Maplewood over 56 names (2019), Home Street over 43 (2024) and 48 units on McKenzie Avenue over a petition (2026). The denials came where turnout was large and the site was mid-block. Neighbours raised traffic, drainage, privacy, trees and property values; some linked renters to crime. Those claims about future tenants are unproven, and in 2016 the city manager told council there had “never been any verified impacts on property values” from multi-family development in the city. Residents also spoke for density: G.S. told council in 2026 that the city needs density “to pay the bills”. Whether a developer had consulted neighbours became an informal test: 216 Hanover (2019) and Third Street (2023) were denied partly because it had not happened.
