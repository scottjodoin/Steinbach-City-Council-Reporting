Transit · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

Could a city of about 20,000 like Steinbach realistically run public transit? What do similar cities run, what does it cost, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • Yes, it is realistic, but not free. Smaller cities run transit: Winkler (13,745 people in 2021) a one-van on-demand service for about $365,000 a year, Selkirk (10,504) a loop bus since 2011, and Swift Current, Saskatchewan (16,750) a bus route and paratransit for about $425,000 (2021). Fares cover only part of the cost.
  • Not every attempt works. Portage la Prairie studied transit and found every option “not financially feasible at this time” (2022). Thompson suspended its fixed-route buses in 2024 after ridership fell to a few people a day.
  • Steinbach is typical on accessible transit and behind on everything else. Its ACCESSible Transit is comparable to Morden’s and Portage’s handi-vans. Unlike Winkler, Portage, Okotoks or Cochrane, it has never studied general transit, applied for transit money or answered a proposal with a decision.

Key findings

1Cities Steinbach’s size do run transit, and some stop

Winkler is the closest comparison: a fast-growing southern Manitoba city, smaller than Steinbach, which studied transit and then launched one accessible van in November 2024. It runs weekdays 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. and Saturdays, is booked by app, web or phone, charges $5 a trip, and carries about 900 trips a month (2025). It costs about $365,000 a year, roughly $34 a trip or $27 per resident; Steinbach’s grant to ACCESSible Transit is about $5.90 per resident (2026, on the 2021 census population). Winkler extended its pilot to 2028. Selkirk, with about 10,500 people, has run an hourly loop bus since 2011 and carried 35,642 rides in 2024. But Portage la Prairie, about Winkler’s size, found every option it studied “not financially feasible at this time” (2022), and Thompson’s mayor said its buses averaged “four to five people on a bus in a day” before service was suspended in 2024. See reason 1 and reason 4.

2The “empty buses” worry fits fixed routes, not what was proposed

Council’s main worry is a bus driving a fixed loop with few riders. That is why the coalition, Winkler, Okotoks and Cochrane chose on-demand service: vans run only when someone books a trip. Okotoks and Cochrane started that way in 2019 and added fixed routes only when ridership reached about 77,000 to 78,000 trips a year. The failures in this comparison were fixed routes or commuter buses: Thompson’s town buses and Selkirk’s Winnipeg commuter runs. See reason 1.

3Commuting figures don’t measure the need

Only 0.2% of Steinbach’s workers commuted by transit in 2021, but there was no transit to take. Winkler’s figure was the same 0.2% before its van launched. Census commuting counts only trips to work and misses seniors, people with disabilities, youth and people without a car. Steinbach’s own signs of need are growing use of ACCESSible Transit (3,853 rides in 2022, 5,477 in 2023), the coalition’s survey of 751 people, mostly its clients (32% walk, 23% rely on rides), and a growing population: 17,806 in 2021, about 22,000 by CBC’s 2026 estimate. See reason 2.

4Transit money goes mostly to cities that already run transit

  • Planning: the federal Rural Transit Solutions Fund paid for studies of up to $50,000 with no local share required, but projects had to be finished by March 31, 2026, so it is effectively closed. Its successor, the rural and remote stream of the Canada Public Transit Fund, takes applications in periodic rounds; we found no round open in 2026, and whether a city of Steinbach’s size counts as “rural” is unconfirmed. A study now would most likely have to be paid for by the city or its partners.
  • Vehicles: the same fund’s capital awards paid for vans and buses across rural Manitoba in 2024–2026, such as the RM of Piney ($154,330 for on-demand vans) and Flin Flon ($1,776,000 for a bus and stops). Its capital intake is closed.
  • Ongoing money goes to existing systems: the Canada Public Transit Fund’s baseline stream (in Manitoba, Winnipeg, Brandon and Selkirk), the 2023 provincial transit relief and the 2026 provincial student transit passes.
  • Operating costs fall mostly on the city since the province ended 50/50 sharing in 2017. Winkler started with a Federation of Canadian Municipalities grant covering 80% of a three-year pilot; it must find the money for the extra years once that runs out in spring 2027.

See reason 5 and reason 6.

5The partner Steinbach already trusts could run more

Council’s 2021 handover to Serving Seniors Inc. kept accessible transit running at about the same cost while rides grew. The coalition’s plan proposed that the same operator, the Pat Porter Active Living Centre, run four on-demand vans. Swift Current shows the model working: one disability non-profit, SaskAbilities, runs both its paratransit and its public bus under a city contract. A partnership of that kind fits the preference council has stated for community and private partners. See reason 3.

6What is missing is a decision

Portage la Prairie’s council studied transit, put the costs in public and said no for now, leaving the door open; residents know why. Steinbach’s council has given its reasons in news interviews and unrecorded sessions, but has not voted on a study, the coalition’s plan or KodaGo’s request. Its stated condition, “a viable plan,” has no one assigned to produce it. See reason 4.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Commission a costed transit study, as Winkler and Portage la Prairie did, so council can decide on the record. With the federal planning grant closed and no rural transit round found open in 2026, the city would likely have to fund it, perhaps with Hanover and the RM of Piney, whose regional study it could build on, and with the coalition.
  • Answer the proposals already on the table with a council motion: the coalition’s LinK Transit plan and KodaGo’s request for a partnership on medical and airport trips.
  • Be ready for the next funding round: with a plan in hand, the city could apply when the federal rural transit stream or an FCM program next opens, instead of starting from nothing.
  • If the study supports it, pilot on-demand service with one or two vans, run by an existing operator such as Pat Porter, on Winkler’s model.
  • Strengthen ACCESSible Transit: publish its hours, fares and ridership, and fund the extra drivers that would allow evening and weekend service.
  • Weigh transit when flexible grants allow it, as the 2026 provincial safety grant did.
  • Keep pressing the province to restore 50/50 operating funding in a form that also covers new services, not only existing ones.
