Transit · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for not going further on public transit, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council’s reasons fall into six groups. Some rest on real limits: transit costs money every year, fares cover little of it, most residents drive, and the province stopped sharing operating costs in 2017. Others are choices: to leave transport to private businesses, to wait for a plan without commissioning one, and to wait for grants without applying. Several of the clearest statements were made at unrecorded sessions and are known only from news reports. Council also did something well: it kept accessible transit running at a steady cost through a capable community partner.

  • 1. “It is very expensive”
  • 2. “We are the automobile city”
  • 3. Private businesses and partners should fill the gap
  • 4. A plan first, “but not immediately”
  • 5. The province should share the cost
  • 6. No money until there are grants

1“It is very expensive”

The cost argument has mostly been made outside recorded meetings. In June 2023 the mayor told SteinbachOnline the city was doing its due diligence on transit: “Every time we talk, there is one common theme, it is very expensive.” In February 2024 Councillor Zwaagstra said a fixed bus loop “would be very expensive and you run the risk of having empty buses driving around” (SteinbachOnline). Even the 2021 handover of Handi-Transit came with a cost condition: staff said council’s direction was to proceed only if it could be done without a significant change in cost. At a recorded meeting, the mayor described what transit costs other cities:

“Public transit, that is something that is very dear to a couple of the bigger cities. Brandon and Winnipeg are experiencing great costs with this.”

Mayor Earl Funk, reporting on the Manitoba municipalities convention · Apr 15, 2025 · 0:03:49
Context

Transit does cost money every year, and fares cover only part of it. Swift Current, Saskatchewan (16,750 people), recovered about 30% of its bus costs from fares in 2019. But the scale matters. Winkler’s one-van on-demand service costs about $365,000 a year (city estimate reported by CBC, 2026). That would be under 1% of Steinbach’s $43.96 million 2026 budget, against the $105,000 (0.24%) the city now gives ACCESSible Transit. Council’s own members agreed early that a fixed loop was the wrong model. The coalition’s plan, and Winkler’s service, use vans that run only when someone books a ride, which is how small cities avoid “empty buses” (see Finding 2). The city has never put a number on what a service would cost Steinbach.

2“We are the automobile city”

“But we also realize that we are the automobile city and we do have to make sure that our vehicles still have a place on the road.”

Mayor Earl Funk, answering a resident at the Official Community Plan hearing · Apr 1, 2025 · 0:46:15

“The reality is that most people in Steinbach do drive vehicles, particularly in the winter months, and we're not going to do anything to impede that.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on the Active Transportation Plan · May 6, 2025 · 1:03:48

“In Steinbach, the vast majority of people who have cars— so that is just simply how most people get around, including people who live on Main Street.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, in a parking debate · Nov 18, 2025 · 0:49:04
Context

The numbers back the description. In the 2021 census, taken during the pandemic, 90.7% of Steinbach’s commuters went by car, truck or van, and 15 people (0.2%) by public transit; 58% commuted under 15 minutes. But census commuting counts only trips to work, so it misses the people transit is mostly for: seniors, people with disabilities, youth and people who cannot afford a car. Winkler’s census share was also 0.2% before it launched its van, which now carries about 900 trips a month. In the coalition’s survey of 751 people, mostly clients of its member agencies, 32% walk, 23% get rides from others and 12% take taxis. ACCESSible Transit’s rides grew from 3,853 in 2022 to 5,477 in 2023. A resident asked the question at the 2025 hearing:

“Because also, how will seniors get around if they can't use their automobiles?”

A.B., resident · Apr 1, 2025 · 0:41:36

One councillor pointed the other way:

“Our planning has always been in our community, like many communities, is how do we move the vehicles? It's not how do we move people outside of vehicles.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · May 6, 2025 · 0:58:35

3Private businesses and partners should fill the gap

“It sounds like this is a request to limit competition. Is that not the case?”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, to Steinbach Taxi, which asked for a by-law · Feb 20, 2018 · 0:51:00

“I think this is a great opportunity here that that to have the service provided by an organization like Serving Seniors that already specializes in providing a variety of services to seniors in our community… It's a natural fit.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving the Handi-Transit handover · Dec 7, 2021 · 0:50:06

“So discuss it, 'cause we don't at this point do that.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on KodaGo’s request for a city partnership · Aug 4, 2026 · 1:13:57

“That's what private enterprise does. Sees a need, builds a product for it, and puts it in there and takes care of it.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Aug 4, 2026 · 1:15:00

In May 2018 council decided against a taxi by-law; Councillor Zwaagstra said it would only “limit competition,” and Deputy Mayor Cari Penner said less regulation was best for competition (SteinbachOnline). In February 2024 Councillor Zwaagstra said the city should not set up a system that competes with taxis. In August 2026 a Councillor Penner, most likely Damian Penner, told KodaGo’s founder that private innovation “is often far superior to public spent dollars,” though the city might look at public transit in the future.

Context

The partner model has worked for accessible transit: Pat Porter has run it since 2022 at about the same city cost, and rides grew. Private options do exist: taxis, KodaGo, and Southeast Shuttle, a Steinbach company that runs booked trips to Winnipeg hospitals and the airport. But they are priced for door-to-door trips: Southeast Shuttle charges $125 plus tax per vehicle each way to Winnipeg (Nov 2026), and cannot carry passengers who must stay seated in a wheelchair. No scheduled bus has run to Winnipeg since Greyhound left Manitoba in October 2018. KodaGo’s founder described the gap:

“Okay, can I get a bus to Steinbach? No bus to Steinbach. Okay, can I get— what about Uber? Yeah, well, you can get to Steinbach, but you can't get back.”

J.O., KodaGo founder · Aug 4, 2026 · 1:02:18

He asked for a partnership on medical and airport trips, and the mayor invited a letter; nothing followed through November 3, 2026. Both the coalition’s plan and Swift Current’s service put an existing community operator in charge, the same partner model council praised in 2021 (see Finding 5).

4A plan first, “but not immediately”

“Community and Regional Planning recommends that Steinbach begin planning for a transit system now and incorporate design considerations for a transit system. Beginning to plan for transit today would position Steinbach very well in the future and could potentially save money on the cost of infrastructure improvements and on property acquisitions.”

S.W., regional manager, Province of Manitoba Community and Regional Planning · Jul 17, 2018 · 0:45:45

“In regards to transportation, we note that their comments are definitely something we should be looking into, and within the future, we do want to take on a transportation plan. But that's not something we would be doing immediately.”

Lacey Gaudet, City Planner · Jul 17, 2018 · 0:56:06

“What we will do, of course, as council, we'll ask administration to give us a small report.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen, on Steinbach Taxi’s request · Feb 20, 2018 · 1:00:56

In February 2024 Councillor Susan Penner said what was missing was “the cost analysis, how this would be funded, what grants are available,” and Councillor Damian Penner said exploring alternatives was “something that we have an obligation to start looking at exploring” (SteinbachOnline). In July 2026 council told CBC it needs “a viable plan” before acting.

Context

Asking for a plan is reasonable; the gap is that the city has not made one. Eight years after the province’s advice, the 2025 Official Community Plan says only that the city “could consider” public transportation, and the Active Transportation Plan covers walking and cycling. The only plans on the table were made by others: a 2006 University of Winnipeg study, commissioned by a Steinbach group, found the city “well-positioned” for transit when it had about 10,000 people (CBC, 2023); the RM of Piney led a federally funded southeast Manitoba transit study (2022–2024) without Steinbach as a partner; and the coalition delivered its LinK Transit business plan in May 2025, which council has not formally answered. Peers that decided either way did their own study first: Winkler started its van after one, and Portage la Prairie’s council studied three options and found them “not financially feasible at this time” (2022). The federal planning grant those communities could use, up to $50,000, required projects to finish by March 31, 2026 and is effectively closed (see Finding 4).

5The province should share the cost

“We're all going to advocate to go back to the 50/50. funding return because the cost is killing the communities that are providing transit.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on the cities’ push at the Manitoba municipalities convention · Apr 15, 2025 · 0:03:49

“And the province lately, it seems that they give us very little time to react.”

Councillor John Fehr, on taxi rules handed to cities · Feb 20, 2018 · 0:55:22
Context

This is a fair complaint. Manitoba ended 50/50 sharing of municipal transit operating costs in 2017; Winkler and other cities are lobbying to restore it, and the province is “receptive” but has promised nothing (CBC, 2026). The province does help with accessible transit: about $30,000 a year to Steinbach through its Mobility Disadvantaged Transportation Program. But most transit money goes only to cities that already run transit: the 2023 provincial transit relief ($13.4 million for Winnipeg, Brandon, Thompson, Selkirk and Flin Flon), the 2026 student transit passes, and the federal Canada Public Transit Fund’s ongoing “baseline” stream (in Manitoba, only Winnipeg, Brandon and Selkirk). Waiting for the province keeps Steinbach outside those programs. A 2026 resolution from community groups asks that any restored agreement also cover new services.

6No money until there are grants

Council has not committed money to the coalition’s plan; it asked in 2024 what grants were available, and gave the plan verbal support only. In July 2026, a grant council could have used came up:

“So some possibles: increasing access to healthcare, public transportation, active transportation, increased RCMP or CSO presence in the community, affordable housing and homelessness, and recreation or leisure programming.”

Adam Thiessen, Senior Manager, Corporate Services, on a $200,000 provincial safety grant · Jul 21, 2026 · 1:22:18
Context

Staff recommended a community safety officer truck ($65,000) and security cameras ($135,000) instead; council deferred the item, and its final use has not been made public. The coalition’s plan already counted on grants ($100,000 in applications) and asked the city for help applying. Grants for small cities’ transit have existed: Winkler’s pilot was 80% funded by a Federation of Canadian Municipalities grant of up to $500,000, and the federal Rural Transit Solutions Fund paid for vans and studies across rural Manitoba, including the RM of Piney’s. We found no Steinbach application. Whether a similar FCM grant is open now, and when the federal rural transit stream will next take applications, could not be confirmed (see Finding 4).
