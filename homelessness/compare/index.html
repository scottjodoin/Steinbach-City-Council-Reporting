Homelessness · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How many people are homeless in Steinbach compared with similar places, how does Steinbach’s council response compare with what other municipal councils do within their powers, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • A small, uncounted problem. SCO’s estimate of about 50 people (2026) is roughly 25 to 28 per 10,000 residents, near the Canadian average, but it is not a count. A regional count including Steinbach was planned for fall 2026.
  • Typical of small Manitoba cities, behind the active ones. Winkler, Morden, Portage la Prairie and Selkirk, like Steinbach, mostly leave homelessness to agencies and have no general overnight shelter. Brandon, Thompson and Lloydminster use city tools Steinbach does not: grants and tax breaks, city land, staff, counts and a cold-night warming shelter.
  • Steinbach’s distinctive move was regulation. It made shelters and transitional housing need a public hearing, while Portage la Prairie used the same zoning powers to let a shelter open.

Key findings

1The problem is small enough to solve, but no one has measured it

SCO’s estimates rose from “a dozen or so” couch-surfing (2021) to about 30 people on the street (2022), about 30 outside and 30 couch-surfing (2024) and about 50 (2026). Its director said in 2024 that the numbers “haven’t shifted significantly” and the rise was mostly in visibility. About 50 people is roughly 28 per 10,000 on the 2021 Census population (17,806) or 25 per 10,000 on the city’s estimate of over 20,000. Canada’s 2025 national count found about 22 per 10,000 overall and 38 per 10,000 in participating communities under 100,000; Brandon’s 2025 count was about 38 per 10,000 and Medicine Hat’s about 21. The methods differ, so these comparisons are rough. Nine in ten Steinbach respondents to a small online CBC survey (2026) think homelessness is getting worse. Without a count, no one can say. See reason 3.

2Zoning has been council’s main tool, and it slowed the one shelter project

Council’s biggest homelessness decisions added a public hearing for any shelter or transitional housing. Council says this protects neighbours, and a hearing is a normal tool. But the change was prompted by one project, went against the consultant’s advice, and the label mattered for funding. The INN’s plan changed from 30–40 overnight mats (2023) to 12 rooms (February 2026) to 30 people in 18-month stays (September 2026); it paid a lawyer to help the city draft the transitional-housing by-law, and still had no permit in September 2026. Portage la Prairie’s council used the same powers the other way, though its shelter closed in August 2021 when COVID relief money ended. See reason 4.

3“Not municipal jurisdiction” is only partly true

The province and Ottawa hold most of the money and services. But councils control tools that decide whether projects get built, and outside money often needs the city’s cooperation:

  • Land and incentives: Brandon’s program gives $20,000 per unit, waives development charges and abates taxes for 20 years, and in August 2026 the city and province called for up to 48 transitional and supportive units on city land offered for $1.
  • Running the money: Thompson, a city of about 13,000, has administered more than $1.7 million in federal Reaching Home funds since 2019 and runs one intake list.
  • Provincial programs: Housing Starts Here offers forgivable loans of up to $70,000 a unit and proposal grants of up to $250,000, with priority for rural projects with homelessness supports (amounts from a search summary of the program page).
  • Federal programs: Reaching Home’s rural stream, run by BNRC, listed about $1.95 million across 11 rural Manitoba projects in 2025–26, including $101,130 to SCO. The 2024 Emergency Treatment Fund needed the city as the applicant; council took no action.

See reason 1 and reason 2.

4Council relies on agencies, but rarely funds them

Members credit Steinbach Community Outreach, Eden, Today House and the churches, and The Bridge shows what they can do with provincial money. But every direct request to the city in this record was taken as information or declined, and the only grants found were $500 in total (2017). SCO, which serves 1,400 lower-income families, told CBC in 2026 it lacks the resources for a shelter. See reason 3.

5Enforcement capacity has grown faster than places to go

Since 2024 the city has added a second community safety officer and private overnight patrols ($145,000 estimated for 2026). For the 2026 provincial safety grant, staff recommended a truck and cameras although homelessness was an eligible use. Mental Health Act calls to the RCMP rose from 251 to 311 year to date between 2025 and 2026. Enforcement moves people along; it does not create places for them to go. See reason 6.

6Council agrees on the need it describes

Councillor Jake Hiebert said people in the camps wanted long-term housing, jobs and treatment; Councillor Siemens called transitional housing “critical”; the mayor said the city needs it. That is the housing-with-support model the evidence favours, and the model of The Bridge, the Bridge on Third and The INN’s later plans. What is missing is a city role in making more of it happen. See reason 5.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Decide The INN’s site on its merits, soon: help it to a conditional-use hearing so its government funding can flow.
  • An incentive by-law for supportive and transitional housing, as Brandon has: per-unit grants, fee waivers and tax breaks that stack with provincial and federal money.
  • City land at nominal cost for a joint project with Manitoba Housing.
  • Be the applicant when only a city can apply, as with the 2024 Emergency Treatment Fund, passing the money to the agency that runs the program.
  • Back the count and a shared intake list: support the regional count, publish its results, and help SCO, Eden, Agape House, Southern Health and the RCMP keep one list.
  • Fund or host the safety plan’s coordinator, with a housing role.
  • An extreme-cold protocol: a city-activated warming space or mobile unit, as Lloydminster runs.
  • Publish the encampment policy the mayor asked for, pairing any enforcement with offers of housing and outreach.
  • Keep pressing the province to extend Your Way Home beyond Winnipeg and add treatment capacity in the Southern Health region.
