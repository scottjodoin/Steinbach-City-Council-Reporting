Services in new neighbourhoods · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do Steinbach’s rules for what developers build, when, and how the city makes sure it gets done compare with other Manitoba and Canadian cities’ published rules, and what would “doing all it can” add?

In short

  • The technical rules are typical. Steinbach’s runoff limit, lot grading plans and grade checks are in line with Manitoba peers. Brandon and Winnipeg go further by having surveyors or engineers certify grades.
  • Sidewalks are the clearest difference. Steinbach collects a sidewalk levy ($1,958 per single-family home in 2026) and builds sidewalks itself after most homes are up. Brandon’s 2025 conditions and Calgary’s agreement have the developer build sidewalks or paths with the road.
  • What Steinbach doesn’t publish is the bigger gap. Brandon puts every subdivision condition, including a 15% letter of credit, in the report council votes on; Morden writes conditions into its minutes; Saskatoon publishes a status list for every park in its new neighbourhoods. Steinbach publishes no agreement terms, securities, park dedication rate or tracker.

How Steinbach compares with the published rules of other cities. Under Manitoba’s Planning Act a council may make a subdivision conditional on an agreement for drainage, roads, street lighting, sidewalks and landscaping “at the owner’s expense,” and may take up to 10% of the land (or cash in lieu) for public reserve. The Act sets no deadlines; those are left to each agreement.

Key findings

1The rules are mostly sound; no one outside City Hall can check them

Steinbach’s drainage and grading rules are in line with other Manitoba cities. What sets it apart is that the development agreement, the document that says what each developer must build and by when, is negotiated by staff after council votes, and council says it doesn’t get involved. Residents at hearings ask about drainage drawings, sidewalks, playgrounds, driveways and construction routes, and are told those will be in an agreement they can’t see. Brandon’s Development Review Group sends council a numbered list of conditions with each subdivision; for one 2025 subdivision it covered road and path construction, stormwater storage, tree contributions, green space and a letter of credit for 15% of the work’s cost. See reason 2.

2Sidewalks-later is a choice peers don’t make

The City Manager called it “a targeted and specific strategy” (2025). Its stated reason, construction damage, is real, but Calgary handles it differently: its master development agreement has the developer build “all sidewalks, curbs and gutters” at its own cost, maintain them for two winters, and repair builders’ damage, with security held (2015 agreement; may since have been updated). Brandon’s 2025 conditions required a multi-use path on one side and a sidewalk on the other as the road was built. Steinbach’s approach collects the money up front, $1,958 per single-family home in 2026, but the first residents walk on the road for years, finished yards are dug up later, and the city adds operating and gas tax money. Steinbach’s sidewalk standard itself (both sides of collectors, local streets where warranted) is in line with the Winkler–Morden district plan. See reason 1 and reason 3.

3Parks follow the phases, so the first residents wait

Winnipeg’s Park Development Guidelines (November 2025) say public reserve should be planned “concurrently with the design of the entire subdivision,” and let the agreement require the developer to “grade, level and vegetate” park land, build primary pathways and “install or pay a share of” playgrounds, backed by security. No peer we found sets a fixed deadline, such as a playground once a set share of homes is built, but securing the work in the agreement means it can’t quietly slip to a later phase. Saskatoon funds new-neighbourhood parks from a levy on new lots and publishes when each will be built. In Steinbach, Hampton Village’s play structure came after neighbours pushed for it, its developer got back part of its donated park land in 2024 because the area still exceeded the minimum, and any Williams Creek park depends on a phase 2. In Niverville a developer donated a playground in 2024 while building was still going on: a gift, not a rule. See reason 1.

4Complaints get checked, but the burden falls on homeowners

The city does respond: it reported back on the 2024 Chrysler Gate complaints within about two months and cleaned out the Keating Drain in fall 2025. But the test is whether work matches the approved plan, and the complaint process asks the homeowner to prove the problem (water over two inches deep for over 36 hours, then grade stakes before an inspection). It does not ask whether a new development next door changed how water moves. After the 2024 and 2025 storms council passed Land Drainage By-law 2274, with inspections and fines, and agreed to take over the Manning Canal; the lot grading policy review was listed but not scheduled. Brandon and Winnipeg require certified grades from a surveyor or engineer. See reason 7 and reason 8.

5Older and fringe areas catch up one fight at a time

Clearspring Greens, Deerfield and Creekside Drive still lack services newer neighbourhoods take for granted. Council has been firm about not creating more such areas: no new wells or septic, no unserviced lot splits, objections to Hanover. For existing ones, it has acted case by case: Home Street’s swale declined as too costly (2017), Clearspring Greens’ path after a three-year fight and a 50/50 split (2021), Park Road West paved from reserves (2023), the Clearspring Greens sewer rerouted (2026). No plan or timeline for water, sewer or drainage in these areas came up at any meeting. See reason 4 and reason 5.

6Council has bent its own standards more than it has added to them

Council refused to add conditions at hearings “on an ad hoc basis,” including the only sidewalk condition a member proposed (2026). But it made exceptions the other way: open swales instead of curb and gutter (2016), no paving for businesses on gravel roads (2017–2018), and a Sterling Crescent variance against the engineer’s stormwater advice (2025, 4–3). If the principle is that rules should be set in advance, the place to set them is the standards, the development fee by-law (in force through 2027) and a published agreement template. See reason 6.

What “doing all it can” would add

  • Publish the conditions before the vote. Attach the main development-agreement terms (drainage, sidewalks and paths, park timing, paving, construction routes and securities) to each subdivision report, as Brandon does, and publish a standard agreement template.
  • Have developers build or secure sidewalks and paths on collector streets and school routes when the road is built, with a maintenance period and a deposit for builder damage, as Calgary and Brandon do, instead of the city adding them after build-out. The fee by-law’s renewal for 2028 is the natural moment.
  • Put playground timing in the agreement: built, or paid for with security, in the phase that creates the demand, following Winnipeg’s guidelines; publish the park dedication rate.
  • Publish a status list of unfinished subdivision works and parks, as Saskatoon does for parks, and a log of drainage complaints and outcomes.
  • Finish the lot grading policy review. It could add surveyor-certified final grades (Brandon, Winnipeg), publish the grading deposit, and check whether new subdivisions change drainage for older lots next door.
  • Close the open complaints on the record: Wyndham Estate Drive and Ash Grove Lane drainage and the Albert Street back lane.
  • Write a servicing plan for fringe areas (Clearspring Greens, Deerfield, Creekside Drive) with options, costs and who pays for water, sewer and drainage.
  • Make construction routes a written condition of approval, not a hope expressed at the hearing.
