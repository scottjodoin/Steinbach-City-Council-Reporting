Services in new neighbourhoods · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for not requiring more of developers up front, and for not doing more for older and fringe areas, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council’s reasons fall into eight groups. Several rest on real points: sidewalks laid early do get damaged by construction, drainage design is engineering work, the city can’t fix every older street at once, and rules should be known before an applicant applies. But together they produce a pattern: services that new residents expect arrive years after they move in, or are paid for by the neighbourhood, and the terms that would settle the question sit in agreements that neither council nor residents see.

  • 1. Sidewalks and roads follow development
  • 2. “The development agreement will deal with it”
  • 3. New development already pays its share
  • 4. The neighbourhood that benefits should pay
  • 5. It costs too much
  • 6. No exceptions, and no conditions added at the hearing
  • 7. It’s on private property
  • 8. Study it first

“However, as a matter of practice, sidewalks are generally not required by developers at time of development of subdivision, and that is a targeted and specific strategy of the city. However, that then is something that the City of Steinbach proceeds to install at a later date in and on streets that are appropriate for the needs and the uses that are occurring in the neighborhood.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on a Park Hill request · Dec 16, 2025 · 0:14:34

“We don't put a sidewalk in every single street. We put them where it makes sense to do so.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Dec 16, 2025 · 0:17:16

“It's development that would prompt improvements to infrastructure.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on Bush Farm Road · Mar 3, 2020 · 0:40:33
Context

Construction damage is a real cost, and the practice is old: in 2018 the City Manager said sidewalks go in at “75 to 80% complete.” The city’s 2024 design standard requires sidewalks on both sides of collector and arterial streets, and on local streets only when warranted; it does not say who builds them or when. Not putting one on every bay or crescent matches that standard. But the wait can be long. Wyndham Estate Drive residents asked for sidewalks in August 2021; part came in 2022, and 320 metres more in 2026. G.W., a Wyndham Estate Drive resident and retired land developer, said putting them in late meant yards “had to be dug up” and re-sodded “at our taxpayers' expense” (Aug 4, 2026). Calgary handles the damage problem differently: the developer builds the sidewalks, maintains them for two winters and pays for builders’ damage, with security held (see Finding 2). For fringe roads the same rule meant Bush Farm Road and Loewen Boulevard stayed gravel while traffic grew, though in 2023 council showed it can choose otherwise, paying $1,970,097 to finish Park Road West rather than wait.

Context

The standards behind the agreements are real. Since at least 2016 new development must not send more water off site than before ((“net zero outflow,” the mayor called it)ldquo;net zero outflow,(“net zero outflow,” the mayor called it)rdquo; Mayor Goertzen called it), each agreement requires a professional lot grading plan before the first building permit, and city staff check rough grades. But the agreements themselves (what the developer must build, by when, and what security the city holds) are not attached to agendas or minutes, and no public list of them exists. Residents keep asking to see the plans before council votes; Golfview Crescent residents asked for independent review because, as the City Manager confirmed, the consultants work for the developer (Jul 21, 2026). Manitoba’s Planning Act lets an agreement cover drainage, roads, street lighting, sidewalks and landscaping “at the owner’s expense,” but sets no deadlines; that is left to each agreement. Brandon lists every condition, including a 15% letter of credit, in the report council votes on, and Morden’s minutes spell out a subdivision’s conditions (see Finding 1). Standards are also negotiable: council approved open swales as a one-off in 2016 and a Sterling Crescent variance against the engineer’s stormwater advice in 2025. Where complaints were checked, the test was whether work matched the approved plan, not whether the plan worked for the neighbours.

3New development already pays its share

“We need, obviously, for new developments to be able to contribute towards the cost of the impact on the city. This goes towards things such as new sidewalks in the area.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, renewing the development fees · Oct 4, 2016 · 0:09:59

“New lots receive sidewalks on lot frontages that are designed and planned to have paths installed later date.”

Councillor Damian Penner · Jan 7, 2020 · 1:26:09

“because there is no development that is actually fronting directly onto Bush Farm Road, there would be no requirement for any improvements to that particular roadway.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Mar 3, 2020 · 0:38:25
Context

Developers do pay. Each new single-family home pays a development fee of $4,502 in 2026, of which $1,958 is the sidewalk part ($1,610 in 2021; $2,055 in 2027), under By-law 2233. Developers also build the roads, pipes and drainage, and the city’s 2022 Acres Drive extension was recovered entirely from frontage levies. But the fee is collected years before the sidewalk is built, and the 2026 Wyndham Estate Drive sidewalk was paid from the operating fund, the capital development reserve and the gas tax reserve. In September 2023 administration said construction inflation since 2013 called for fees about 30% higher. Under the frontage rule, roads beside a development but not fronting it (Bush Farm Road in 2020) get no upgrade from it. In Calgary the developer builds “all sidewalks, curbs and gutters” at its own cost before the city accepts the street.
