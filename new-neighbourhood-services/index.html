Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Services in new neighbourhoods

New neighbourhoods: do drainage, sidewalks and parks arrive on time?

When Steinbach City Council approves a new subdivision, what must the developer build, and by when: lot grading and drainage, sidewalks and pathways, parks and playgrounds, paved roads, street lights? Who checks that it gets done, and what has happened when residents said it hadn’t? What about older and fringe areas that rely on wells, septic tanks, ditches or low-pressure sewer, and land annexed from the RM of Hanover? How did each council member act, and how do other cities handle it?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 9, 2026. This report replaces our October 2026 page on 2022–2026 and extends it back to 2015, with peer figures re-checked in November 2026.

Executive summary

Across three councils Steinbach kept one model: council approves each subdivision “subject to a development agreement” that staff negotiate, the developer builds roads, pipes and drainage, and sidewalks, playgrounds and street lights often come later, paid by fees, by the city or by the neighbours.

  • Approved on terms council doesn’t see. Council approved every new residential neighbourhood phase in this record unanimously, Every residential subdivision from 2015 to 2026 passed unanimously, “subject to a development agreement” whose termsldquo;subject to a development agreementEvery residential subdivision from 2015 to 2026 passed unanimously, “subject to a development agreement” whose termsrdquo; whose terms and securities are not published. Neighbours’ drainage questions were answered with “the development agreement will deal with it,” and in 2026 the mayor said “council doesn’t get involved in the development agreement.” Brandon puts every condition, including a 15% letter of credit, in the report its council votes on.
  • Sidewalks later, by design. Developers are not required to build sidewalks, “a targeted and specific strategy” (City Manager, 2025). New homes pay a sidewalk levy ($1,610 in 2021, $1,958 in 2026) and the city builds sidewalks at about 75–80% build-out, on streets that need them; Wyndham Estate Drive, where residents asked in 2021, got sidewalks in 2022 and 2026. Requests to require them at approval were refused in 2025 and twice in 2026. Calgary and Brandon have developers build them with the road.
  • Parks come later, or from residents. Hampton Village’s play structure arrived in 2023 after neighbours worked with the city; in 2024 the developer got back part of the park land it had donated there; Williams Creek’s first 153 lots (2026) pay a fee, with any park left to a second phase. No rule says when a playground must be built.
  • Drainage: sound rules, slow fixes. New development may not add runoff, and each lot needs a grading plan. Complaints were checked against the approved plan (“built according to what was spec’d,” 2024). After the 2024 and 2025 storms council passed Land Drainage By-law 2274 with inspections and fines (2026), but left its lot grading policy review for “the future.” It also bent its own standards: open swales as a 2016 “one-off” and a 2025 variance against the engineer’s stormwater advice (4–3).
  • Older and fringe areas pay or wait. Clearspring Greens, approved long ago with ditches, wells and low-pressure sewer, got a pathway only after 78 objections and a 50/50 deal capped at $1,610 a lot (2021); a charity was charged for Acres Drive (2020); a Home Street swale fix was judged too costly (2017). Council paved Park Road West from reserves (2023) and rerouted the Clearspring Greens sewer (2026), and bars new wells and septic, but has no plan for existing well-and-septic areas.

The story in three councils

Under Mayor Chris Goertzen the debates were about standards: when to make exceptions, and who pays for older streets. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council spent years on Clearspring Greens, a low-service subdivision earlier councils approved. His second council faced storms, drainage complaints and neighbours asking for services up front, and answered mostly with new by-laws and later city projects rather than new conditions on developers. The council elected in October 2026 inherits Williams Creek’s development agreement and an unstarted lot grading review.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named; residents, applicants and other members of the public are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words, false starts and mis-heard names (“Lowen” for Loewen, “Windham” for Wyndham). The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every named quote is from a speaker identified with high confidence: from hand-checked speaker maps for meetings from January 2022, and for earlier meetings from how speakers were addressed and from the minutes. One Hampton Village quote (June 20, 2023) is attributed to the City Manager from context, in a voice the speaker map flags as shared with members of the public. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, applicants, developers’ agents and other members of the public are identified only by initials (first and last), including in quote citations; the city’s planning consultant from WSP appears as K.S. Two different residents in these records share the initials G.W. (Wyndham Estate Drive, Aug 4, 2026; Giesbrecht, Sept 1, 2026). Companies and organizations, such as Stonebridge Development Corporation, Goldmark Developments, Penn-Co Construction and Envision Community Living, are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain. Where we trim a remark, leave out a personal passage, or identify a speaker by initials, the full remarks can be watched at the linked moment in the official meeting video.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched for subdivisions, development agreements, grading and drainage, sidewalks and paths, parks and playgrounds, paving, street lights, local improvements, wells, septic, low-pressure sewer and annexed land, and the relevant passages were read in full: transcripts of 243 meeting videos, and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (not yet transcribed) and four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021). Study sessions and committees are closed and unrecorded. The development agreements themselves are not public, so what they require is known only from what was said at meetings.
  • What counts. 79 items: subdivision approvals where services were discussed, design and drainage standards, residents’ complaints about unfinished services, development fees’ sidewalk levy, local improvements on older streets, parks and playgrounds in new areas, street lights for new streets, and wells, septic, roads and garbage service on the fringe and annexed land. Sewer backups are covered in the sewer report and housing supply in the housing report. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes before 2018. There are no minutes online for 2015–2017. Votes were by voice and counts were rarely announced, so who voted which way is taken from what members said in the meeting.
  • Not confirmed. Steinbach’s park dedication rate, current lot grading deposit, development-agreement securities and warranty periods, and any deadline for paving or the final asphalt layer; a written local improvement policy (the 50/50 split is from staff statements in 2016 and 2020); whether the Old Tom Road and Loewen Boulevard lots, paving or green space promised in 2022 had been delivered; the outcome of the Municipal Board hearing on Hanover’s fringe rezoning; whether the Keating Drain cleanout covered the creek a Keating Road delegate described; any answer to the annexed resident’s 2018 garbage-levy request or the Albert Street back lane. The $500 deposit from 2020 that our October 2026 page called a grading deposit is the residential construction-completion deposit.
  • Related litigation. A homeowners’ lawsuit over sewer backups, which includes claims about new subdivisions tied into a strained line, is unproven and is covered in the Sewer backups report.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
