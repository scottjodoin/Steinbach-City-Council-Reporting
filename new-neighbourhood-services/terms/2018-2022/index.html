Services in new neighbourhoods · By term

2018–2022: Mayor Earl Funk’s first council

What did Steinbach City Council say and do about services in new and fringe neighbourhoods from November 2018 to October 2022?

In short

  • Clearspring Greens defined the term. A subdivision approved by earlier councils with ditches, wells and low-pressure sewer and no sidewalks asked for a safe path out. Council first asked owners to pay about 80% (5–2), drew 78 objections, then settled on a 50/50 split capped at $1,610 a lot, the sidewalk fee new lots pay. The path was built in 2021.
  • Who pays was the dividing line. Councillor Siemens dissented for lower resident shares on Clearspring Greens and against charging a charity for Acres Drive; Councillor Susan Penner brokered the 50/50 compromise; Councillors Zwaagstra and Damian Penner argued fairness to other taxpayers.
  • New subdivisions still came with no conditions set at the table. Hampton Village’s last phase was approved over neighbours’ objections about one access and no sidewalks; a concrete-bottom swale in Harmony Lane was left to the developer.
  • Fringe rules were firm. No new wells or septic: council refused a lot split on unserviced Hespeler Street South land and objected to rural lots just over the line in Hanover. Fringe roads (Bush Farm Road, Loewen Boulevard) waited for development to front them.
  • Sidewalks came later. A Brandt Street sidewalk was cut from a 2021 tender for cost, then built in 2022 with sidewalks on McKenzie Avenue and part of Wyndham Estates.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert.

What happened

2018–2019: the rules restated, the fringe held

A month into the term, a Park Road West resident whose land was annexed in January 2018 told council she had paid the $133 garbage levy all year but got no pickup until September. The mayor left it with staff; no credit or answer appears in later records. In April 2019 council objected to the RM of Hanover allowing rural-residential lots just west of the city:

“because it impedes growth of an urban area when you develop lots that are virtually unserviceable by urban services.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Apr 2, 2019 · 0:23:56

Asked at a rezoning hearing whether a new apartment site would worsen drainage nearby, the City Manager stated the rule for all development:

“every developer must manage all water that is generated on site. … the drainage from a building site cannot be any greater or more rapid than what was the case prior to development.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · May 21, 2019 · 0:25:06

Council protected that rule in August 2019, refusing (5–1) a side-yard variance in new Lexington Village because it would crowd the drainage swale between houses. In 2019 it also built the Deerfield detention pond and pathway from city reserves ($588,550), renewed the development fee with its sidewalk levy, and let Goldmark Developments move Lexington Village’s park land with its relocated pond.

In July 2019 a delegation of Clearspring Greens residents asked again for a safe walking route out. They said children could not walk or bike to school safely and that candidates had promised the path.

“Well, we're not stalled. We're working through our budgets. Our 2019 budget did not allow us to do anything in that area at all.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jul 16, 2019 · 1:26:03

2020–2021: Clearspring Greens, from 80/20 to 50/50

In January 2020 council held the hearing on an 850-metre lit asphalt path costing $625,000, with the 146 benefiting lots paying it all (about $4,281 each), and the city paying only for land it bought (about $120,000). The City Manager explained why:

“And this in particular, the rural level of service model does not require the developer to include sidewalks or pathways in its design.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Jan 7, 2020 · 0:14:10

“council, from discussions that have been held to date, is of the opinion that the neighbourhood derives most of the benefit from the pathway and so should be responsible to pay for most of the cost.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Jan 7, 2020 · 0:15:30

Residents packed the hearing; the minutes list 26 objectors heard. Members agreed earlier councils had created the problem, and disagreed about who should pay for it:

“I agree with the residents that this is something that should have been planned for when this rural residential service zoning was approved.”

Councillor Damian Penner, moving the plan · Jan 7, 2020 · 1:26:09

“But this is the first time that we're asking residences from any area to pay the majority of the cost of a pathway to connect them to our community.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, voting against · Jan 7, 2020 · 1:39:10

“The difference here is a difference of opinion over who pays and how much.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jan 7, 2020 · 1:54:31

“I feel for— I feel for the neighbourhood because the level of service does not reflect your tax assessment.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jan 7, 2020 · 2:02:10

A cheaper limestone path without lights, proposed by Councillors Siemens and Jake Hiebert, failed 2–5, and the plan passed 5–2. Two weeks later council moved the $208,000 lighting cost to the city (6–1), cutting each lot’s share to about $2,850. It still drew 78 objections at the Municipal Board. In January 2021 Councillor Susan Penner proposed starting over:

“I would like council to consider doing a new plan for the sidewalk that would see the lights removed from the project and then splitting the cost of the asphalt path 50/50. … This is a fee that was not factored in the price of the lots of Clear Spring Greens when it was developed.”

Councillor Susan Penner · Jan 5, 2021 · 0:35:38

Each lot’s share was capped at $1,610, the sidewalk part of the development fee that new lots paid in 2021. The new plan drew a handful of objections, passed unanimously and was built in 2021. Sixty owners prepaid, so the city borrowed $136,850 instead of $233,450.

“Many councils have tried to implement a pathway here. This is the council that got it done.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Feb 16, 2021 · 0:20:50

2020–2021: roads and frontages

At the Park Hill phase 2 hearing in March 2020, a Bush Farm Road resident asked when the muddy gravel road would be upgraded as traffic grew. The answer set out the rule for fringe roads:

“because there is no development that is actually fronting directly onto Bush Farm Road, there would be no requirement for any improvements to that particular roadway.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Mar 3, 2020 · 0:38:25

Council widened Bush Farm Road’s right-of-way for utilities and stormwater ponds in 2021, but no paving plan followed in this term. In December 2020 council charged the cost of extending Acres Drive in the industrial park by frontage to its two owners: the city’s own development corporation and Envision Community Living, a charity that said it got no benefit and would have to cut services.

“There's never been a time that I'm aware of where the city has ever, in a local improvement plan, carved out a particular property and said this property doesn't have to pay but the other ones do.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Dec 15, 2020 · 0:24:45

“This project, though, building the street benefits all of Steinbach and the CDC.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, voting against · Dec 15, 2020 · 0:29:58

Envision’s share was about $102,876 over five years, by Councillor Zwaagstra’s figures, under its 2007 development agreement. The Municipal Board approved the plan with an amendment, and the road was built in 2022.

2021–2022: Hampton Village, Harmony Lane and the fringe

In August 2021 Stonebridge Development Corporation’s final 33-lot Hampton Village phase drew objections from Wyndham Estate Drive: construction traffic, children walking to the bus with no sidewalks, and 128 lots on a single access although the city’s guideline calls for a second access above 120. The city planner said the guideline is flexible, and that sidewalks were coming but not yet budgeted:

“There will be sidewalks all the way down Windham Estate Drive. They are not scheduled in the capital budget as of yet.”

Lacey Gaudet, City Planner · Aug 17, 2021 · 0:53:35

“We are developing basically at the speed of light, and there's lots of problems with that.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Aug 17, 2021 · 0:54:34

Council approved the phase unanimously; the Municipal Board imposed no conditions. Some Wyndham Estates sidewalks were in the 2022 capital works; the rest came in 2026. The same evening council welcomed Penn-Co’s 139-lot plan west of Clearspring Mall, which its planners said set aside about 16 acres for parks and trails. In October a neighbour asked whether Harmony Lane’s next phase would have a concrete-bottom swale like the last:

“I don't believe so. The previous phase, it was not a city requirement. That was solely a developer Initiated or prompted installation.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Oct 19, 2021 · 0:16:38

In November 2021 council refused to split a 2-acre house lot off unserviced Hespeler Street South land:

“If we allowed this subdivision to go ahead, The next request could potentially be private wells and sewage or field as a sewage. And we don't allow that within our city borders.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Nov 16, 2021 · 0:20:32

The term ended with the Old Tom Road and Loewen Boulevard subdivision, on land annexed in 2018. The applicant promised Loewen Boulevard frontage upgrades; neighbours on the gravel road raised dust, flooding and a first phase with no green space. Council approved it unanimously, subject to a development agreement:

“And some of these things take time because as development comes, we let the development happen and then we can work on the road system.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 6, 2022 · 0:24:23

“You can never put a timeline on development because you develop as there's need.”

Mayor Earl Funk, asked when a later phase would come · Sept 6, 2022 · 1:12:14

Street lights were added street by street as new phases opened, all as routine resolutions with the city paying the yearly Hydro cost (for example, 31 lights in the Langill Way area for $5,472.12 a year).

Where members differed

Five votes on this topic divided council. Three were about who pays for Clearspring Greens; Councillor Siemens was in the minority on all five, usually for a smaller charge on residents.
