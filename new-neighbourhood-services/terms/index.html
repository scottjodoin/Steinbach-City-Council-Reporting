Services in new neighbourhoods · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council handle services in new and fringe neighbourhoods (grading and drainage, sidewalks and paths, parks, paving, street lights, wells and septic, annexed land) between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

The basic model did not change in eleven years. Council approves each subdivision “subject to a development agreement” that staff negotiate; the developer builds roads, pipes and drainage to the city’s standards; new lots pay a development fee that includes a sidewalk levy; and the city adds sidewalks, street lights and often playgrounds later. What changed was the pressure on it. Under Mayor Goertzen the debates were about standards and exceptions. Mayor Funk’s first council spent much of its energy on Clearspring Greens, a low-service subdivision approved long before. His second council faced storms, drainage complaints and neighbours asking for services up front, and answered mostly with by-laws and later city projects rather than new conditions on developers.

Mayor Goertzen’s council

The sidewalk levy, a one-time swale exception, the Home Street swale judged too costly, annexation and septic rules, and a 4–3 vote on park land.

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018. Michael Zwaagstra, who took part in more of these items than any other member and argued against adding conditions “on an ad hoc basis,” and Jac Siemens, who proposed the only sidewalk condition and pressed for lot grading plans and paths, did not run. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), and Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association in his first elected office, are new. Susan Penner, who brokered the Clearspring Greens compromise, is Deputy Mayor.

What the new council inherits:

  • Williams Creek. Its 153-lot first phase had second reading and a conditional subdivision approval in August 2026; third reading and the development agreement (swale, construction access, Loewen Boulevard improvements) were still to come.
  • Unfinished drainage work. A review of the decades-old lot grading policy and a drainage master plan were listed only as possible future projects (March 2026). The climate adaptation plan presented in August 2026 had not been adopted. Land Drainage By-law 2274 now allows inspections and fines.
  • The Manning Canal. The takeover agreement needs provincial cabinet approval; the Old Tom Road and Loewen Boulevard development, approved in 2022, still waited on it.
  • Sidewalks and paths. The active transportation plan (about $11 million) has no budget, and sidewalks in new areas still depend on the city’s capital program. The development fee by-law runs through 2027, when the single-family sidewalk levy reaches $2,055; its renewal is the next chance to change who builds sidewalks.
  • Fringe areas. The Clearspring Greens sewer reroute was due for construction in 2026. No plan exists for the area’s septic tanks and ditches, or for Creekside Drive’s wells. The Municipal Board’s decision on Hanover’s fringe rezoning had not been reported.
  • Open complaints. Wyndham Estate Drive and Ash Grove Lane drainage, and an Albert Street back lane residents say was promised grading, had no recorded answer.

At the inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026, the mayor reappointed Councillor Jake Hiebert to the watershed district board; no other items on this topic came up.
