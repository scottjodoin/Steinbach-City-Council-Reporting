Services in new neighbourhoods · By term

2014–2018: Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council

What did Steinbach City Council say and do about services in new and fringe neighbourhoods (grading and drainage, sidewalks, parks, paving, wells and septic, annexed land) from December 2015 to October 2018?

In short

  • The model was already in place. Subdivisions were approved “subject to a development agreement” with no talk at the table of what the developer must build. New lots paid a sidewalk levy in the development fee, and the city built the sidewalks later, at about 75–80% build-out.
  • Firm on drainage for owners, flexible for one developer. Council refused to waive a homeowner’s drainage plan in 2016 (“we have to stick to our policy”), but the same year let a developer use open swales instead of curb and gutter as a “one-off,” over the City Manager’s and Councillor Zwaagstra’s warnings about precedent.
  • Older and fringe streets waited. A Home Street North swale fix was judged too costly (about $500,000) in 2017; Albert Street and Old Tom Road sidewalk requests went to reports and a future sidewalk plan; Clearspring Greens residents asked for a walking link and were sent to a study session.
  • Annexation brought gravel roads and septic rules. About 3,000 acres and 11 miles of gravel roads joined the city. The new 2018 plan let failing septic fields be replaced only in one reserve area; elsewhere owners must connect to city sewer or use a holding tank, even where no sewer runs.
  • The most divided term. Six split votes, on the swale exception, a lot-line change, paving on gravel roads and closing part of a Harmony Lane green space (4–3).

Council: Mayor Chris Goertzen; Councillors Earl Funk, Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Cari Penner and John Fehr. This record starts in December 2015, the first meeting with a transcript. There are no minutes online before 2018, so earlier votes come from what was said in the meetings.

What happened

2015–2016: renewal, a boundary road and a swale exception

The term’s first local improvement rebuilt about three blocks of Barkman Avenue, an older street, with $1 million borrowed over five years and most of the rest from federal and provincial grants. The mayor presented it as planned renewal:

“this is part of a long-term strategy that we've had and that we've been implementing over the last quite a number of years, and that is to renew our underground infrastructure and making sure that we are ahead of problems rather than reacting to problems.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen · Dec 15, 2015 · 0:09:53

Old Tom Road, a boundary road residents had long complained about, was paved in 2016 at full scope although the tender came in about $200,000 over its $1 million budget. Lyons Gate residents asked for a sidewalk to go in with the paving. Staff said their figure ($700,000–$800,000) would nearly double the project, and the mayor pointed to future pathways once annexed land joined the city:

“if we do look at regional pathways as the friendly annexation process does proceed and there is land that becomes part of the city on the east side of this road allowance, there might be some new opportunities there at that time that we can consider in the longer term.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen · Feb 2, 2016 · 1:22:56

The same spring, Steinbach Land Holding Partners asked to build a commercial subdivision (Steinbach North) with gravity sewer and concrete streets but open swales instead of curb and gutter. Staff warned it lowered the city’s standard toward “a rural profile” and that every developer would expect the same. Council approved it on May 3, 2016 as a one-time exception through the development agreement; Mayor Goertzen did not take part, having declared a perceived conflict.

“I feel that city specs need to be relaxed. I believe it's in the best interests of the city to relax our specs and allow this development to go forward.”

Councillor Cari Penner, seconding · May 3, 2016 · 0:21:25

“The question is, are we prepared to say that in our commercial zoning, that from here on in, we're now saying that open ditches is now standard practice in the city? … It creates a precedent.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, voting against · May 3, 2016 · 0:22:37

For individual owners, council held the line. In September 2016 a homeowner asked to be excused from a $2,000–$3,000 drainage plan required for a shed variance. Council said the policy stands:

“the water that we don't take care of here becomes someone else's problem, and that's why we have these development plans. … As much as I want to help, we have to stick to our policy, and I just encourage council to do that today.”

Councillor Earl Funk · Sept 20, 2016 · 0:25:23

In the fall council renewed the development fees to 2019, rising 2.5% a year. Councillors explained that the residential fee includes a sidewalk levy, so new neighbourhoods get sidewalks while older streets wait. An Albert Street resident’s sidewalk request the same night was sent for a report under the older formula: half paid by the owners who benefit, half by the city.

“And we do have some established neighborhoods like this one that don't have sidewalks, and we're gradually sort of making a difference in that in terms of putting in sidewalks in as many places as we can. And obviously for future neighborhoods, this is not an issue because this now builds into development fee, those fees that we're charging that we just talked about.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Oct 4, 2016 · 0:22:10

No Albert Street report or sidewalk followed in this term. In 2016 the mayor also confirmed that new subdivisions must keep “net zero outflow” of water, after a golf club said development was sending more water through its course; no city works were decided.

2017: the Home Street swale, gravel roads and annexation

Home Street North residents had written in December 2015 and brought a petition in February 2017 about an open swale that sends water and ice across their street, in an area the city took over from Hanover decades ago. Council asked for costed options. In December the answer was no:

“this is something that we inherited from Hanover when we took over that area. It's designed the way it is. We wouldn't design it that way now, but it is how it is, and to correct it would be over $1 million to correct.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:27:59

The City Manager had put the crossing at about $500,000 plus property purchases; the mayor’s “over $1 million” is his own summary. The city would monitor and maintain the spot until the street is someday rebuilt. The same year council built a 500-metre sidewalk on Clearwater Avenue for students (estimated $185,000), renewed playgrounds in older parks with provincial grants, and began a run of decisions excusing businesses on gravel roads, such as Park Road West, from paving their parking. The province approved the annexation of land from the RM of Hanover, adding just under 3,000 acres.

2018: sidewalks after build-out, septic rules and a green space

Council’s 2018 neighbourhood sidewalk program reached newer streets (Lyndhurst Gate, Brighton Lane, Park Hill Crescent, Kroeker Avenue). When owners on Lyndhurst Gate asked to move the new sidewalk away from their house, council left it where the standard put it. A month later Councillor Fehr asked how sidewalks are planned:

“when we approve these subdivisions, do we know where the sidewalks go? I'm trying to avoid What happened about a month ago regarding the sidewalk.”

Councillor John Fehr · Jun 5, 2018 · 0:07:45

“the city does collect capital development fees from developers with the understanding that a portion of those fees are allocated for sidewalk development. However, due to the high level of construction activity that goes on, those sidewalks typically do not get installed in subdivisions until it's more or less 75 to 80% complete.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Jun 5, 2018 · 0:08:21

That night council also chose, 4–2, to require only an all-weather surface for parking where a site faces an unpaved road. Councillor Funk called it “like for like”; the mayor and Councillor Cari Penner voted against.

“I think going away from the high standard that we have is a mistake, and so I will not support this.”

Mayor Chris Goertzen · Jun 5, 2018 · 1:12:39

A Clearspring Greens resident asked for sidewalks and walking paths out of the low-service subdivision, which has ditches, wells and low-pressure sewer. Councillor Siemens agreed the area had no safe link; the request went to a closed study session, and no decision came before the election.

“I think, Mr. Mayor, this has been one of the pieces of our community that doesn't have access by foot or by bike unless it's on a narrow asphalt highway or, well, highway, a road.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Jun 5, 2018 · 1:19:15

The new Official Community Plan and Zoning By-law 2100, adopted in October 2018 mainly for the annexed land, let farming continue and limited septic-field replacement to one area. A Hespeler Street South owner learned what that means:

“City of Steinbach official community plan policies do not permit the replacement of existing septic fields, and that then would prompt the need for either connection to city sewer Or the installation of a holding tank.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Sept 4, 2018 · 0:10:59

City sewer does not run down that part of Hespeler Street. The term ended with a 4–3 vote to close and reconfigure part of a Harmony Lane public reserve so a new ownership group’s lots would need fewer variances. Two Chrysler Gate buyers said city maps had shown the green space behind them.

“this was the only place that we could get feedback from the general public, from the people who live in that area. I think we need to hear their voices.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, voting against · Oct 16, 2018 · 1:16:21

“In terms of proper planning, we want to have fewer variances and have it developed properly.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving · Oct 16, 2018 · 1:13:47
