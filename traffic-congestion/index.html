Steinbach City Council · Dec 2015 – Nov 2026 · Topic: Traffic and congestion

Traffic in Steinbach: what can council do, and what has it done?

What has Steinbach City Council done since December 2015 about traffic congestion and road safety on Main Street, Highways 12 and 52 and city streets? What reasons has it given for not doing more, how did each council member act, and how do other growing municipalities manage traffic?

Written for residents. Start with the summary, then choose what to read. Last updated Nov 7, 2026.

Executive summary

Steinbach’s worst traffic is on roads the city does not control, and for eleven years council’s answer has been the same: count the traffic, ask the province, share the cost, and leave its own streets to engineering rules and the police.

  • The busiest roads are provincial. Main Street is Provincial Trunk Highway 52 and Brandt Street is PTH 12. The province decides on their signals, signal timing, crossings and speed limits. Council’s tools are a traffic count it has paid for every year since 1997, letters to the province, and paying a share of upgrades.
  • Those tools worked, slowly. Signals at Penfeld/Acres Drive were built in 2016 under a shared PTH 12 program. Industrial Road and PTH 52 got signals only after four straight years of counts showed they were needed (approved 2018). Loewen Boulevard and PTH 12, flagged as the worst corner from 2016, was designed in 2021 and rebuilt in 2024–25 for about $11.2 million, about $6.1 million of it the city’s. Signals at Giesbrecht Street and PTH 52, requested in February 2022, were operating by October 2026.
  • On city streets, the engineering warrant usually wins. Residents’ requests for stop signs and traffic calming were nearly always refused because counts didn’t justify them. The exceptions: a four-way stop at Barkman and Fourth (2018, against staff advice) and a 30 km/h school zone at Southwood School (2025). A three-way stop at Loewen and Hespeler lost 3–4 in 2024; the roundabout the mayor prefers is not budgeted.
  • Council’s own initiatives were enforcement and speed rules. After complaints about street racing it paid for a dedicated RCMP traffic officer from 2022 (about $200,000 a year, the main reason for that year’s tax increase) and backed joint patrols with the Ste. Anne police in 2025. When the province handed over speed limits on city streets in 2019, council passed its own by-law that summer and lowered several.
  • No plan for the traffic that growth brings. There is no transportation master plan; staff said in 2018 one was wanted “within the future.” Traffic studies are required only near the highways, so residents asking for them at rezonings were turned down. The 2025 Active Transportation Plan was accepted but has no funding.

The story in three councils

Mayor Chris Goertzen’s council finished a cost-shared rebuild of PTH 12 intersections and waited years for the province on Industrial Road. Mayor Earl Funk’s first council got control of speed limits on city streets, hired a traffic officer and won a deal to rebuild Loewen Boulevard. His second council saw that rebuild and the Giesbrecht signals through, and divided most over residents’ stop-sign requests. The bypass question is covered in a separate report.

About this report

Quotes are verbatim from automatic transcripts and link to the meeting videos. Council members, the mayor and staff are named; residents are identified by initials. The analysis and plain-language summaries are ours.

Methods, coverage and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Transcripts. Meeting quotes come from automatic transcripts of the official videos and are left as transcribed, including repeated words, false starts and misheard street names (“Lowen” is Loewen, “Geesebrecht” or “Giesburg” is Giesbrecht, “Brant” is Brandt, “Penfield” is Penfeld). The one exception is the city’s name, which the transcription often misspells and which quotes show correctly as Steinbach. Ellipses mark trimmed text; no sentence breaks were added. Each quote links to the start of the speaker’s turn in the video; check there before quoting elsewhere.
  • Speakers and names. Every quote from a council member or staff is from a speaker identified with high confidence: from hand-checked speaker maps for meetings from January 2022, and for earlier meetings from how speakers were addressed and from the minutes. Council members, the mayor and city staff are named in full. Residents, delegations and other members of the public, including the RCMP detachment commanders who report to council, are identified only by initials. Organizations and businesses are named. In 2015–2017 the two Councillors Penner often cannot be told apart in the recordings, and their remarks are not attributed unless certain.
  • Coverage. Every council meeting from December 15, 2015 to November 3, 2026 with a recording or minutes was searched: transcripts of 243 meeting videos and minutes from January 2018 on (the city’s older minutes are not online). Not covered: the first four 2015 videos (not yet transcribed); four videos that failed to download (April 4 and October 17, 2017; September 15, 2020; July 6, 2021), where the minutes were used if they exist; and the April 5, 2022 meeting, which has no audio. Committee meetings (such as the RCMP advisory committee) and budget study sessions are not recorded.
  • What counts. 103 items: the provincial highways through the city, city-street intersections, stop signs, school zones and speed limits, traffic enforcement, residents’ traffic requests and the answers they got, and traffic raised at rezoning hearings. The Main Street bypass is covered in its own report, public transit in Can Steinbach have transit?, and policing generally in Crime and policing. Classifying how an item ended (done, partly, no public result, declined) and tagging council’s reasons is our judgment; the reasons are recorded for each item in the data.
  • Votes. Council votes on traffic were almost all unanimous. Before 2018 there are no minutes online; votes were by voice, so who supported what is taken from what members said. Several of the 13 split votes in this record are on rezonings or a pathway levy where traffic was one reason among others; each is explained where it appears.
  • Figures. The Loewen Boulevard and PTH 12 cost changed over time: an $8.275 million estimate (2021), a $9.98 million contract (2024), a “$14 million intersection” in the mayor’s words (2025), and about $11.2 million in the end, with about $5.1 million from the province and $6.1 million from the city, of which $3 million was borrowed over five years at 3.83% (SteinbachOnline). We use the final figure. The cost of the Giesbrecht signals has not been published, and SteinbachOnline reported them operating on October 13, 2026 without giving a switch-on date.
  • Not confirmed. Any provincial reply to council’s 2021 request for red-light cameras or its 2022 request for “no engine brake” signs on the highways; the outcome of the 2024 request for flashing crosswalk lights on Main and Brandt; whether the province has retimed the three PTH 12 intersections named in the 2025 count; the by-law amendment for the Southwood School zone; the start year of Steinbach’s 30 km/h school zones; and any collision data for the city after 2017.
  • Data. The data downloads list every item, every council member’s part in it, every split vote, and every quote with its timestamp and link. Quotes in the downloads come only from council members and staff identified with high confidence. Every quote was checked word for word against its transcript.
  • Council change. The council elected in October 2026 held its inaugural meeting on November 3, 2026. Quotes are from members serving at the time, some of whom are no longer on council. Election results are from SteinbachOnline (unofficial counts).
