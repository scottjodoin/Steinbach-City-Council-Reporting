Traffic and congestion · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons has Steinbach City Council given since 2015 for not doing more about traffic congestion and road safety, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given the same eight reasons for a decade. The first two are real limits: the province does control the busiest roads and does decide when to act. The next two, engineering warrants and police enforcement, are standard practice and kept council from deciding by anecdote. The weakness is in how the reasons fit together. On the highways council waits for the province; on city streets it waits for warrants, development or a budget; and nobody is charged with planning ahead for the traffic that growth brings.

1It’s a provincial highway

“Obviously, this is not directly within city jurisdiction because this is a provincial highway, but I think that by the city adding its voice to the request and letting the province know about it, and also the fact that this will also get into the news, I think these are all good, good ways of adding profile to the request.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on flashing crosswalk lights · Feb 20, 2024 · 0:26:33

“the province is responsible for everything above ground, the city is responsible for everything underground, because those are our utilities.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on the Loewen Boulevard rebuild · May 21, 2024 · 0:23:00

“the provincial trunk highways, if there are signals located on them, their department policy is, for instance, not to permit flashing yellows at signalized intersections.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Mar 21, 2023 · 0:41:08
Context

The jurisdiction is real. Under Manitoba’s Transportation Infrastructure Act the province runs PTH 12 (Brandt Street) and PTH 52 (Main Street), including where they cross city streets; it decides on signals, signal timing, crossings and, through the Highway Traffic Board, speed limits (Transportation Infrastructure Act; Highway Traffic Act). Photo enforcement is allowed only in Winnipeg. But the same law lets the province and a city share costs by agreement, which is how the PTH 12 intersections were rebuilt in 2014–2016 and Loewen Boulevard in 2024–25. Selkirk and Portage la Prairie have rebuilt their highway main streets the same way (How Steinbach compares). The highway label also reaches city streets: because the city’s planning policy asks for traffic impact studies only where development affects PTH 12 or 52, residents asking for studies on city streets have been told none is required (reason 5).

Context

The waits were long. Industrial Road and PTH 52: signals warranted in every count from 2014, requested January 2017, approved March 2018, operating by early 2020. Loewen Boulevard and PTH 12: named the signalized corner most in need in January 2016, the city’s priority to the minister in 2019, a deal in 2021, rebuilt 2024–25. Giesbrecht Street and PTH 52: requested February 2022 for 2023, “scheduled for 2024,” operating by October 2026 (SteinbachOnline). Smaller requests got no recorded answer: crosswalks on PTH 12 (asked 2017), “no engine brake” signs (2022), red-light cameras (2021) and flashing crosswalk lights on Main and Brandt (2024). The resident who asked for those lights, B.B., told council that after three years there was still no answer from the province: “I probably never will” (Feb 20, 2024, 0:23:30). Waiting also shaped local decisions. In 2022 council approved apartments beside Giesbrecht and PTH 52 over a Loewen Windows representative’s warning (J.S.) that “Traffic is frequently backed up and stopped on the highway and on Geestryck Street, causing a safety concern.” (Nov 1, 2022, 0:29:38), on the expectation that the signals would come first. Council has no standing agreement with the province on the two corridors, only project-by-project requests; the one quick result, the 2023 signal retiming, came four months after council pressed in person.

Mayor Earl Funk, on the new community plan · Apr 1, 2025 · 0:45:23

Councillor Jac Siemens, on the Active Transportation Plan · May 6, 2025 · 0:58:35
Context

The city’s yearly count measures turning movements at highway intersections; it does not track where trips start and end, through traffic or trucks, so the claim that traffic “comes to” rather than through Steinbach is a reading of the counts, not a measurement. The province said in 2018 it had no plans for a bypass. The province’s short counts (not adjusted for season) show the fastest growth on Main Street west of Brandt, up about 71% from 2008 to 2024, which fits commuters coming in from the west, as the mayor described at Giesbrecht. If most trips do start or end in Steinbach, the remaining tools are signal timing, more connecting streets, and ways to make short trips without a car. The 2025 plan is a first step on the last of these, but leaders framed it as secondary to driving. For the bypass question in depth, see A bypass around Main Street?; for buses, Can Steinbach have transit?
