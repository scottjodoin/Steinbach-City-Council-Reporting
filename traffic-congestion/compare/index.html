Traffic and congestion · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How do other growing municipalities with provincial highways through town plan for traffic, share highway costs, set speed limits, calm neighbourhood streets and enforce the rules, and what would “doing all it can” add in Steinbach?

In short

  • Strong on data and enforcement. Steinbach pays for its own yearly count of the highway intersections, which no law requires, and funds a dedicated traffic officer. Its cost-shared highway rebuilds are typical of what peers do with the province.
  • Weak on planning. Steinbach has no transportation master plan, no traffic-calming policy, no roundabout in its budget and no traffic studies for development on city streets. Most Manitoba peers lack a master plan too; the clearest examples are Cochrane, Alberta (2017), and Brandon’s older road plan written with the province (2007).
  • Slow results are a Manitoba-wide problem, but Niverville and Portage la Prairie have negotiated whole-corridor projects with the province (and, for Portage, Ottawa), not one intersection at a time.

Key findings

1On the highways, council uses every tool it has, and waits

The province really does control PTH 12 and 52, and council has used what it has: its own counts, letters, public pressure and cost-sharing. That produced the PTH 12 program, the Industrial Road signals, the 2023 retiming and the Loewen rebuild. But each big ask took three to six years, and council has no standing agreement with the province on the two corridors, only requests one project at a time. Niverville’s PR 311 deal (a rebuild, a roundabout, new signals and a transfer of the road) shows a bigger package is possible. See reasons 1 and 2.

2On the streets it controls, there’s no plan

For city streets, decisions are made one complaint at a time: a warrant check, a referral to the RCMP, a stop sign usually refused. Engineering warrants are good practice, but they measure volume and delay, not speed or a school nearby, and there is no calming policy or budget to answer the complaints they miss. A master plan would say in advance which intersections get upgraded, when and who pays, including the Loewen–Hespeler roundabout the mayor wants. See reasons 3, 6 and 7.

3Growth adds traffic before roads catch up

Council’s approach is to let development come first and fix roads afterwards. Each building adds few cars, but traffic studies are required only near the highways, so the combined effect on streets like First Street, McKenzie Avenue and Loewen Boulevard is never studied before approval. Brandon now charges developers specifically for road and intersection upgrades. For how much the city is expected to grow, see Planning for the future. See reason 5.

4Enforcement is the strongest local tool, but not a reliable one

Council chose to pay for a dedicated traffic officer, and credits it with less Main Street racing. But the officer is often pulled to other calls, the detachment runs short, and Manitoba law rules out photo radar outside Winnipeg. Street design works when no officer is there; Steinbach has no policy for it. See reason 4.

5“Traffic comes to Steinbach” has not been measured

Council uses its counts to argue that traffic comes to the city rather than through it, but the counts record turns at intersections, not where trips start and end, and there are no truck counts. The province’s own counts show the fastest growth on Main Street west of Brandt. An origin–destination study, which Brandon did with the province, would test the claim behind both the bypass debate and the case for walking and cycling. See reason 8 and A bypass around Main Street?

What “doing all it can” would add

  • A transportation master plan for city streets and both highway corridors, prepared with the province and sized for the growth the city’s community plan expects, so upgrades are scheduled rather than reactive.
  • A corridor agreement with the province for PTH 12 and 52: signal coordination, the three retimings the 2025 count recommended, the Superstore/Co-op access and the crosswalk lights, with dates the city can report on.
  • Publish the yearly traffic count on the city website with the province’s answer to each recommendation, and add origin–destination and truck counts to test whether traffic comes to Steinbach or through it.
  • Decide on Loewen and Hespeler: put the roundabout in the capital plan with a year and cost, or adopt an interim measure.
  • Require traffic studies for larger developments on city collectors and arterials, not only near the highways, and consider a road charge on development as Brandon and Cochrane have.
  • A traffic-calming policy with criteria that count speed and school areas as well as volume, and a small yearly budget, so residents’ requests aren’t decided only by stop-sign warrants.
  • Fund early Active Transportation Plan items, such as the PTH 12 crossing near McKenzie Avenue, and apply to the federal active transportation fund.
  • Report back on referrals, starting with the Park Hill School parents’ safety request.
