Traffic and congestion · By term

The story by term

How did Steinbach City Council’s approach to traffic and road safety change from one council to the next between December 2015 and November 2026, and what does the new council inherit?

In short

The method stayed the same across all three councils: pay for a yearly count of the highway intersections, send it to the province with a request, share the cost when the province agrees, and on city streets follow the engineering warrant. What changed was the pressure. Traffic kept growing, each big ask of the province took three to six years from request to result, and residents’ complaints moved from the highways to their own streets. Council’s answer to the second kind of complaint was mostly enforcement: a traffic officer from 2022, joint patrols from 2025.

2014–2018

Mayor Goertzen’s council

A shared PTH 12 program finished; Industrial Road signals after four years of warrants; a four-way stop against staff advice; Loewen Boulevard named the worst corner.

33 items · 1 split vote

2018–2022

Mayor Funk’s first council

City speed limits, a traffic officer for street racing, the Loewen rebuild financed, Giesbrecht signals requested, infill approved without traffic studies.

27 items · 6 split votes, mostly on who pays or rezonings

2022–2026

Mayor Funk’s second council

Signal timing fixed, a stop sign defeated 3–4, Loewen rebuilt, a school zone beyond staff advice, “the automobile city,” and the Giesbrecht lights at last.

42 items · 6 split votes

2026–2030: the new council

The council elected on October 28, 2026 is the first change in membership since 2018, and it loses council’s two most active voices on traffic. Michael Zwaagstra moved most of the traffic counts, the Loewen rebuild and the traffic officer, and chaired the RCMP advisory committee; Jac Siemens brought the most residents’ complaints to the table and lobbied ministers on highways through the Eastman regional committee. Neither ran. At the November 3 inaugural meeting the mayor named Damian Penner to the RCMP advisory committee and Jake Hiebert to the Eastman committee. Ron Falk, a Hanover School Division trustee for 34 years (14 as chair), and Greg Carlos, a past president of the South Eastman Filipino Association, have no record on this topic yet. Susan Penner is Deputy Mayor.

What the new council inherits:

  • PTH 12 signal timing. The 2025 count recommended retiming three PTH 12 intersections (Clearspring/Golf, Park Road and Superstore/Co-op). Only the province can do it, and no result had been reported by November 2026.
  • Loewen Boulevard and Hespeler Street. The three-way stop lost 3–4 in 2024; the mayor’s preferred roundabout is in no published budget.
  • School-area requests. The Park Hill School parents’ traffic-safety request (April 2026) was still waiting for a staff report; the by-law amendment for the Southwood School zone approved in 2025 was not found in the minutes.
  • Requests still with the province. Flashing crosswalk lights on Main and Brandt (2024), and possible signals at PTH 52 and Lund Road, which the 2025 count said to monitor. The province’s own 2026–2030 list includes improvements at PTH 12 and PTH 52 (Brandt and Main); its cost and timing are not confirmed.
  • A plan. No transportation master plan, no traffic-calming policy, and an Active Transportation Plan with no funding attached.

The inaugural meeting had no traffic items on its agenda.
