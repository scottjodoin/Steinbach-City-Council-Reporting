Traffic and congestion · By term

2018–2022: Mayor Earl Funk’s first council

What did Steinbach City Council do about traffic and road safety from November 2018 to October 2022, and where did its members disagree?

In short

  • Speed limits came home. When the province gave cities control of speed limits on their own streets, council passed By-law 2123 in 2019, lowering Old Tom Road to 60 km/h and some annexed roads from 90 to 70.
  • Loewen Boulevard finally got a deal. After the count showed a 9.5-minute wait to turn left, council asked the province to make the corner a priority (2020), hired a designer (2021) and borrowed $3.5 million toward an $8.3 million rebuild, with the province paying most of the rest. Construction then slipped to 2024.
  • Street racing brought a traffic officer. After many complaints, council asked in 2021 for an 18th RCMP officer dedicated to traffic (about $200,000 a year) and for the power to use red-light cameras. The officer started in May 2022; the cameras never came.
  • Neighbourhood complaints went to staff and police. A four-way stop at McKenzie and Giesbrecht became a push-button crossing; speeding was “an RCMP issue”; and requests for traffic studies before more apartments were refused because policy only calls for them near the highways.
  • Members split over who pays and over rezonings, not over the highway asks: Jac Siemens and Jake Hiebert cast most of the dissents.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert.

What happened

2019: the city sets its own speed limits

Provincial changes required every municipality to pass a maximum speed limits by-law by September 1, 2019. PTH 12 and 52 stayed with the province. Council used the change to lower limits on roads that growth had made busier:

“I think it's appropriate for the amount of traffic we have on our roads, and I'm also very glad that the province has given us this jurisdiction that we can do this.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on By-law 2123 · Aug 6, 2019 · 0:22:49

Old Tom Road was one of them. Clearspring Greens residents, about 100 of whom came to council in July 2019, said paving it had turned Old Tom and Park Road into a fast route around the city with no safe way to walk in. Council added streetlights and the lower limit, then divided over who should pay for a pathway (see the votes below).

2020: a new worst corner

The 2019 count, presented in February 2020, showed what years of PTH 12 work had left behind:

“So what were previously our most congested and most poorly performing intersections were improved, and by knocking off the worst ones, we now have a new worst one.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Feb 18, 2020 · 0:38:39

“I just calculated the minutes here, about, it's about 9.5 minutes of waiting. That's a long time to be waiting to turn left.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on Loewen Boulevard at PTH 12 · Feb 18, 2020 · 0:42:12

Council forwarded the count and asked the province to make Loewen a priority (R20-034). On the city’s own edge roads, a Bush Farm Road resident asked when the gravel road carrying cross-town traffic would be upgraded; staff said upgrades come only when development fronts a road. The mayor promised a plan, which did not come to a public meeting:

“I think we need to be looking at this and any— in the, in the future, come up with a plan for how we will mitigate these, these, these roads and how we're going to make sure that these roads will handle the traffic, because we are growing.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Mar 3, 2020 · 0:50:45

A resident’s request for a four-way stop at McKenzie Avenue and Giesbrecht Street was counted; traffic did not warrant a stop, but pedestrians warranted a push-button crossing, which council approved in November. A new Traffic By-law 2164 kept residents’ right to ask council for traffic control devices.

2021: the Loewen deal and street racing

Council hired WSP to design the Loewen Boulevard and PTH 12 rebuild ($414,302) and passed a by-law to borrow $3.5 million over five years toward an $8.275 million estimate, with $4.545 million from the province and other sources. The mayor said he had named it the city’s priority to the infrastructure minister in 2019.

In September, after letters and calls about racing on Main Street, council voted unanimously for a dedicated traffic officer:

“they're not going to sit on Main Street waiting for to see if they're going to catch more people. … An additional RCMP officer would be about $200,000 a year. It's not an insignificant cost.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, moving the request · Sept 7, 2021 · 1:00:47

“as the staff sergeant in charge of Steinbach 14 years ago or so, I approached council at that time for a full-time member to do traffic in the city.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert · Sept 7, 2021 · 1:08:07

The same motion asked the province for authority to use red-light cameras on Main Street. Members said they disliked cameras but wanted the option; no reply was ever reported.

Traffic from new housing was handled differently. Asked when a traffic impact study is required for an apartment rezoning on First Street, staff said there is no set trigger; the city may ask for one near a provincial highway. Council added 12 units without a study, 6–1:

“And I wonder at what point do we actually stop multifamily dwellings in that area for the purposes of getting a proper traffic study done. … So again, my objection is subject to a traffic study. I think that development has to cease.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, voting against · Sept 7, 2021 · 0:37:37

2022: Giesbrecht Street, and a plan that wasn’t there

The October 2021 count showed signals warranted at PTH 52 West and Giesbrecht Street for several years running. In February 2022 council asked the province to install them in its 2023 program (R22-038):

“Anyone who's ever driven on Giesburg Street and tried to exit onto Highway 52 particularly during a busy time, knows how incredibly difficult that is to make that turn.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Feb 15, 2022 · 0:22:24

The traffic officer started full time in May, and paid for most of that year’s tax increase:

“There is a small tax increase of 0.4 mills, and that is mainly because of the extra RCMP officer that we're putting in place for this year in order to deal with traffic issues in our city.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · May 3, 2022 · 0:04:56

Council also asked the province for “no engine brake” signs on all four highways into the city (no installation was reported) and, against staff advice, banned weekday parking on Cottonwood Bend after complaints of racing. In September, residents at the hearing for a 95-lot neighbourhood at Old Tom Road and Loewen Boulevard asked what was planned for its intersections and whether any traffic study had been done:

“And some of these things take time because as development comes, we let the development happen and then we can work on the road system.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Sept 6, 2022 · 0:24:23

“But specifically to Old Tom and Lowen Boulevard, it's been talked about, it's part of our long-term planning strategies. There's not a definitive plan.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Sept 6, 2022 · 1:19:40
Context

The neighbourhood was approved unanimously. A Loewen Boulevard resident of almost 50 years, D.E., had asked for a traffic count taken when the road was not closed for construction, because he could hardly leave his driveway at rush hour. Two months later council advanced apartments beside PTH 52 and Giesbrecht 5–2 over objections from Loewen Windows that traffic already backed up there; Councillor Damian Penner said the requested signals would be up “well in advance” of the building. The province did not fund them in 2023 or 2024, and they were operating by October 2026.

Where members differed

Every request to the province, the Loewen borrowing and the traffic officer passed unanimously. The splits were over who pays for the Clearspring Greens pathway, a school in the industrial park (refused partly over truck traffic), a building beside the Loewen turn, and adding apartments on First Street without a traffic study.
