Behind closed doors · By term

2022–2026: Mayor Earl Funk’s second council

From November 2022 to October 2026, how much of Steinbach City Council’s work happened in committee, study sessions and closed meetings, and how did the public decisions relate to what was discussed there?

In short

  • Committee first, a short vote after. Policies, standards, by-laws and an event centre loan were worked through at the Strategic Priorities Committee (SPC) or “committee meetings”, where staff took council’s “direction” or found changes “generally agreed to”. In public most passed unanimously with brief comment.
  • One clear case the other way. In 2023 council cut the development-fee increase discussed at SPC from 7.5% to 5% a year, in a public 5–2 vote with reasons on both sides, and a councillor put the committee’s four options on the record.
  • Money is decided at study sessions. Every budget, including the 2026 “flood” budget built over two days in November 2025, and most groups’ funding requests were settled there; the public sees one unanimous vote after the hearing.
  • Gaps in the record. Five major items were decided on documents council received that day; closed meetings were held after adjournment in March and July 2026 with nothing in the minutes; and a $200,000 grant decision was sent to an August 2026 committee meeting, after which no public resolution was found.
  • Good practice and a denial. The mayor disclosed a meeting with a developer, staff said “resolutions are better” than consensus, and every regular meeting was livestreamed. When a resident suggested decisions favour insiders, the mayor and deputy mayor rejected it; the vote record for this term supports them on developers’ files (the four tests).

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra, Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert, all re-elected in 2022.

What happened

2022–2023: the routine, and the development fee exception

Two weeks into the term, a councillor gave viewers an unusually open account of the private side of the job:

“This past week, Council had a 3-hour orientation session where our roles and responsibilities were reviewed. The upcoming— this upcoming weekend, Council will meet for a day and a half for our fall study session to determine spending priorities for the city.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert, opening remarks · Nov 15, 2022 · 0:00:16

The priorities set there appeared in the 2023 financial plan, adopted unanimously in March after a hearing. The mayor described the work behind it:

“I know we've all worked on it. We've discussed this very to the point. Very long meetings we've had discussing this. and we spent weekends on it.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on the 2023 budget · Mar 21, 2023 · 0:25:28

Through 2023 the council expense policy and the asset management policy came from SPC; the mayor said reviewing policies and by-laws was one of SPC’s “main goals”, and staff said the asset management policy was back in public only because a federal grant required a resolution “in public meeting”. A minor hockey funding request went to the November study session by consensus.

Development fees were the exception. Staff presented options at the September 12, 2023 SPC meeting and recommended a larger increase; the by-law came to council at 7.5% a year. At first reading Councillor Zwaagstra moved 5% instead:

“I realize that initially at the SPC meeting, 7.5% was discussed, but that's not a final decision at that point. It comes to council and we've all had time to reflect on it.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:17:50

“When this was brought to SPC, administration gave us 4 options, 1 option to consider.”

Councillor Damian Penner, voting against the 5% version · Nov 7, 2023 · 0:06:44

Councillor Damian Penner went on to say a staff note at SPC put the increase needed to restore past contributions at 11% a year. The 5% by-law passed 5–2 at every reading, Councillors Damian Penner and Jake Hiebert against. The case cuts both ways: the final decision was made in public, against the figure favoured in private, which is the opposite of a pre-arranged vote; but the public never heard the committee’s discussion, and one councillor described the figure discussed there as council’s recommendation to administration, in effect direction to staff. The same day, at its organizational meeting, council reviewed Procedures By-law 1809, which closes every study session, and accepted it unchanged.

2024–2025: agreed at committee, delivered the same day

In 2024 the city’s design and construction standards for new development came to council already settled:

“The updates that were discussed and generally agreed to by council are now presented here for your information.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Feb 6, 2024 · 0:06:08

The details the city engineer presented at committee “have not been included again” in the public package, staff said, because council had already reviewed them. The sponsorship and naming-rights policy was drafted from council’s “requests and recommendations” at a February committee meeting, as the minutes themselves record; a provincial community safety presentation was given to SPC rather than a public meeting; and a supplier’s bankruptcy first raised at SPC was then reported in public with questions answered.

Councillor Susan Penner · Mar 4, 2025 · 0:18:14

She also asked for the Chamber of Commerce’s business-tax grant to be reviewed at the study session; no public result was found. In October 2025 the mayor set an example of disclosure:

“Yeah, council, I have— I've met with the developer. It's a bit of a unique development that they're looking at, and I would really like to hear from the public to see what their thoughts are in the neighborhood.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on a 36-unit rezoning at 635–641 Main Street · Oct 21, 2025 · 0:50:30

The related parking variance, which staff recommended denying, was approved 5–2 after a long public debate.

2026: the flood budget, closed meetings and a grant sent to committee

After the September 2025 storm, residents’ drainage questions were sent to the November study session; even a senior manager said he was “not privy to that agenda”. The 2026 budget, with a $35.8 million capital program, was adopted unanimously in March:

“I want to go back to the November meeting of council, the study session where we talked about flood mitigation. We spent many hours over those 2 days talking about flood mitigation and what it would look like.”

Councillor Jac Siemens, on the 2026 budget · Mar 17, 2026 · 0:46:30

Asked by a resident at the hearing why a sewer reroute was chosen, the mayor answered: “It was the direction of council” and “as council discussed it, it kind of made sense” (0:36:24). The study session’s options and trade-offs were not made public. That night ended with an announcement after adjournment, repeated in July:

“There's a closed meeting to, uh, to follow council”

Mayor Earl Funk · Mar 17, 2026 · 2:01:10

On July 7 the mayor asked the City Manager after adjournment, “is it still necessary to have a closed meeting?” (0:57:40). Closed meetings are lawful for staff, legal and negotiating matters; but neither set of minutes records a resolution to close, the legal ground or the general nature of the matter, as the Municipal Act and the city’s own Procedures By-law require.

A $400,000 loan to the event centre’s operator was introduced in June as “a file that has been discussed previously at committee”, where the centre had explained its finances; the amount was not said aloud at first reading, but at final readings Councillor Zwaagstra set out the terms in public: $400,000, repaid over four years. In May, when a request first made “at committee meeting” reached council, he asked whether a resolution or a consensus was needed:

“Consensus is always Good, but resolutions are better.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · May 5, 2026 · 0:17:17

In July a $200,000 provincial public safety grant came back for a decision. Staff proposed $65,000 for a community safety officer’s truck and $135,000 for security cameras:

“while administration would typically refer a discussion like this to SPC, we don't have a meeting for SPC until September.”

Adam Thiessen, Senior Manager, Corporate Services · July 21, 2026 · 1:22:18

“Yeah, my preference would be if we could have a discussion on this at an SPC or something like that. Just to further understand the rationale.”

Councillor Susan Penner · July 21, 2026 · 1:29:21

The City Manager pointed out that it could simply come back to one of two regular meetings in August. Council chose August 11 instead, which the mayor called “a special meeting of council” and Councillor Zwaagstra “an SPC meeting”. The minutes record: “Consensus of council was to defer the decision until a Strategic Priority Committee meeting could be held on August 11, 2026.” The city’s calendar has no entry for August 11; no resolution on the grant appears in the minutes from August 4 to October 6; and resolution numbers run without a gap from August 4 to August 18, so no numbered council resolution was passed on August 11. If the matter was settled at a committee with no council resolution, it would conflict with the Act’s rule that council acts only by resolution at a council meeting. This could not be confirmed either way.

At the Bridgepark Manor hearing the same night, a resident, M.R., said Steinbach has “a culture of helping out the guy you know and passing things”, adding she was not commenting on that project. The allegation is unproven. The mayor replied at once:

“to, to insinuate those kind of things is actual speculation and It's, it's not what goes on.”

Mayor Earl Funk · July 21, 2026 · 0:35:46

Council set parking at 1.25 stalls a unit rather than the 1.0 the developer sought, and approved the variance 6–1. At the term’s last meeting in October, two more funding requests were sent to the new council’s study session “because it's a budget item”.
