Behind closed doors · By term

2018–2022: Mayor Earl Funk’s first council

From November 2018 to October 2022, how much of Steinbach City Council’s work happened in committee, study sessions and closed meetings, what did members say about it, and where did they disagree?

In short

  • One public vote on the practice itself. In January 2019 three councillors wanted the purchasing by-law sent to the Strategic Priorities Committee (SPC) so new members could ask questions. The majority said it was simple and should be decided in public; the referral failed 3–4.
  • Members spoke up for openness. Councillor Susan Penner explained in-camera meetings in public (2019); Councillor Michael Zwaagstra warned against “decisions behind closed doors” and said SPC meetings were open to the public (2020), a claim this review could not confirm; the mayor kept an RCMP delegation on the record.
  • Committee first, a short vote after. Staff introduced items as discussed, agreed by “consensus” or given “direction from council” at SPC: pool fees, design standards, new by-laws, a housing grant process, a street naming policy. Most then passed unanimously with little public debate.
  • Budgets and grants at study sessions. Each budget, groups’ funding requests and the Clearspring Greens cost split were worked out privately. On Clearspring, council then changed course in public after meeting residents.
  • No trace of closed meetings, and some same-day documents. Closed meetings were announced after adjournment in May 2020 and October 2021 with no closure in the minutes. The event centre agreement and other major items were decided on packages council received that day. Good practice: meetings livestreamed from April 2020, and land purchases approved in public with prices.

Council: Mayor Earl Funk; Councillors Michael Zwaagstra,Jac Siemens, Susan Penner, Damian Penner, Bill Hiebert and Jake Hiebert.

What happened

2018–2019: a new council settles its habits

The new council began with a budget study session in November 2018. Its first open disagreement about where business should be done came two months later, over a by-law raising the City Manager’s spending authority from $250,000 to $350,000:

“There are a number of councilors on council that are new, a lot of things that we may not understand or fully know the intent and reasoning behind them. … And I'd like to make a motion that it be moved to an SPC meeting for further discussion.”

Councillor Jake Hiebert · Jan 15, 2019 · 0:58:12

“when we get them in advance, there is an opportunity to ask questions. At any time we can contact the city manager. … This is not complex, and we've already given first reading to this.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Jan 15, 2019 · 1:00:16

The referral failed 3–4 (Councillors Jake Hiebert, Siemens and Bill Hiebert for), and the by-law passed unanimously the same night. A month later a pool fee increase showed the more usual path:

“There was some significant discussion and debate over it. It is being forwarded at the recommendation of committee to council meeting for a decision.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Feb 5, 2019 · 0:09:43

The committee’s report was copied into the public package; at the meeting there were no questions, and the increase passed unanimously. Members were also clear about when council closes its doors, and why:

“However, there are times when we have to make a decision or in the process of making a decision, we cannot discuss it publicly. For decisions like these, we have in-camera meetings. … Essentially, the transparency has to come after the fact.”

Councillor Susan Penner, opening remarks on labour, legal and land matters · Sept 17, 2019 · 0:00:14

When an RCMP commander asked during his April 2019 presentation whether he could speak “in camera”, the mayor reminded him “But it's on video”, and the meeting stayed open. In October 2019 the event centre memorandum of understanding, committing the city to $10 million, came as “information to follow”: staff said the package reached council “earlier this evening, just prior to council meeting”. After a presentation and speeches from every member it passed unanimously, the mayor saying he did not “have to spend time convincing council”.

2020: “public business done in public”, and the Clearspring Greens levy

In January 2020 the Clearspring Greens pathway levy showed both sides of the practice. At the hearing the mayor told residents council had “deliberated on numbers” to decide how much their neighbourhood should pay, and that buying the lot for the path had to be kept quiet:

“So this is why we had to do this privately and quietly. We can't talk about land deals because what happens is the prices go up.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jan 7, 2020 · 2:04:20

The by-law passed second reading 5–2, Councillors Siemens and Jake Hiebert against. Two weeks later, on a staff report emailed that day, council moved the lighting cost to the city (6–1):

“there's nothing wrong with changing your mind after you've heard new information, and that's what we heard as we met with, with the community, the neighborhood.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Jan 21, 2020 · 0:18:21

With 78 objections filed, council cancelled the plan a year later. Councillor Susan Penner, after meeting residents, brought a 50/50 plan capped at $1,610 a lot to a public meeting, and it drew only six objections. In February 2020 the deputy mayor opened a meeting with a warning:

“But if we are honest with ourselves, we recognize that the temptation to make decisions behind closed doors outside of public scrutiny is one that all politicians, particularly at the local level, must constantly resist.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, deputy mayor · Feb 4, 2020 · 0:00:17

“Not only are our regular council meetings and SPC meetings open to the media and the public, we even record our meetings and put them online.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Feb 4, 2020 · 0:00:17

The mayor thanked him for the reminder “to keep public business done in public”. Regular meetings were indeed recorded, and from April 7, 2020 livestreamed. But no public notice, agenda, minutes or recording of any SPC meeting was found for this term, so the statement that SPC meetings are open to the public could not be confirmed. Three months later, the mayor adjourned a meeting and announced that council would “reconvene here for a in-camera meeting”; the minutes record no resolution to close and no subject.

Through 2020 more items came to council already agreed at committee. On the city’s design and construction standards, the public package held only a summary:

“There was consensus among council that these adjustments or these amendments be accepted.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · June 2, 2020 · 0:25:05

A dwelling-safety by-law was drafted because the committee’s “recommendation from council was for administration to prepare the bylaw”, and the new traffic by-law carried amendments council “requested” at a September committee meeting; its mover said he did not think there was “any further to add”. Councillor Siemens, by contrast, pointed residents to the committee report in the public package.

2021–2022: direction from committee, budgets from study sessions

“This bylaw was discussed at the April 13th Strategic Priorities Committee meeting. At that time, direction from council was to proceed with first reading of the amending bylaw.”

Adam Thiessen, Corporate Services Manager · May 4, 2021 · 0:28:33

The same pattern produced the affordable housing grant process (deadline set by “Consensus” at SPC, though council then departed from staff’s recommendation in public, with reasons), by-law updates on business licences, burning and swimming pools, and, in April 2022, an honorary street naming policy:

“this recommended policy has been previously submitted to council at Strategic Priorities Committee meeting for initial consideration. After much debate at that meeting, administration took the the comments and advice provided back and have recrafted the proposed policy…”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager · Apr 19, 2022 · 0:06:47

In public, two councillors spoke briefly in favour and it passed unanimously in under three minutes.

Money followed the study-session calendar. Councillor Siemens described a council that “spends a year making the budget”, starting in May; each financial plan was adopted unanimously after the public hearing, but the trade-offs were settled beforehand. Groups’ funding requests (STARS air ambulance, an animal rescue, the seniors’ centre) were sent to study sessions rather than answered in public; STARS later received $15,829 for 2021, visible only in the yearly grants summary; its representative said in 2022 that council had settled on $1 a person at the fall 2020 study session. In October 2021 the mayor again announced “a short closed meeting” at the end of a meeting, again with nothing in the minutes. A month later council reviewed Procedures By-law 1809, which closes every study session, and kept it unchanged; the only question was about quorum.

Land was handled as the law allows: the event centre parcels were negotiated privately and each purchase approved at a public meeting with its price ($250,000, $475,000 and $1.4 million), though the $1.4 million purchase, like the $1.28 million aquatic centre tender and the $1.9 million event centre design contract, was decided on a package council received that day.

Where members differed

Only one split vote was about where business should be done: the 2019 motion to send the purchasing by-law to SPC. The other three were on the Clearspring Greens levy, where the cost split worked out in private was argued over in public.
