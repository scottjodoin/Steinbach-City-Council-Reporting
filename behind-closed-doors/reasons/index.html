Behind closed doors · Council’s reasons

Council’s reasons, in its own words

What reasons have Steinbach City Council and its staff given since 2015 for discussing and settling business in study sessions, committee and closed meetings rather than in public, and how well do those reasons hold up?

In short

Council has given eight reasons, most of them reasonable in themselves. Land prices, staff matters and lawsuits do need confidentiality; budgets are best weighed as a whole; formal votes are held in public. The weaker points are where a reason stretches beyond what it covers: committee talk described as “not a decision” while staff take “direction” there; “the law allows it” without the minuted closure the law requires; and land confidentiality applied to a grant request turned purchase.

  • 1. Committee talk is not a decision
  • 2. It all becomes public in the end
  • 3. Land, staff and lawsuits must stay confidential
  • 4. Weigh money with the whole budget, and save time
  • 5. Staff need council’s direction
  • 6. Some talks go better off camera
  • 7. The law allows it
  • 8. Nothing is hidden: the meetings are public and recorded

1Committee talk is not a decision

“I realize that initially at the SPC meeting, 7.5% was discussed, but that's not a final decision at that point. It comes to council and we've all had time to reflect on it.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, on development fees · Oct 17, 2023 · 0:17:50

“…we are going to have numerous other infrastructure recommendations and suggestions that we are hoping council, you know, will get on board with that I won't— can't say right now publicly, but there will be recommendations at that meeting coming forward.”

Councillor Susan Penner, on the coming study session · June 18, 2019 · 0:37:23

The same reason is written into the city’s rules. Procedures By-law 1809 (2006, reviewed and kept by council in 2021 and 2023) says in s. 12.1 that study sessions may be held “where no official action is contemplated”, and that “A study session will be closed to the public as all matters are in preliminary stages and will eventually be decided upon at a public meeting.”

Context

Formal votes are indeed taken in public, and the 2023 development fee vote shows a public decision departing from the committee’s: 5% a year, not the 7.5% discussed there. But the line between discussion and decision is not always kept. Staff have described changes as “generally agreed to by council” at committee (2024) and items as coming with “direction from council” (2021); members have said they “did push an amendment at the SPC meeting, so we won't do it here” (2015); and in 2026 a $200,000 decision was deferred to a committee meeting. Manitoba’s Municipal Act also does not make openness depend on whether a decision is taken: “Every meeting of a council or council committee must be conducted in public” (s. 152(1)). See Finding 2.

2It all becomes public in the end

“Essentially, the transparency has to come after the fact.”

Councillor Susan Penner, on in-camera meetings · Sept 17, 2019 · 0:00:14

“in November, when we went into our study session, we went in with the promise that we were going to do projects that were going to mitigate floodwater mitigation and sewer backup in our community.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on the 2026 budget · Mar 17, 2026 · 0:29:41

“they've asked the city to provide a short-term loan of $400,000 to supplement operating cash flows. Important to note, the— this is a loan, it is not a grant, and so it will be repaid over a 4-year period”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, stating the event centre loan terms in public · June 16, 2026 · 0:39:07
Context

The outcomes do become public: budgets, by-laws, land purchases and the loan were all approved by public resolution, and some members took care to explain them. What usually stays private is the options and reasons. The public saw the 2026 flood budget but not the choices weighed over two days; it saw a yearly grants summary but not why requests were granted or refused; and it learned of the golf club deal on the night of the vote. The Manitoba Ombudsman has said that “Information previously discussed at a closed meeting that no longer needs to be confidential should be released to the public” (Dunnottar, 2022), and praised a municipality that reported “the discussions and the outcome” of closed sessions afterward (St. Francois Xavier, 2023–24). Steinbach has no practice of reporting out.

3Land, staff and lawsuits must stay confidential

“Like other councils, we do not discuss specific personnel issues in public. We also cannot publicly discuss possible land purchases or sales because then the prices of the land that we're looking at could change dramatically.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra · Feb 4, 2020 · 0:00:17

“Every time that we've negotiated a deal on any piece of property in the city anywhere, it has always been behind closed doors. We never discuss deals until we make them. because it's just not prudent to do so.”

Councillor John Fehr, on the golf club land purchase · Dec 5, 2017 · 1:20:24

“Yes, we do negotiate land deals in camera when it's actually a normal land deal, not when it's this weird, convoluted grant request/land deal sort of just slid into here.”

Councillor Michael Zwaagstra, replying at the 2018 budget · Mar 20, 2018 · 0:30:22
Context

This is the strongest reason, and the law recognizes it: a meeting may be closed for an employee’s matters, legal proceedings, or a matter “in its preliminary stages” where public discussion “could prejudice” negotiations (s. 152(3)). There is no general land ground; a land deal must fit that last test. Steinbach’s ordinary practice deserves credit: downtown lots (2016–2018), the event centre parcels (2021–2022) and the Clearspring Greens lot were negotiated privately and then approved at public meetings with their prices. The golf club deal was different. It began as a $500,000 grant request that council refused; the request for a public presentation was itself refused in a closed meeting; and its terms were first public on the night of the vote. The Manitoba Ombudsman has found that “Discussing confidential information provided by a third party is not an exception to the public meeting requirement” (2022–23 annual report). Whatever the subject, the Act also requires the closure and its general nature to be minuted, which Steinbach’s minutes do not do (reason 7).

4Weigh money with the whole budget, and save time

“because it has to do with— it's a financial ask. If you're okay with it, I would like to recommend we give this to our administration, put it on our study session so that it can be discussed better, like at length with our budget in front of us.”

Mayor Earl Funk, on a minor hockey funding request · Oct 3, 2023 · 0:26:11

“I think City Council spends a year making the budget. We start in May of each year and it gets approved the next March or April.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · Apr 6, 2021 · 0:34:22

“The details that were presented at that meeting have not been included again, as council had an opportunity to review and ask questions and better understand the proposed changes being made.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, on design standards · Feb 6, 2024 · 0:06:08
Context

Weighing requests against the whole budget is sound, and building a budget in workshops is common. The question is whether the workshops are public. Brandon holds two livestreamed budget deliberation days and Portage la Prairie debates its budget in an open, streamed committee (see How Steinbach compares). In Steinbach the answer to a group’s request usually surfaces only inside the next budget or a yearly grants summary: the seniors’ centre, which asked in public in 2018, “merely received a pat on the back”, in Councillor Funk’s words, and requests from minor hockey (2023) and the Eastman Raiders (2025) have no public answer. Saving time has a cost too: when the supporting detail stays in committee, the public record of a decision lacks the information council used, and same-day documents (five major items in 2024–2026) left little time for anyone to study them. Councillor Susan Penner asked in 2025 that study-session grant decisions at least be visible in the budget’s grants line.

5Staff need council’s direction

“That decision and that discussion doesn't come at a council meeting. That discussion comes at an SPC meeting where we get full response from staff.”

Councillor Jac Siemens · May 3, 2016 · 0:32:46

“This bylaw was discussed at the April 13th Strategic Priorities Committee meeting. At that time, direction from council was to proceed with first reading of the amending bylaw.”

Adam Thiessen, Corporate Services Manager · May 4, 2021 · 0:28:33

“while administration would typically refer a discussion like this to SPC, we don't have a meeting for SPC until September.”

Adam Thiessen, Senior Manager, Corporate Services, on a $200,000 safety grant · July 21, 2026 · 1:22:18
Context

Staff do need to know what council wants before drafting a by-law, and full answers from staff are easier to get around a table. But under Manitoba’s Act, “A council may act only by resolution or by-law” (s. 140(1)), a resolution “is not valid unless it is passed at a council meeting” (s. 141), and a committee’s resolution is not binding until council passes it (s. 108). Ontario’s law differs, but its Ombudsman’s test is a useful benchmark: a council materially advances its business when it “provides direction or input to staff” (Hawkesbury, 2026). Steinbach’s own staff have pointed the way: “Consensus is always Good, but resolutions are better” (City Manager, May 2026), and at the new council’s first meeting staff raised a point of order so that appointments were ratified by resolution. Where direction from committee is recorded, as in the 2024 minutes on the sponsorship policy, residents can at least see that it was given. See Finding 5.

7The law allows it

“If there are particular meetings of council that deal with closed matters, those matters are to remain closed until council as a body makes the decision to bring the matter into an open meeting.”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, answering Councillor Funk · Nov 21, 2017 · 0:23:28

“This bylaw, which is the procedure bylaw of the city, also is subject to the same requirements of the Municipal Act that council approve one and that it be reviewed at least once during a term of council”

Troy Warkentin, City Manager, at the 2021 organizational meeting · Nov 2, 2021 · 0:02:49

“Seeing nothing, then that has been reviewed as well and we will adopt it for the Another year.”

Mayor Earl Funk · Nov 2, 2021 · 0:04:15
Context

The law does allow closed meetings, but on two conditions: council must decide “during the meeting” to close, with “the decision and general nature of the matter” recorded in the minutes, and the subject must fit one of the listed grounds (s. 152(3)). Steinbach’s own Procedures By-law repeats both conditions word for word (s. 11.9). Yet the same by-law closes every study session as a rule (s. 12.1), and a text search of every set of minutes from July 2017 to October 2026 found no resolution to close a meeting, no ground and no subject. Closed meetings announced after adjournment (2020, 2021, 2026) left no trace at all. The Manitoba Ombudsman has called the closure rules “requirements and… not guidelines” (Dunnottar, 2022). This is our plain reading of the texts; no court or Ombudsman has ruled on Steinbach’s practice. See Findings 3 and 4.
