Behind closed doors · How Steinbach compares

How Steinbach compares

How does Steinbach City Council’s practice of working through business in committee, study sessions and closed meetings sit against Manitoba’s open-meeting rules, the city’s own by-laws and what other councils do, and what would full openness add?

In short

  • Strong where the cameras are. Regular meetings are recorded and livestreamed, votes and dissent are minuted, land purchases are approved in public with prices, and in 2022–2026 the vote record shows no sign of developers’ files being settled in advance.
  • Weak where they aren’t. The Strategic Priorities Committee and budget study sessions have no public notice, agenda or minutes, and no minutes since 2017 record a closed meeting. Manitoba’s Act, and Steinbach’s own Procedures By-law, say committee meetings are public unless properly closed and minuted.
  • Neighbours do it differently. Selkirk and Morden hold their whole-council committee in public with minutes; Brandon and Portage la Prairie deliberate on their budgets in public; Morden and the RM of Ste. Anne minute every closed session with its legal ground. None of them is perfect, and Steinbach records more of its regular meetings than Selkirk or Morden.

Key findings

1No sign that developers’ votes are arranged in advance (2022–2026)

The three vote tests for January 2022 to September 2026 point the same way. Development is where this council disagreed most, contested files got public reasons, and every departure from staff advice was argued out in the meeting (the four tests). Unanimous votes on routine items are normal and are not evidence of private agreement. This supports what the mayor and deputy mayor said when a resident suggested otherwise (reason 8). The tests cover only that period, and they cannot show what was said in sessions that are not recorded.

2The SPC is a committee the public can’t attend or read

By Steinbach’s own Organization By-law, the SPC is “the entire Council sitting as a committee”, set up under the section of the Act that creates council committees. Under the Act its meetings must be public unless closed for a listed reason, and its minutes are public records (s. 263). In practice no notice, agenda or minutes have appeared anywhere public in the eleven years reviewed, and Councillor Zwaagstra’s 2020 statement that SPC meetings are open to the public could not be confirmed. Members’ view that SPC is “not a final decision” is true in law (s. 108), but the openness rule applies to committee meetings whether or not they decide anything (reason 1, reason 6). The Ombudsman’s Harrison Park report shows what compliance looks like: an agenda with the date, time and place posted 48 hours ahead.

3The study-session rule conflicts with the Act and with the by-law itself

By-law 1809 closes every study session because “all matters are in preliminary stages” (s. 12.1). The Act allows that ground only where public discussion “could prejudice” the city’s activities or negotiations, and only by a decision recorded in the minutes. The same by-law repeats the Act’s test (s. 11.9), says it applies “in all committees” (s. 1.2), and says it “shall at no time, set a lower standard” than the Act (s. 2.2). Most study-session topics on the record (grants, budgets, policies, fee options, flood projects) would be hard to fit within the Act’s test. Council reviewed the by-law and kept it unchanged in 2021 and 2023 (reason 7). This is a plain reading of the texts; no court or Ombudsman has ruled on it.

4Closed meetings leave no trace in the minutes

Closing a meeting for a personnel, legal or negotiating matter is lawful and sometimes necessary (reason 3). The gap is the record. A search of all 211 sets of minutes from July 2017 to October 2026 found no resolution to close a meeting, no ground under s. 152(3) and no subject, and closed meetings were announced after the public meeting had adjourned (2020, 2021, 2026). Before 2018, the golf club deal was negotiated over about a year of closed meetings and a closed 4–3 vote refused a public presentation; the public knows of that vote only because two members described it. Neighbouring Morden and the RM of Ste. Anne show the routine alternative: a minuted resolution naming the ground and the subject, every time.

5Agreement and direction outside council meetings is borderline

Formal votes happen in public, as the law requires. But the record includes design standards accepted by “consensus among council” (2020) and “generally agreed to by council” (2024) at committee, by-laws drafted on “direction from council” given at SPC (2020–2024), and a $200,000 grant decision deferred to an SPC meeting in August 2026 with no public resolution found afterwards. Under ss. 140–141 council can direct its staff only by resolution at a council meeting. Ontario’s law is different, but its Ombudsman’s test is a useful benchmark: a council materially advances business when it “reaches an agreement” or “provides direction or input to staff” (reason 5).

6Budgets and grants are decided where the public can’t follow

Every budget in this record was adopted unanimously after a public hearing, as the province requires, but built over months of study sessions; hearings drew few or no speakers. Groups’ funding requests are sent there “because it’s a budget item”, and the answers surface only inside the next budget or a yearly grants summary, if at all. Brandon and Portage la Prairie show that a Manitoba city can deliberate on its budget in public, item by item (reason 4).

7Same-day documents limit scrutiny, in every term

The golf club lease was not in the printed package (2017); the $10 million event centre agreement arrived “just prior to council meeting” (2019); and five major items in 2024–2026 were decided on documents council received that day. This is lawful, but neither residents nor, in practice, councillors could study them in advance, as Councillor Siemens’ withdrawn one-year-trial motion on the 2025 ice agreement shows (reason 4, reason 2).

What the rules require

Manitoba’s Municipal Act

  • Committee meetings are public too. “Every meeting of a council or council committee must be conducted in public” (s. 152(1)), and “Everyone has a right to be present” (s. 152(2)).
  • Closing a meeting has two conditions. Council must decide during the meeting to close, with “the decision and general nature of the matter” recorded in the minutes, and the subject must fit a short list: an employee, legal proceedings, an investigation or enforcement, security, an Ombudsman report, or “a matter that is in its preliminary stages and respecting which discussion in public could prejudice a municipality’s ability to carry out its activities or negotiations” (s. 152(3)). There is no separate ground for land.
  • No decisions in private. “No resolution or by-law may be passed at a meeting that is closed to the public” (s. 152(4)). “A council may act only by resolution or by-law” (s. 140(1)), and a resolution “is not valid unless it is passed at a council meeting” (s. 141). A committee’s resolution is not binding unless council passes it (s. 108).
  • Committee minutes are public records, except for parts of a meeting properly closed (s. 263(1)(f)).
  • Unchanged since 2004. The only amendment in 2015–2026 (2021) allowed electronic meetings and deemed them public if the public can follow them.

The Manitoba Ombudsman has applied these rules strictly. In a 2022 report on the Village of Dunnottar it said the closure grounds “are requirements and are not guidelines”, that “council cannot make decisions in private”, and that “If in doubt, municipalities should keep their meeting open or request legal advice from the municipality’s lawyer.” In a January 2025 report on the Municipality of Harrison Park it said: “Every meeting of a council committee must be conducted in public. The public has a right to be present at council committee meetings.” Harrison Park agreed to post committee agendas 48 hours ahead. In a 2023–24 case the Ombudsman found the RM of St. Francois Xavier had closed a meeting properly because it gave notice, minuted its reasons and reported the outcome afterwards. Manitoba has no closed-meeting investigator like Ontario’s; a resident’s main route is a complaint to the Ombudsman, who can recommend but not overturn.

Steinbach’s own rules

  • The SPC is a council committee. Organization By-law 2025 (2013) sets it up as “the entire Council sitting as a committee” that “shall not take any official action while in Committee” (s. 5.2). The public may be excluded only when the issues fall under the Act’s grounds (s. 5.4), and any member may object to an SPC agenda item that should be at a regular meeting (s. 5.5).
  • The Procedures By-law repeats the Act’s test. By-law 1809 (2006) applies “in council, and in all committees thereof” (s. 1.2), may never set “a lower standard” than the Act (s. 2.2), counts SPC agendas as agendas (s. 3.1.1), and allows a closed meeting only if “the decision and general nature of the matter are recorded in the minutes” (s. 11.9).
  • But it closes every study session. Section 12.1 says study sessions may be designated Strategic Priorities sessions “where no official action is contemplated”, and “A study session will be closed to the public as all matters are in preliminary stages and will eventually be decided upon at a public meeting.” Their agendas go to council and staff, not the public.
  • Council’s own openness pledge. The conflict of interest policy each member signs says councillors “will endeavour to conduct and convey council business in an open and public manner… so that stakeholders can determine the process, logic and rationale used to reach conclusions or decisions.” The city has no separate open-government or transparency policy.

What the public record shows

  • The city’s meeting calendar lists regular and inaugural council meetings and the Mayor’s Open Door Days; no committee, study, special or closed meetings (checked November 7, 2026).
  • None of the 211 sets of minutes from July 2017 to October 2026 contains “in camera”, “closed meeting”, “closed session”, “152(3)” or “committee of the whole”. Study sessions or the SPC appear in 12 of them, always as a place items were referred to or discussed.
  • No SPC or study-session agenda or minutes were found on the city’s website, in agenda packages or in the Internet Archive.

Testing the vote record for decisions made in advance (2022–2026)

This analysis, from our October 2026 page, covers only January 2022 to September 2026: 103 regular meetings, 1,270 recorded motions and transcripts of the meeting videos. A unanimous vote on its own proves nothing. Most municipal business is routine, and 96.5% of the 1,270 motions passed unanimously. So we used four tests, each looking for a trace that private agreement would leave in the public record. The vote tests use the 527 substantive decisions (excluding agendas, minutes and ceremonial items) coded for the council deliberation report.

The three exceptions in Test 2
  • Nov 1, 2022, rezoning near Old Tom Road and Loewen Boulevard. The objections had been heard and debated at an earlier hearing (Sept 6, 2022). This was a later reading.
  • Jan 20, 2026, third reading of the new Zoning By-law. Objections went through the Municipal Board process, and the by-law had been debated at earlier readings.
  • June 2, 2026, Steinbach Housing at 185 Friesen Avenue. The conditional-use vote took about a minute, but it followed a 38-minute joint hearing. The companion variance was debated by seven councillors and passed 5–2.

The vote record therefore gives no sign that developers’ decisions were settled privately before the vote in this period. The fourth test points elsewhere: to the sessions where council discusses items before they reach a public meeting, which is what the rest of this report describes. The deliberation report’s coding is AI-assisted and provisional; “residents objected” was flagged from coded summaries of public input and checked by hand.

What full openness would add

Each step below is already done by another Manitoba municipality, or is set out in the Act or Steinbach’s own by-laws:

  • Post notice and an agenda for every SPC meeting, and publish its minutes, as the Ombudsman recommended in Harrison Park and as Selkirk and Morden do.
  • Hold SPC and budget study sessions in public by default, as Selkirk and Morden do for their committees of the whole and Brandon and Portage la Prairie do for budget deliberations, closing only the parts that fit s. 152(3).
  • Amend By-law 1809, s. 12.1, so study sessions follow the Act and the by-law’s own s. 11.9 rather than closing automatically. The new council’s required review of the by-law is the moment to do it.
  • Minute every closed session: the resolution, the ground under s. 152(3) and the general nature of the matter, as Morden and the RM of Ste. Anne do; and report out afterwards, as Selkirk does and Brandon’s by-law requires.
  • Give direction to staff only by resolution in a council meeting, and report any consensus reached at committee in public, as the City Manager’s “resolutions are better” suggests.
  • Answer funding requests in public: a yearly list of requests sent to study sessions, with the amount and the outcome of each.
  • Post supplementary packages online as soon as they go to council.
  • Keep disclosing contacts with applicants, following the mayor’s example in October 2025.
