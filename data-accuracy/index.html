About this site

Data accuracy

Every report here is built from machine transcripts of council meetings. This page explains how accurate those transcripts are, how sure we are about who said what, and how to tell us when something is wrong.

Last updated Nov 14, 2026. A listening check of a random sample is under way, and we will update these figures when it is done.

In short

  • Words: about 95 in 100 are right (our estimate). Two independent transcripts of the same meetings agree on 93 of every 100 words. Where they differ, at least one of them is wrong.
  • Names are the weak spot. Neither transcription system spells “Zwaagstra” or “Hiebert” correctly. We correct council members' names from the official roster.
  • Speakers: 91% of lines carry a high-confidence name across all 88 meetings from November 2022 to November 2026, and 95% of the lines that say something of substance. Most of the uncertainty is on short lines like “Thank you.” The rest are marked as less certain, and the transcript says so.
  • You can always check. Every quote links to that moment in the city's video. The video and the city's minutes are the official record.
  • The transcripts are free to use. All 88 meetings, with speakers, confidence levels and video links, are on GitHub.
  • Found an error? Email steinbachcitycouncilreporting@gmail.com.

How the transcripts are made

  1. Transcribe. Each recorded council meeting on the city's YouTube channel is transcribed by AssemblyAI, a speech-to-text service. It splits the audio into speakers by voice (“Speaker A”, “Speaker B”) but does not know who they are.
  2. Check the words. YouTube makes its own automatic captions of the same video. Comparing the two shows where at least one of them misheard.
  3. Name the speakers. Each voice is matched to a person using the meeting itself (the mayor handing the floor to “Councillor Penner”, people introducing themselves) and the city's minutes (who moved and seconded each motion, who asked which question). AI does the first pass, and every match records its evidence and a confidence level.
  4. Listen. A person listens to the uncertain spots, and to a random sample of ordinary ones, and corrects what is wrong.
  5. Protect residents. Members of the public who speak at hearings are not named. Reports use initials; transcripts use “Resident 1”, “Resident 2” and so on.

How accurate are the words?

We compared the AssemblyAI transcript with YouTube's captions for every council meeting from January 2022 to October 2026: 104 meetings, about 760,000 words each. We ignored differences that don't change meaning, like “2” and “two” or “centre” and “center”.

MeasureResult
Words the two transcripts agree on93.4% on average (90.5% in the hardest meeting, 95.9% in the clearest)
Where they disagreeAbout 1 word in 15. At least one transcript is wrong there, sometimes both.
Our estimate for the transcripts we publishAbout 95 words in 100 right. AssemblyAI is the stronger of the two (see names below), so most of the disagreements are likely YouTube's errors. A listening check of a random sample will firm this up.

That is why AssemblyAI is our main transcript and YouTube is the check, and why we correct council members' and staff names from the roster in the minutes. Names of residents and businesses that come up in a meeting may still be misspelled.

Words that were never said

AssemblyAI sometimes adds words that nobody said. In the meetings since November 2022 it wrote “Your Worship” 319 times; YouTube's captions never contain it, and when we listened, people had actually said “Mr. Mayor”, or nothing at all. It also invents short phrases such as “Thank you, Brian” and “Alderman Demong” (a name no one in Steinbach has). We check every one of these against YouTube's captions at that moment: 301 were removed, 18 were changed to “Mr. Mayor”, and 78 invented phrases were removed.

The vocabulary of local names has one side effect: occasionally the transcription writes a council member's name in place of someone else's (for example a former councillor's name for a delegate). Wherever a council member's name appears at a time they were not on council and YouTube heard a different name, it is replaced with “[name unclear]”.

Which lines matter

A wrong name on “Thank you.” changes nothing. A wrong name on a councillor's argument puts words in someone's mouth. So every line also gets a substance score, set by fixed rules from its words:

SubstanceWhat it meansShare of linesHigh confidenceLow confidence
SubstantiveSays something: a question, a position, an explanation, a presentation, a reply.46% (4,804)95%1%

Counted by words spoken rather than by lines, 98% of everything said is in a line with a high-confidence name. The uncertainty sits mostly on short interjections, which are hard to place and rarely matter.

What listening has shown so far

  • The doubtful spots are mostly right. We listened to 21 lines that the checks had flagged as uncertain. 19 had the right person.
  • The weakest spot is a few words inside someone else's line. When two people speak back to back, the transcript can attach the first words of one to the end of the other. Four of the five such pieces we checked were wrong, and they are now fixed and split out.
  • Short interjections are hard. “Thank you.”, “Second.” or “Yes.” are often impossible to place. When we can't tell, the transcript says “Unidentified speaker”.
  • Shared surnames need care. Council has had two Hieberts and two Penners. The minutes record motions by initial (“B. Hiebert”, “J. Hiebert”), which is how we tell them apart.
  • Automatic speaker naming alone is not enough. AssemblyAI can also guess names. It is usually right about the mayor, the City Manager and councillors with distinctive voices, but across the 88 meetings it repeatedly swapped the two Hieberts, sometimes named a resident as a councillor, and could not see when two or three people's voices had been grouped together, which happened in most meetings. That is why every name is checked against the minutes.
  • Voices help tell people apart. When the words alone can't settle it, we measure the pitch of the voice in the recording: it reliably tells Susan Penner from Damian Penner, and helps separate the two Hieberts.

Our estimate: about 95 in 100 high-confidence lines name the right person. The random listening check under way will replace this estimate with a measured figure.

Reports published before November 2026

Report an error

If a quote, a name or a summary looks wrong, please email steinbachcitycouncilreporting@gmail.com. It helps to include:

  • the report or page (a link is best);
  • the meeting date, and the time in the video if you have it;
  • what you think is wrong, and what was really said or who said it.

We check every report against the video and the minutes, fix what is wrong, and note the correction. We use your email address only to reply, and we never publish it.
