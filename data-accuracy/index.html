About this site

Data accuracy

Every report here is built from machine transcripts of council meetings. This page explains how accurate those transcripts are, how sure we are about who said what, and how to tell us when something is wrong.

Last updated Nov 14, 2026. A listening check of a random sample is under way, and we will update these figures when it is done.

In short

  • The video and the city's minutes are the official record. Every quote in our reports and transcripts links to that moment in the city's video, so anyone can check it.
  • Words: we estimate about 95 in every 100 are right. Most errors are small: a misheard word or a misspelled name. Council members' and staff names are spelled from the city's minutes.
  • Speakers: 9,443 of 10,337 transcript lines (91%) carry a high-confidence name, across the 88 meetings from November 2022 to November 2026. Less certain lines are marked as such in the transcript.
  • Votes, motions and decisions come from the minutes, never from the transcript alone.
  • The transcripts are free to use. All 88 meetings, with speakers, confidence levels and video links, are on GitHub. See also How these reports are made.
  • Found an error? Email steinbachcitycouncilreporting@gmail.com.

How the transcripts are made

  1. Transcribe. Each recorded council meeting on the city's YouTube channel is transcribed by AssemblyAI, a speech-to-text service. It splits the audio into voices (“Speaker A”, “Speaker B”) but does not know who they are.
  2. Check the words. YouTube makes its own automatic captions of the same video. Comparing the two shows where at least one of them misheard.
  3. Name the speakers. Each voice is matched to a person using the meeting itself (the mayor handing the floor to “Councillor Penner”, people introducing themselves) and the city's minutes (who moved and seconded each motion, who asked which question). An AI assistant proposes each match from these clues and records its evidence and a confidence level.
  4. Listen. A person listens to samples of the uncertain spots and of ordinary lines, and corrects what is wrong. So far that is a few dozen lines; most lines have not been listened to by a person, which is why each line carries a confidence level.
  5. Protect residents. Members of the public who speak at hearings are not named. Reports use initials; transcripts use “Resident 1”, “Resident 2” and so on.

How accurate are the words?

We compared the AssemblyAI transcript with YouTube's captions for every council meeting from January 2022 to October 2026: 104 meetings, about 760,000 words each. We ignored differences that don't change meaning, like “2” and “two” or “centre” and “center”.

MeasureResult
Words the two transcripts agree on93.4% of words on average (90.5% in the hardest meeting, 95.9% in the clearest)
Where they disagreeAbout 1 word in 15. At least one transcript is wrong there, sometimes both.
Listening check so farWe listened to 14 of those disagreements. AssemblyAI was right in 8, YouTube in 1, both were acceptable in 1, both were wrong in 3, and 1 was unclear.
Our estimate for the transcripts we publishAbout 95 words in 100 right. This is an estimate from the comparison and that small sample, not a measured rate. A larger random listening check will replace it.

Words the transcription service added

Speech-to-text software sometimes adds words that nobody said. These come from the software, not from anyone at the meeting, and we remove them before publishing. In the meetings since November 2022, AssemblyAI wrote “Your Worship” 319 times; YouTube's captions never contain it, and in the spots we listened to, people had actually said “Mr. Mayor”, or nothing at all. It also added short phrases such as “Thank you, Brian” and the names of people who were not at the meeting. We check every one of these against YouTube's captions at that moment: 301 were removed, 18 were changed to “Mr. Mayor”, and 78 added phrases were removed.

The list of local names we give the service has one side effect: occasionally it writes a council member's name where someone else was speaking. Wherever a council member's name appears at a time they were not on council, and YouTube heard a different name, it is replaced with “[name unclear]”.

How sure are we about who said what?

Every council meeting from November 2022 to November 2026 has been rechecked: 88 meetings and 10,337 transcript lines. Earlier years are next.

“High confidence” means the evidence agrees, not that a person has listened to that line.

Where the uncertainty is

Getting the name right matters most where someone makes a statement or asks a question. So each line is also sorted by its form, using fixed rules about its words:

Line typeWhat it coversLinesHigh confidenceLow confidence
StatementA statement, question, explanation, presentation or reply.4,80495% of these lines1%

Short reply“Thank you.”, “Yes.”, “Okay.”2,54078%10%

Counted by words spoken instead of lines, 98% of the words are in lines with a high-confidence name. Most of the uncertainty is on short replies, which are hard to place in a recording. Votes, motions and seconders are taken from the minutes, never from who said “Yes.”

The line type describes the form of a line, not the value of what anyone said. It is not a measure of any council member and should not be used to compare people: a “Yes.” can be a decisive moment, and whoever chairs a meeting speaks mostly in procedure because of the role.

What listening has shown so far

  • The doubtful spots were mostly right. In a first sample, we listened to 21 lines that the checks had flagged as uncertain. 19 had the right person.
  • The weakest spot is a few words inside someone else's line. When two people speak back to back, the transcript can attach the first words of one to the end of the other. Four of the five such pieces we checked were attached to the wrong person; they are now fixed and split out.
  • Short replies are hard. “Thank you.”, “Second.” or “Yes.” are often impossible to place. When we can't tell, the transcript says “Unidentified speaker”.
  • Shared surnames need care. Council has had two Hieberts and two Penners. The minutes record motions by initial (“B. Hiebert”, “J. Hiebert”), which is how we tell them apart.
  • The service's automatic guesses are never used unchecked. AssemblyAI can also guess who is speaking. Its guesses are often right about the mayor and the City Manager, but they mixed up people who share a surname and could not tell when two or three voices had been grouped together. So every name is checked against the minutes and the meeting itself.
  • How a voice sounds can help. When the words alone can't settle it, we compare how high or low the voices sound in that meeting's public recording. It is one clue among several and never decides a name on its own. We don't build voiceprints.

Our estimate is that about 95 in 100 high-confidence lines name the right person. This is an estimate for the transcript data set, based on the small listening samples above, not a measured rate. The random listening check under way will replace it with a measured figure.

Reports published before November 2026

Earlier reports identified speakers quote by quote, using the same kinds of clues, with the meeting video linked for every quote. If a recheck shows a quote was attributed to the wrong person, we correct the report and note the correction on it.

Report an error

If a quote, a name or a summary looks wrong, please email steinbachcitycouncilreporting@gmail.com. It helps to include:

  • the report or page (a link is best);
  • the meeting date, and the time in the video if you have it;
  • what you think is wrong, and what was really said or who said it.

We check every error report against the video and the minutes, fix what is wrong, and note the correction. We use your email address only to reply, and we never publish it.
